Introduction

Back to the Bauhaus

The Bauhaus, founded in Weimar in 1919, sought to dissolve the boundaries between art, craft, and industry. Its preliminary course — the legendary Vorkurs taught by Johannes Itten, László Moholy-Nagy, and Josef Albers — asked students to confront materials, color, and form as a kind of laboratory, before any application to a "real" task. That spirit of structured experimentation, of treating the alphabet of design as a system to be understood from first principles, animates this book.

Wassily Kandinsky abstracted the visual world down to point, line, and plane. Moholy-Nagy used photography and light as a designable substance. Albers taught that color is never seen by itself: it is always a relationship. A century later, software has changed the tools but not the fundamentals. Pixels, vectors, layers, and timelines are simply new vehicles for the same elemental questions of composition, contrast, and order.

The premise of The New Basics is that design education must bridge two worlds: the visual thinking inherited from the modernist studio, and the screen-based, code-aware practice of contemporary work. Each chapter that follows isolates a single concept — formstorming, gestalt, modularity, motion — and gives it room to breathe, accompanied by an interactive demo that lets you handle the principle yourself.

Chapter One

Formstorming

"I like a lot the adage that for every problem there is a solution that is simple, obvious, and wrong." — Malcolm Grear

Formstorming is to visual designers what brainstorming is to writers: a structured way to outrun the obvious. The first idea is almost always a cliché — the lightbulb for "idea," the globe for "international," the handshake for "agreement." Designers move past these reflex responses by generating quantity before quality, then judging what they have made.

A practical method is to take a single subject and produce many small studies of it — fifty, a hundred — pushing each one into a different conceptual register. Begin with the literal (icons that resemble the subject), shift into the indexical (marks that point to it through cause and effect), and end in the symbolic (signs whose meaning is purely cultural). The grid below cycles through twenty iterations of a single form, a circle, recast each time as a different visual idea.

Demo 01 — One hundred iterations
Each tile is a small study of "circle," reinterpreted as icon, index, or symbol. The exercise trains designers to keep producing past the obvious.

Icon

A sign that resembles its subject. A pictogram of a chair means "chair" because it looks like one.

Index

A sign that points to its subject through cause and effect — smoke for fire, footprints for a walker.

Symbol

A sign whose meaning is purely cultural and conventional. A red cross or a national flag.

Exhaustive iteration

Producing many alternatives before judging. Quantity surfaces options judgment alone cannot.

02
Chapter Two

Point, Line, Plane

"A line is the track made by the moving point . . . it is the point's most concise form of becoming." — Wassily Kandinsky

Almost every two-dimensional design can be analyzed as some combination of three elemental units. A point marks a position — a dot, a punctuation mark, a stop. A line is the trace of a point in motion, a one-dimensional path connecting positions. A plane is a flat region with width and length: a sheet, a field of color, a paragraph of text experienced as a block.

These categories blur. A printed dot is, on closer inspection, a tiny plane. A long enough rectangle reads as a line. A line repeated densely becomes a plane of texture. Designers exploit this elasticity: a row of tightly set letters reads as a line of text; the same letters scaled up isolate as points of interest; a paragraph clustered tightly becomes a gray plane on the page. In digital media, this distinction is reinforced by formats — vectors describe shapes geometrically, while bitmaps describe them as a plane of pixels.

Demo 02 — Click to place points, watch lines emerge
Click anywhere to place a point. When two points fall close enough, a line forms between them. With enough density, the lines themselves become a plane.

Point

A mark of position. The smallest visible element on the page or screen.

Line

The trace of a point in motion. A path, a contour, a rule, a stem of type.

Plane

A two-dimensional surface or field — the page itself, a block of color, a paragraph as gray tone.

Halftone

A printing technique that simulates continuous tone using arrays of tiny dots of varied size.

Vector vs. bitmap

Vectors store geometry; bitmaps store pixels. The first scales infinitely; the second has a native resolution.

03
Chapter Three

Rhythm & Balance

"I pay close attention to the variety of shapes and sizes; their distribution becomes the rhythm of the page." — Sergei Forostovskii

Balance is one of the deepest human responses to form. We stand upright; our bodies are bilaterally symmetrical; we feel a kind of vertigo at images that tilt without resolution. Designers manage that physical instinct on the flat surface of the page. Symmetrical balance — a centered axis with equivalent weights on either side — reads as formal, monumental, classical. Asymmetrical balance — distributing unequal elements so they still feel resolved — reads as dynamic, modern, energetic.

Rhythm is balance in time. A repeating element establishes a pulse; varying that element introduces syncopation. Designers create rhythm with size, color, spacing, alignment, and the implied direction of forms. Too much repetition becomes monotony; too much variation becomes noise. The challenge is to find a beat that is steady enough to register and irregular enough to keep the eye moving.

Demo 03 — Toggle balance modes
The same five elements arranged around a central axis (symmetrical) and along an active diagonal (asymmetrical). Both compositions can feel balanced — they balance different forces.

Symmetry

Mirror-image balance around a central axis. Conveys stability, tradition, formality.

Asymmetry

Unequal weights resolved by counterbalance. Conveys movement, contemporaneity, energy.

Visual weight

The relative attention a shape commands — larger, darker, more saturated elements weigh more.

Dynamic balance

Equilibrium achieved through tension rather than mirroring. The classical asymmetric ideal.

Repetition with variation

A consistent beat punctuated by change. The basis of all visual rhythm.

04
Chapter Four

Scale

"Miss Darcy was tall, and on a larger scale than Elizabeth; and, though little more than sixteen, her figure was formed." — Jane Austen, Pride and Prejudice

Scale is at once a measurable fact and a felt impression. Objective scale is the literal dimension of a thing — a billboard versus a business card, a 12-point caption versus a 96-point headline. Subjective scale is the sense of size you carry away: an image of an ant filling the screen reads as huge, even though its actual pixels may be modest.

Designers use scale to create hierarchy, depth, and meaning. Putting two elements at extremely different sizes — what is called scale contrast — generates tension and drama; it also clarifies importance. A composition with no scale contrast feels static. Cropping is a scale device: showing only part of an object enlarges what remains and forces the viewer to extrapolate. Whether a thing on the page feels intimate, vast, or human-sized has more to do with relationships than measurements.

The literal, measurable dimensions of an element in inches, pixels, or points.

05
Chapter Five

Texture

"If you touch something (it is likely) someone will feel it." — Rick Valicenti

Texture exists in two registers. Physical texture is the actual surface of a thing — the tooth of uncoated paper, the burr of letterpress, the slick of a foil stamp. Optical or virtual texture is the appearance of texture on a smooth surface: halftone dots that imitate the grain of a photograph, repeated lines that simulate the warp of fabric, a digital noise filter that gives a flat field the feel of a worn poster.

Typography itself is a texture. A page of dense text reads as a gray field at a distance; condensed letters create a tighter weave than expanded ones; tight leading thickens the gray, loose leading thins it. Designers compose with textures the way painters compose with brushwork — pairing prickly with soft, smooth with rough, sparse with dense — to control how a surface invites the eye to enter, rest, or move on.

Physical texture

The tactile surface of a printed or fabricated object — paper grain, embossing, varnish.

Optical texture

The visual sensation of texture on a smooth surface, simulated by pattern, halftone, or noise.

Typographic texture

The gray value created by a block of text, controlled by weight, size, leading, and tracking.

Texture contrast

Pairing dissimilar textures — smooth/rough, dense/sparse — to direct attention and create rhythm.

Chapter Six

Color

"All colors are the friends of their neighbors and the lovers of their opposites." — Marc Chagall

Color is the most emotionally charged element of design and the most context-dependent. A single swatch of gray will read as warm against a cool surround and cool against a warm one; a saturated red looks brighter against gray than against another red. Josef Albers spent decades demonstrating that "color is the most relative medium in art." Designers do not choose colors so much as they choose relationships.

The standard color wheel organizes hues into primaries, secondaries, and tertiaries. Pairs sitting opposite each other are complements; placed side by side they vibrate, set inside one another they neutralize. Analogous colors — neighbors on the wheel — produce harmony. Beyond hue, every color has a value (lightness) and a saturation (intensity), and most decisions in practice are decisions about those three dimensions, not about the hue alone.

The mechanics differ by medium. Screens emit light and mix it additively in RGB — red, green, and blue combining to white. Print absorbs light and mixes it subtractively in CMYK — cyan, magenta, yellow, and black layered to absorb everything but the color you see. A designer choosing colors for both must navigate the gap between gamuts: a vivid screen color can be unreachable in ink, and a deep printed color can look dull on screen.

Chapter Seven

Gestalt Principles

"The form of an object is not more important than the form of the space surrounding it." — Malcolm Grear

Gestalt psychology, developed in early twentieth-century Germany, proposes that perception is not passive intake but active organization. The eye does not register isolated pixels; it builds wholes. Confronted with scattered elements it groups them, with broken contours it closes them, with ambiguous figures it flips between readings. Designers exploit this constant interpretive activity by giving the eye just enough information to complete the picture itself.

The classical grouping principles — similarity, proximity, closure, continuity, common region, symmetry — describe the rules by which the visual system assembles parts into wholes. Equally important is the figure/ground relationship: any closed shape sits inside a surrounding field, and the eye negotiates which is which. Reversible figures (Rubin's vase, the face-or-vase paradox) demonstrate that figure and ground are not fixed properties of the image but decisions the viewer makes.

Active perception

Seeing is constructive. The brain builds wholes from fragments, edges, and contrasts.

Figure / ground

The negotiation between an object and its surrounding field. Negative space is never empty.

Grouping

By similarity, proximity, common region, or shared motion — the rules by which parts cohere.

Closure

The tendency to perceive incomplete forms as complete, filling in missing contour.

Continuity

The eye prefers smooth paths. Lines that cross are read as continuing, not bouncing.

Ambiguity

Images that admit more than one reading. A productive resource when meanings can coexist.

08
Chapter Eight

Framing

"The frame is in no way a background . . . but neither is its thickness as margin a figure." — Jacques Derrida

Frames are everywhere: the white margin of a page, the edge of a screen, the matte around a photograph, the caption beneath it, the meta tag around a web image. A frame announces: this is the territory of the work; everything outside is something else. Cropping is a kind of frame too — the decision about where an image stops shapes what it means.

Designers control the relationship between image and frame deliberately. A full bleed pushes the image to the very edge, creating energy and immersion — the eye has nowhere to rest. A wide margin creates breathing room and formality, treating the image as an object to be contemplated. A partial bleed lets the image escape on one or two sides while staying contained on others, creating asymmetric tension. A border closes the image off, framing it like a painting. Cropping tightens onto a detail, implying continuation beyond.

Demo 08 — Switch framing modes
The same image, five framings. Each one changes the rhythm of attention and the implied scale.

Cropping

Cutting an image to reframe it. A close crop intensifies; a wide crop contextualizes.

Margins

Empty space around content. Breathing room, formality, and a place for the eye to land.

Bleeds

Imagery that extends past the trim edge. Creates immersion and visual energy.

Borders & rules

Lines that contain and define territory, separating one zone from another.

Captions

Verbal frames. They tell you how to look at the image — and sometimes change what you see.

Chapter Nine

Hierarchy

"Design is the conscious effort to impose a meaningful order." — Victor Papanek

Visual hierarchy is the arrangement of elements in order of importance, so the eye reads through them in a deliberate sequence. Without hierarchy a page is democratic to a fault — every word equally insistent, no path through the content. With hierarchy the designer says: look here first, then here, then here, releasing the reader from the burden of triage.

The signals are familiar but combine in countless ways. Scale declares importance loudly. Weight (bold versus regular) introduces emphasis without changing size. Color draws the eye to a single accent in an otherwise neutral field. Spacing separates groups and creates pauses. Alignment establishes a structural grammar. The strongest hierarchies use several of these signals in concert, each reinforcing the others.

Chapter Ten

Layers

"Under cities you always find other cities, like geological strata." — Pablo Picasso

Layers are stacks of information. In printing they are literal: a four-color separation is four sheets of acetate, each carrying one ink. In design software a layer is a transparent plane on which a single piece of content sits, freely moved and recombined. In collage the layers are torn paper. In a map they are roads on top of terrain on top of administrative boundaries. In music notation they are voices on a staff.

Designers reach for layers when they want to coordinate independent pieces of information in the same field without merging them. A magazine cover stacks photography, headline, masthead, and barcode. A user interface stacks content, controls, navigation, and notifications. Each layer can be edited without disturbing the rest, and the relations between them — opacity, blending, masking — are themselves part of the composition.

Demo 10 — Toggle the layers
Four independent layers — a blue field, a yellow sun, red diagonal hatching, and a word — combine into a single image. Toggle any of them off to see how the composite changes.

Physical layers

Print separations and physical collage. Each sheet carries one piece of the final image.

Digital layers

Independent planes in software (Photoshop, Figma, After Effects) — editable, transparent, stackable.

Collage

The deliberate juxtaposition of fragments from different sources, layered into a new whole.

Data layers

Maps, dashboards, and diagrams that overlay variables — roads above terrain above zoning.

Temporal layers

In music and motion, simultaneous voices that align in time but remain distinct.

Chapter Eleven

Transparency

"Transparency means a simultaneous perception of different spatial locations." — Gyorgy Kepes

Transparency is the property of seeing one thing through another. In physical life it lives in glass, water, vellum, and gauze; in print it is the overlap of inks, the trace of a watermark, the gradient of a screened tint. In software it is opacity, blending mode, alpha channel — the ability to set any layer to any percentage of presence and watch new colors emerge where layers cross.

Gyorgy Kepes, writing in The Language of Vision, observed that transparency creates a paradox of position: an object that is transparent occupies two places at once, both in front of and behind. Designers use that paradox to imply depth on a flat surface, to compress multiple meanings into a single image, and to dissolve sharp boundaries between layers. A logo set in 30% opacity over an image becomes a watermark; a colored shape at 50% over another creates a mixed third color that belongs to neither.

Demo 11 — Drag the sliders
Where the two semi-transparent rectangles overlap, a third color appears — neither red nor blue, but a mixture that exists only at the intersection.

Opacity

The degree to which a layer obscures what is beneath it, from 0% (invisible) to 100% (opaque).

Graphic transparency

An illusion of layering produced by overlapping lines, hatches, or shapes — without literal alpha.

Blend modes

Mathematical rules for combining stacked layers — multiply, screen, overlay, difference.

Fades & dissolves

Animated transparency over time — a layer appearing or disappearing gradually.

Chapter Twelve

Modularity

"Two eight-stud LEGO bricks can be combined in twenty-four ways. Six bricks, in over a hundred million." — LEGO Group

A module is a unit that combines with copies of itself to make a larger whole. The Roman alphabet is modular: twenty-six letters can spell every word in English. A LEGO brick is modular: a small number of shapes generates an effectively infinite set of structures. In design, modular systems liberate rather than constrain — the rules of combination, once fixed, free the designer to focus on what is being said with the modules rather than how each module is drawn.

Modular thinking underlies most contemporary design systems: corporate identity programs that compose every artifact from a fixed kit of parts; pixel typefaces, where every letter is built from on/off cells on a grid; component libraries in software, where buttons, cards, and dialogs are assembled from shared atoms. The pixel grid below is one of the simplest possible modular systems: 81 cells, each on or off. Out of those two bits per cell flow letters, ornaments, symbols, faces — anything you can draw at 9×9.

Demo 12 — Click cells to build a letterform
A 9×9 grid of binary cells. The same 81 modules can produce every letter of an alphabet, plus icons, symbols, and ornaments — bounded creativity from a minimal kit.

Module

A unit designed to combine with copies of itself. The atom of a modular system.

Constraint liberates

Fixing the kit of parts removes a class of decisions and clarifies what remains.

Pixel typefaces

Letters built from on/off grid cells. The native typography of low-resolution displays.

Design systems

Component libraries that compose entire products from a finite vocabulary of parts.

Chapter Thirteen

Grid

"Typography is mostly an act of dividing a limited surface." — Willi Baumeister

A grid is an invisible armature of columns, rows, margins, and gutters that subdivides a page or screen into manageable units. The Swiss grid tradition, codified by Josef Müller-Brockmann in the 1950s, treats the grid as a tool of democracy: there is no privileged center, every position is equivalent, and the designer's job is to find the most legible distribution of content across a neutral field. Many of the most enduring publication and identity systems were built on such grids.

Grids are not cages. They are starting points from which a designer departs. The most interesting compositions tend to follow a grid for most of their content while breaking it precisely where attention is wanted. A 12-column grid is a classic default because it subdivides cleanly into 2, 3, 4, and 6 columns; a designer can begin a layout in one configuration and shift fluidly into another. Chapter Fourteen

Pattern

"The secret to success in all ornament is the production of a broad general effect by the repetition of a few simple elements." — Owen Jones, The Grammar of Ornament

Pattern is the repetition of an element across a surface according to a rule. The simplest patterns repeat a single element on a regular interval — dots in a grid, stripes at fixed spacing, diamonds in a tessellation. More elaborate patterns combine motifs that interlock, vary in scale, or shift in color across the field. Every textile tradition, every wallpaper, every tiled floor in history is an essay in pattern.

The modern designer has access not only to the historical vocabulary of ornament but to algorithmic methods of pattern generation. A few lines of code can produce a randomized scatter, a Voronoi tessellation, a recursive subdivision, or a binary-tree branching. The canvas below cycles through five patterns, each generated procedurally — the same idea Owen Jones articulated in 1856, but produced by the rules of a program rather than the hand of a draftsman.

Five procedural patterns, each generated live from a small rule. Dots, stripes, and diamonds tile mechanically; scatter introduces randomness; the binary tree is recursive.

Dots

Isolated points repeated across a field. The simplest pattern element.

Stripes

Parallel lines at regular intervals — the simplest one-dimensional pattern.

Grids & tessellations

Two-dimensional tilings that fill the plane without gaps or overlaps.

Figure / ground in pattern

Every repeating element creates an equivalent repeating space between elements.

Cultural ornament

Chapter Fifteen

Diagram

"In emphasizing evidential quality and beauty, the design of statistical graphics should serve the reader, not the designer." — Edward R. Tufte

A diagram makes relationships visible. Where an illustration depicts what something looks like, a diagram depicts how it works, or how its parts connect. A subway map is a diagram of stations and lines; a family tree is a diagram of descent; a network graph is a diagram of nodes and edges. The discipline of information design — codified by Edward Tufte, Otto Neurath, and the Isotype team — asks how to present data so that the structure of the data, not the cleverness of the designer, comes through.

Paul Baran's famous 1964 trio of diagrams contrasted three network topologies. A centralized network has one hub through which all traffic passes — fragile, since the hub is a single point of failure. A decentralized network has a spine of regional hubs — more robust, still structured. A distributed network has no privileged nodes; every point connects directly to several neighbors — the topology of the Internet. The same three nodes, in three relationships, generate three radically different systems.

Demo 15 — Three network topologies
Three diagrams of the same set of nodes. The relationship between them — not the nodes themselves — defines the system.

Nodes and edges

The two atoms of any network diagram — points of activity and the connections between them.

Centralized

One hub, many spokes. Efficient but fragile — the hub is a single point of failure.

Decentralized

Multiple regional hubs. Compromise between efficiency and resilience.

Distributed

No privileged nodes. Maximum redundancy, the topology of the Internet.

Information vs propaganda

Tufte's distinction: clear data presentation serves the reader; rhetorical chartjunk serves the designer.

Chapter Sixteen

Time & Motion

"Every drawing can be understood as a motion study, since it is born from movement." — László Moholy-Nagy

Motion adds time to design. A static composition manages tension across the surface of the page; a moving composition manages tension across the duration of an animation. Designers working with motion think in terms of in-points and out-points, easing curves, beats, pauses, and pacing — borrowed in equal measure from film, music, and dance.

Even on the page, motion is often implied. A diagonal line moves the eye; a tilted figure leans into the next frame; a sequence of repeated shapes establishes a pulse. Eadweard Muybridge's nineteenth-century motion studies and the Italian Futurists' multi-exposed figures showed that drawing could record time as well as position. Today, the same instincts are exercised in animated logos, interface transitions, and scroll-driven storytelling.

There are roughly seven independent dimensions along which an object can change over time: its position, rotation, scale, shape, color, layer depth, and transparency. The richest animations modulate several of these at once.

Demo 16 — Seven modes of change
A single shape demonstrates each of the seven independent modes of change. Compound animations combine several at once.

Implied motion

The illusion of movement in a static image — diagonals, blur, repeated forms, leaning bodies.

Easing

The acceleration curve of an animation. Linear feels mechanical; eased curves feel natural.

Storyboards

Sequences of frames that plan a motion piece shot by shot, beat by beat.

Style frames

High-fidelity stills that establish the visual language of a motion piece without animating it.

Interactive motion

Motion driven by user input — scroll, hover, click — rather than by a fixed timeline.

Chapter Seventeen

Rules & Randomness

"The idea becomes a machine that makes the art." — Sol LeWitt, Paragraphs on Conceptual Art

Sol LeWitt's wall drawings are instructions, not images. The artist writes a rule — "ten thousand straight lines, ten thousand random lines" — and a draftsperson executes it. The output is not a single picture but a class of pictures, each one obeying the rule, each one different. This is the central insight of rule-based design: a system specified at a higher level than the artifact can generate many artifacts, and the variation between them is part of the work.

Digital tools take this further. A handful of variables — number of elements, range of sizes, palette, distance threshold — define a space of possible compositions. The designer adjusts the rules, the computer samples the space, and an entire identity, publication, or interface can be generated as variations on a theme. Programs like Processing, p5.js, and modern shader languages made this approach accessible to designers. Casey Reas (C.E.B. Reas), one of Processing's co-creators, has spent his career exploring how rules and randomness can collaborate.

The canvas below sketches the idea. A few sliders define a small generative system; pressing Generate samples a new artifact within those rules. Tight rules and a fixed seed would produce a stable identity. Looser rules with more randomness produce a family of related but never-repeated images.

Demo 17 — Generative system
Four parameters define a generative system. Each press of Generate samples one composition from the space of all possible compositions the rules allow.

Algorithmic design

Designing the system that designs the artifact. The work is the rule, not just its output.

Processing & p5.js

Programming environments for designers, popularized by Reas and Ben Fry around 2001.

Variables

The dials of a generative system. Adjusting them traverses the space of possible outputs.

Rules vs randomness

Pure rules produce sameness; pure randomness produces noise. The interesting region is between.

Sol LeWitt

Conceptual artist whose wall drawings demonstrated that an instruction can be a work.

The Book

Graphic Design: The New Basics is a foundational textbook by Ellen Lupton and Jennifer Cole Phillips, published by Princeton Architectural Press and developed out of the design program at the Maryland Institute College of Art (MICA). It treats the principles of design as a teachable, testable system — formstorming, point and line, color, gestalt, modularity, motion — bridging modernist visual training and contemporary software-based practice.
