Part One

The Foundational Insight

The single most important idea in the entire book — and the idea most copywriters get catastrophically wrong.

"The copywriter does not create mass desire — he channels and directs it."

Mass desire pre-exists your product. It is already out there, moving through the market like an underground river. People already want to lose weight, be admired, feel safe, grow richer, live longer. Your job is not to create this desire. Your job is to find it, harness it, and direct it specifically toward your product.

This is the central distinction between amateur advertising and professional advertising. Amateurs try to convince people to want things. Professionals recognize what people already want and show them why this particular product satisfies that desire better than anything else.

Key Insight

Copy does not create desire. It takes the mass desire already present in a marketplace and focuses that desire on your specific product. You cannot create the desire — but you can amplify, concentrate, and direct it.

The Three Dimensions of Mass Desire

Every mass desire has three measurable dimensions. Your copy must evaluate all three before you write a single word. They determine how hard you must work — and how rich the reward can be.

I

Urgency

How intensely does the market want this right now? How many times a day does the prospect think about this desire? How much does it dominate their attention, their conversation, their dreams? The more frequently and intensely it occupies mind-space, the easier it is to trigger with your headline.

II

Staying Power

How long will this desire last? Is it seasonal? Is it a permanent feature of human psychology, or a passing trend? A desire with long staying power (health, wealth, love) allows you to build a franchise around your product. A trend only gives you a single season's sale.

III

Scope

How many people share this desire simultaneously? This determines the potential size of your market. A desire felt by millions of people at once is more commercially valuable than a desire felt by thousands. Scope multiplied by urgency gives you the economic potential of the market.

The Automobile Example: When Giants Stumble

Schwartz uses the American automobile industry to demonstrate what happens when advertisers confuse product creation with market creation. Ford, Chrysler, and GM did not create the mass desire for personal transportation — that desire existed before the automobile was invented. The horse and buggy industry was already channeling it.

What the automobile makers discovered — and what advertising made possible — was that you could redefine how a desire gets satisfied, not the desire itself. The desire to travel, to be free, to project status, to be comfortable — these were permanent. The automobile simply offered a new mechanism for fulfilling them.

The lesson of the Edsel: Ford tried to create desire for a car rather than channel an existing desire. They built the Edsel based on what engineers and executives thought people should want, not what people actually desired. The market was completely indifferent. The car died. Nearly $400 million went with it.

You do not create desire. You find it — flowing through the market like a great river — and you channel it toward your product. The Edsel failed because its creators tried to dam the river rather than redirect it.

— Paraphrasing Schwartz's core principle

The Amplification Effect

One of the most remarkable claims in the book — and one Schwartz supports with detailed argument — is the economic leverage that properly channeled copy creates.

$1 Ad Spend
→
$50 to
$100 in Sales
=
50–100× Amplification

Schwartz argues that truly great copy — copy that perfectly channels a mass desire — can amplify every dollar of ad spend into fifty to one hundred dollars in sales. This is not hyperbole. It is the economic justification for why world-class copy is so extraordinarily valuable, and why mediocre copy is so extraordinarily wasteful.

The leverage exists because you are not persuading people from scratch. You are igniting a desire that was already there, already pressurized, waiting for the right spark. When your headline strikes that spark perfectly, the response is enormous relative to the investment.

Permanent Forces vs. Forces of Change

Schwartz distinguishes between two types of market forces that your copy must account for:

Permanent Forces Always Present
  • The desire for health, longer life, freedom from pain
  • The desire for money, wealth, financial security
  • The desire for love, admiration, social status
  • The desire for safety, comfort, relief from fear
  • The desire to be a good parent, provider, spouse
  • Vanity, pride, the desire to appear youthful
  • Hunger for knowledge, self-improvement, mastery
Forces of Change Shift Over Time
  • New scientific discoveries that reframe health desires
  • Economic upheaval shifting financial anxieties
  • War, crisis, or social change creating new fears
  • Technological innovation opening new possibilities
  • Cultural shifts changing what status symbols mean
  • New demographic groups entering the market
  • Previous advertising that has educated the market

Permanent forces give you the bedrock of your appeal. Forces of change tell you how to frame that appeal right now, in this market, at this moment in history. The best copy anchors itself to a permanent force and updates its expression through the forces of change.

Part Two

The Three-Question Framework

Before writing a single word of copy — especially before writing a headline — you must answer three questions precisely. Every word of your ad flows from these answers.

"The headline is not the first thing you write. It is the last. First you must know everything about the desire, the awareness, and the sophistication of your market."
Q1

What is the Mass Desire this product is meant to satisfy?

Product Analysis — Understanding what the product does, not what it is

Before you can channel a desire, you must identify it with surgical precision. This means conducting a thorough product analysis — and Schwartz draws a critical distinction between two ways of thinking about any product:

The Physical Product

What the product is. Its physical description, components, ingredients, construction. This is how engineers describe it, how manufacturers catalog it, how warehouse managers inventory it.

Example: "A capsule containing 500mg of compound X, manufactured in gelatin casing, packaged in bottles of 60."

This is the wrong starting point for copy. Nobody buys a physical product. They buy what it does for them.

The Functional Product

What the product does. The benefit it produces, the problem it solves, the transformation it enables. This is how your prospect thinks about it — in terms of what it gives them.

Example: "Thirty days from now you will have lost the weight you've been trying to lose for years — without hunger, without exhaustion, without giving up the foods you love."

This is your starting point. Map the full chain from product to ultimate benefit.

The mass desire lives at the end of the functional chain. Start from "what does this product do?" and keep asking "and what does that mean for the buyer?" until you reach the deepest emotional reality. That is the desire you are channeling.

Q2

What is the State of Awareness of your market?

How much does your prospect already know — about you, about your product, about what you're selling?

The State of Awareness determines what your headline must accomplish and how directly you can approach your prospect. A market that already knows your product needs only a reminder and a deal. A market that has never heard of your product — or of solutions to their problem at all — requires an entirely different approach.

Schwartz identifies five distinct awareness stages, each demanding a different headline strategy. Getting this wrong wastes every dollar you spend. If you write a "Most Aware" headline for a "Completely Unaware" market, your prospect simply won't recognize that the ad is meant for them.

This question leads directly to the Five Stages of Awareness, detailed in the next section. Your awareness analysis tells you: Where is my prospect on the journey from ignorance to purchase-ready?

Q3

What is the State of Sophistication of your market?

How many products like yours have already been advertised to this market?

Sophistication is a function of time and competition. In a brand-new market, people haven't heard any claims yet — so any direct claim is fresh and compelling. In a mature market, people have heard every claim imaginable — so the same direct claim is stale, unbelievable, or invisible.

The State of Sophistication determines how much work your claim has to do. In Stage 1, a simple "it works" is enough. In Stage 5, you must reach the prospect at the level of personal identity — because every rational and emotional argument has already been made by your competitors.

This question also leads to a five-stage framework, detailed further on. Your sophistication analysis tells you: How jaded is my market, and what level of claim or mechanism will break through?

The Interaction Effect

Awareness and Sophistication interact. A market can be Completely Unaware of a product category but simultaneously Stage 5 Sophisticated about the underlying desire. Your copy must navigate both dimensions simultaneously.

Part Three

The 5 Stages of Awareness

Every prospect exists somewhere on this scale. Your headline's entire job is to meet them exactly where they are — then move them one step closer to purchase.

1

Most Aware

Your prospect knows your product and wants it — just give them the deal

▼

At Stage 1, your prospect knows who you are, knows your product, and wants it. They are essentially raising their hand. The only question remaining is: At what price? or On what terms? Your headline needs only to restate the product name and the offer.

This is the most economical stage to write for, because your copy does not need to build any case. The market has already been educated, either by your previous advertising or by the product's dominant market position. You simply need to make the offer visible and attractive.

What Your Headline Must Do

Name the product, state the price or offer, give enough detail to confirm this is exactly what they want. Nothing more is needed — and anything more may actually dilute the response.

Classic Stage 1 Headline Revere Zomar Lens... Now Only $119.95

This headline works because the reader already knows what a Revere Zomar Lens is, already wants one, and is simply checking prices. The headline needs only to announce that the desired object is now available at an attractive price.

2

Product Known, Not Yet Desired

Your prospect knows your product exists but hasn't been moved to want it

▼

At Stage 2, your prospect knows your product — perhaps by name, perhaps by category — but has not yet been convinced they want it. The gap between awareness and desire has not been bridged. Your headline must do that bridging work.

Schwartz outlines seven specific tasks the headline must accomplish at this stage. Each is a distinct way of creating desire where awareness already exists:

  1. Reinforce the prospect's existing desire for a product like yours, then show how your product uniquely satisfies that desire
  2. Sharpen and focus the prospect's vague or diffuse desire into a specific, clear want for your exact product
  3. Show the prospect the desire behind your product that they haven't yet consciously connected to it
  4. Acknowledge a dissatisfaction with current alternatives, then position your product as the solution to that specific dissatisfaction
  5. Take a concrete new development in your product and turn it into a desire-generator — use news to create want
  6. Develop a use or application the prospect hadn't considered, opening entirely new desire contexts for your product
  7. Expand the prospect's image of what owning the product means — escalate the emotional, status, or identity payoff
Stage 2 Example — Steinway Piano "The instrument of the immortals"
Stage 2 Example — Rolls-Royce "At 60 miles an hour the loudest noise in this new Rolls-Royce comes from the electric clock"
Stage 2 Example — Hertz "Hertz puts you in the driver's seat"

Each of these examples uses a vivid, specific detail or image to create desire in someone who was already aware of the product's existence but hadn't yet been moved to want it specifically.

3

Desire Known, Product Unknown

Your prospect knows what they want but doesn't know your product can give it to them

▼

At Stage 3, the desire is conscious and active in your prospect — but they don't yet know your product satisfies it. They are already looking. They may be actively shopping, researching, or simply hoping that something exists to solve their problem. Your headline must name the desire or the solution and show that your product is the answer.

The copy strategy here is to lead with the desire or the solution, not the product. You meet the prospect at the desire level, then introduce your product as the vehicle for fulfilling it.

What Your Headline Must Do

Call out the desire directly, or name the solution. The product is introduced as the mechanism by which the desire gets satisfied. The headline is desire-led, not product-led.

Stage 3 Classic — Cigarette Campaign "Light a Lucky and you'll never miss sweets that make you fat"
Stage 3 Classic — Dale Carnegie "How to Win Friends and Influence People"

In both cases, the desire is primary — slimness, social effectiveness — and the product (cigarettes, a book) is positioned as the means of achieving it. The prospect recognizes their own desire in the headline and is drawn in to learn how the product fulfills it.

4

Need Known, Product Unknown

Your prospect feels the problem but hasn't framed it as a solvable desire yet

▼

At Stage 4, the prospect experiences a problem but may not have fully articulated it as a desire for a solution. They know something is wrong. They are uncomfortable, frustrated, or suffering — but they haven't yet reached the point of actively seeking a product to solve it. Your headline must crystallize their diffuse discomfort into a specific, recognizable problem and hint that a solution exists.

The technique here is to name the problem in the most stark, immediate, recognizable terms possible. The single-word problem-statement can be devastating in its efficiency: it catches exactly the right person mid-sentence in their own inner monologue.

Stage 4 — Single-Word Problem Statement "Corns?"
Stage 4 — Problem with Solution Hint "Shrinks Hemorrhoids Without Surgery"

"Corns?" stops exactly the right person. They don't need to be told what corns are. They don't need the problem elaborated. The single word, posed as a question, mirrors their own ongoing internal complaint and promises that what follows will help. Efficiency is the virtue here — the prospect self-selects instantly.

"Shrinks Hemorrhoids Without Surgery" works similarly: it names the problem and immediately addresses the primary objection (fear of surgery) before any further engagement. The headline does the heavy filtering work instantly.

5

Completely Unaware

Your prospect doesn't know they have a problem, a desire, or that you exist

▼

This is the most challenging and most creative stage. Your prospect is not looking for a solution, does not know they have a problem, has never thought about your product, and would not recognize a direct statement of what you're selling as relevant to them. You cannot mention the product. You cannot name the desire. You must reach them through a different door entirely.

Schwartz's solution: identification headlines. Rather than naming the desire or the product, you create a headline that identifies the type of person who has the problem you solve — in terms so vivid and accurate that the right reader immediately recognizes themselves and reads on to find out more.

What Your Headline Must Do

Stop the right person cold with a statement, fact, story, or question that is so perfectly aimed at who they are — not what they want — that they cannot help but keep reading. The connection to your product is made later, inside the copy.

Stage 5 — Identification via Aspiration "University of the Night"
Stage 5 — Identification via Problem State "Why Men Crack"
Stage 5 — Identification via Social Situation "Often a Bridesmaid, Never a Bride"
Stage 5 — Identification via Shared Experience "They Laughed When I Sat Down at the Piano — But When I Started to Play!"
Stage 5 — Identification via Character "Blow Some My Way" (Cigarette ad directed at women)

None of these headlines mention a product. None of them name a desire in direct terms. But each one stops a very specific type of person who suddenly feels that this advertisement is speaking directly and privately to them. The headline's only job is to make that person read the next sentence. The copy then does the work of introducing the product.

Part Four

The 5 Stages of Sophistication

How jaded is your market? Every claim you could make has been made before — the question is how many times, and what you must do differently as a result.

"In a new market, you can sell with a single word. In a tired market, you must sell with an idea so fresh, so personal, so deeply embedded in who the reader is, that no competitor has touched it."

Schwartz traces the full arc of market sophistication through the history of the cigarette industry — a perfect case study because it covers all five stages across several decades of advertising history. Follow the cigarette industry from its first mass campaigns through Marlboro's iconic imagery to understand how sophistication forces innovation at every stage.

1
Stage 1 — First to Market

State the Claim Directly

You are the first product in a new market. Nobody has made this type of claim before. The desire is there — hungry, waiting, entirely unfulfilled by advertising because no one has spoken to it yet. Your only job is to make the claim as boldly and as clearly as possible.

There is no need for subtlety, no need for mechanism, no need for sophisticated positioning. The sheer novelty of the claim — the fact that someone is finally saying this is possible — does all the work. Direct, giant, unhedged claims win Stage 1 markets.

Stage 1 — Weight Loss Example "NOW! LOSE UGLY FAT!"
Cigarette market, early days: Camel's first mass campaigns simply announced the pleasure of smoking. The desire existed; the advertising only had to say "yes, this is the thing that gives you that pleasure."
2
Stage 2 — Competitors Emerge

Enlarge the Claim

Now competitors have made the same basic claim. Your audience has heard "lose weight" before. The claim alone no longer breaks through — it has become invisible through repetition. Your solution: enlarge the claim. Make it more specific, more dramatic, more measurable, more extreme.

Bigger numbers, faster results, more precise outcomes. The claim is still the lead — but it must out-shout and out-specifize every previous version of the claim the market has heard.

Stage 2 — Enlarged Claim "LOSE UP TO 47 POUNDS IN 4 WEEKS"
Lucky Strike entered: "It's Toasted" — a differentiated claim about process, slightly enlarging/distinguishing from generic cigarette pleasure claims by implying their tobacco was specially treated for superiority.
3
Stage 3 — Claims Are Exhausted

Introduce the Mechanism

Claims are now everywhere. Every competitor has made every conceivable version of the core claim. Consumers have become skeptical — they've heard it all. Simply saying "lose weight fast" triggers a cynical "yeah, sure" response. You must introduce a mechanism: the specific, named, unique reason your product works. The mechanism replaces the claim as the headline's lead.

The mechanism doesn't need to be new — it may be something your product has always done. But by naming it for the first time, you give the prospect something new to believe in. A specific mechanism is more credible than a general claim.

Stage 3 — Mechanism Replaces Claim "FLOATS FAT RIGHT OUT OF YOUR BODY!"
Filter cigarettes arrive. The mechanism — "filters out harmful elements" — becomes the new claim frontier. Each brand races to own the filter story. Mechanism has replaced simple pleasure as the headline approach.
4
Stage 4 — Mechanisms Are Exhausted

Elaborate and Own the Mechanism

Now every competitor has a mechanism. Every brand has introduced its own scientific explanation, its own named process, its own proprietary system. Mechanisms have become as stale as claims. Your solution: go deeper into your mechanism. Own it more thoroughly, elaborate it more richly, make it more vivid, more scientific, more specific, more exclusive.

Often the strategy here is to name your mechanism as the first and only of its kind — "the first," "the only," "no other product can claim." Exclusivity of mechanism re-energizes the claim even when the basic benefit is well-worn.

Stage 4 — Elaborated Mechanism "FIRST NO-DIET REDUCING WONDER DRUG — Discovered by European doctors, now available in America for the first time"
Cigarette filter wars: each brand claims its filter is uniquely superior. Micronite filters, charcoal filters, recessed filters — the mechanism is elaborated and differentiated as the primary competitive battlefield.
5
Stage 5 — Complete Saturation

Identification — Bypass the Claim Entirely

The market has heard every claim and every mechanism. Rational and emotional arguments have been exhausted. Nothing you can say about the product will break through, because the prospect has built immune defenses against every type of product pitch. The only avenue left is to bypass the product pitch entirely and connect at the level of personal identity.

Stage 5 copy does not sell the product. It sells a self-image. It shows the prospect the kind of person they want to be — or the kind of person they believe they already are — and then associates your product with that identity. The product becomes an emblem, not an object.

Stage 5 — Identity, Not Claim "WHY MEN CRACK" — (Identifying the prospect as someone under pressure, a serious person with serious responsibilities)
Stage 5 — The Marlboro Tattoo Ads No claims. No mechanisms. Just the image of the roughneck cowboy with a tattoo on his wrist — the ultimate icon of masculine independence. The cigarette is present but not mentioned.
Marlboro's complete transformation — from a "ladies' cigarette" to the most masculine brand in history — was accomplished entirely through Stage 5 identification. The cowboy, the landscape, the tattoo: all pure identity, zero product claim. It remains one of the most effective repositioning campaigns in advertising history.
The Stage 5 Paradox

At Stage 5, the less you say about the product, the more powerfully you sell it. Direct claims are counterproductive. Silence, image, and identity do the selling. The product is simply present in the right context.

Part Five

38 Ways to Strengthen Your Headline

Every headline can be made stronger. These 38 specific techniques, each with a concrete example, are the complete toolkit for headline strengthening from Schwartz's own analysis.

01
Specificity

Add a Specific Number

Replace vague claims with exact figures. Specificity implies testing, implies confidence, implies truth.

"Lose 47 Pounds" instead of "Lose Weight Fast"
02
Specificity

Add a Specific Time Frame

Give the prospect a clear timeline for their result. This converts an abstract benefit into a concrete promise.

"In 4 Weeks" — "By Next Tuesday" — "In 30 Days or Less"
03
News & Novelty

Use a "How To" Introduction

The words "How to" promise specific, actionable instruction. They tell the prospect: what follows is practical, useful, immediately applicable information.

"How to Win Friends and Influence People"
04
News & Novelty

Add the Word "New"

One of the most powerful words in advertising. "New" signals a fresh solution to an old problem — it bypasses the prospect's previous disappointments with similar products.

"New Formula," "New Discovery," "Now, a New Way to..."
05
Emotion

Start with a Challenge or Dare

Challenge the reader's current beliefs or situation. The emotional tension of being challenged drives reading — nobody walks away from a direct challenge to their self-image.

"Are You Making These Mistakes in English?"
06
Identification

Address the Reader Directly

Use "you" or speak directly to a specific type of person. Creates immediate personal relevance — the reader feels singled out.

"To Men Who Want to Quit Work Someday"
07
Emotion

Use a Startling Statement

Open with a fact, statistic, or claim so surprising it demands explanation. The reader cannot proceed without knowing more.

"The Amazing Diet Secret of a Desperate Housewife"
08
Credibility

Include a Testimonial or Authority

Third-party validation in the headline instantly lends credibility. The reader doesn't have to take the advertiser's word for it.

"Harvard Study Reveals..." or "Doctors Confirm..."
09
Emotion

Use a Story Opening

Begin with the seed of a narrative. Humans are wired to follow stories. Even a two-word story beginning compels completion.

"They Laughed When I Sat Down at the Piano — But When I Started to Play!"
10
Mechanism

Name the Mechanism

Give your product's key process a name. A named mechanism feels proprietary, testable, and real — even if the underlying process is common to all competitors.

"The Vitamin X Formula," "The 3-Step Sleep Protocol"
11
News & Novelty

Promise a Secret or Revelation

People are drawn to hidden knowledge. "The secret of..." or "What they don't want you to know about..." activates intense curiosity.

"The Secret of Making People Like You"
12
Specificity

Add a Geographic Qualifier

Localizing a claim or story makes it feel more credible and more relevant to that specific audience.

"Ohio Farmer Discovers..."
13
Identification

Identify a Specific Audience

Name the exact type of person this ad is for. Those who don't qualify will ignore it; those who do will feel it's tailor-made for them.

"For Men Over 40 Who Are Tired of Feeling Old"
14
Emotion

Use Fear or Negative Emotion

Fear is a powerful motivator. Headlines that highlight a threat, danger, or negative outcome can be tremendously effective when the fear is real and relevant.

"Why Men Crack" — "Are You Ever Tongue-Tied at a Party?"
15
Credibility

Use "Which" or Comparative Structure

Presenting two alternatives and asking the reader to choose makes the claim feel more objective and fair. It acknowledges complexity while still guiding the reader to a conclusion.

"Which of These 5 Skin Troubles Would You Like to End?"
16
News & Novelty

Use the Word "Introducing"

Signals a launch, a debut, a first. Gives even an established product a fresh-start feeling. Works especially well when backed by a genuine innovation.

"Introducing the First Car Built for City Living"
17
Emotion

Use Irony or Unexpected Contrast

Juxtaposing what people expect with something surprising creates cognitive dissonance that demands resolution — and therefore, reading.

"Often a Bridesmaid, Never a Bride"
18
Specificity

Name a Concrete Example

Instead of a generalized claim, give a single concrete instance. The specific case is always more believable and more vivid than the general claim.

"This Man Paid His Mortgage Off in 3 Years on a Teacher's Salary"
19
Mechanism

Describe the Mechanism in Action

Show the mechanism doing its work — "floats fat out," "seals in freshness," "locks out bacteria." Action verbs make mechanisms vivid and credible.

"FLOATS FAT RIGHT OUT OF YOUR BODY"
20
Identification

Use a Character or Role

Give the reader a character to inhabit — a role that represents who they want to be. They read on to discover how to achieve that identity.

"University of the Night" — "The Man Who Could Work Miracles"
21
Credibility

Give a Guarantee

A specific, bold guarantee in the headline eliminates risk and dramatically increases believability. It shows the advertiser's confidence in the product.

"Lose 30 Pounds or Your Money Back — No Questions Asked"
22
Emotion

Ask a Question

Questions engage the reader's mind directly. The reader cannot help but generate an answer — and in generating that answer, they become invested in the ad's resolution.

"Do You Make These Mistakes in English?"
23
News & Novelty

Lead with "Announcing"

Creates a sense of event, of something genuinely newsworthy. Frames the ad as a news item rather than a pitch — borrowing credibility from journalism.

"Announcing the New Edition of the Encyclopedia That Makes Learning Fun"
24
Specificity

Use Price as a Qualifier

A specific price in the headline does dual work: it pre-qualifies buyers and makes the offer feel concrete and real, not vague and hypothetical.

"Revere Zomar Lens... Now Only $119.95"
25
Emotion

Promise Ease or Effortlessness

Removing the barrier of effort is one of the most powerful motivators. If the prospect's primary objection is "it's too hard," directly counter that objection in the headline.

"Effortless Weight Loss While You Sleep"
26
Identification

Identify a Shared Enemy or Problem

Unite the reader with you against a common adversary — whether a disease, an industry, a belief system, or a social problem. Shared enemies create powerful tribal bonds.

"The Truth the Drug Companies Don't Want You to Know"
27
Mechanism

Claim "First" or "Only"

If you are genuinely the first or only product with a feature, say so explicitly. Exclusivity is one of the strongest possible claims — it cannot be matched by any competitor.

"The Only Mattress with Dual-Zone Support"
28
Credibility

Use Understatement Deliberately

In a world of screaming superlatives, quiet confidence is startling. Understatement implies such certainty that no exaggeration is needed — and signals authenticity.

"Think Small." — Volkswagen Beetle campaign
29
Emotion

Use Alliteration or Rhythm

Headlines that sound good are remembered better. Alliteration, rhythm, and sound patterns make headlines lodge in memory.

"Winston Tastes Good Like a Cigarette Should"
30
Specificity

Name the Specific Problem Eliminated

Instead of promising the positive outcome, name the specific negative that disappears. Elimination of a specific pain is often more motivating than acquisition of a general benefit.

"Goodbye Arthritis Pain" — "End Back Pain Forever"
31
News & Novelty

Lead with a Fascinating Fact

A counterintuitive, surprising, or little-known fact pulls the reader in through curiosity. Facts carry inherent credibility and imply depth behind the headline.

"The Amazing Story of a Bare-Footed Bedouin Who Became a Millionaire"
32
Identification

Create a "Before" State

Describe the reader's current situation — their pain, frustration, embarrassment — so accurately that they feel understood. Understanding creates trust.

"Sick and Tired of Being Sick and Tired?"
33
Mechanism

Name a Proprietary Process

Create a name for your product's unique process and lead with it. The name itself implies ownership, research, and refinement.

"The Rinso Active Suds Formula Lifts Dirt Without Scrubbing"
34
Credibility

Use "Because" to Give a Reason

Providing a reason — even a general one — dramatically increases compliance. The word "because" triggers an automatic assumption of legitimacy.

"Most People Fail to Save Money Because They Never Learn This One Trick"
35
Emotion

Invoke Pride or Shame

Pride in achievement and shame at failure are both powerful motivators. A headline that accurately names either state immediately resonates with the reader who feels it.

"Blow Some My Way" — (Pride in being the kind of woman who can say this)
36
News & Novelty

Borrow Newsworthiness

Connect your product to a current event, a cultural moment, or a trending topic. Your product becomes news by association.

"As Seen on the Wall Street Journal Front Page..."
37
Identification

Use Aspirational Self-Image

Show the reader not who they are, but who they want to become. The aspiration creates a gap — and your product is the bridge across it.

"The Marlboro Man" — pure aspirational identity, no product claim needed
38
Credibility

Acknowledge the Objection

Name the reader's primary doubt or objection directly in the headline, then counter it. This disarms skepticism before it can block engagement.

"Shrinks Hemorrhoids Without Surgery" — names and eliminates the #1 fear
Part Six

The 7 Techniques of Breakthrough Copy

Once you've solved the headline, these are the structural techniques that turn a good ad into a great one — building unstoppable belief and desire through the body of the copy.

Intensification

Making every element of the ad more vivid, more specific, more emotionally resonant. Intensification is not exaggeration — it is precision. The more exact your image, the more powerfully it strikes the reader's imagination.

Schwartz identifies 13 specific techniques for intensifying copy. Each transforms a flat, general claim into a vivid, specific, living reality in the prospect's mind:

  • 1
    Sensory Specification Replace abstract descriptions with specific sensory details. "Soft" becomes "smooth as a baby's cheek." "Fresh" becomes "the smell of rain on dry earth."
  • 2
    Exact Measurement Substitute measured quantities for approximate ones. "More than a foot long" becomes "14 inches long." Exactness implies testing, implies truth.
  • 3
    Action Verbs Replace static descriptions with dynamic action. "Contains" becomes "delivers." "Has" becomes "floods your system with." Verbs that move create copy that moves.
  • 4
    Comparison and Analogy Make the unfamiliar concrete through comparison. The rose example: instead of "a beautiful rose," describe each petal as though it were a separate living thing, trembling with color.
  • 5
    Expansion through Detail The model airplane example — instead of "detailed," describe every single part: the cockpit instruments, the riveted seams, the hand-painted identification numbers. Details accumulate into overwhelming vivid reality.
  • 6
    The Superlative in Use Don't say the product is the best. Show it being the best — in a specific situation, with a specific type of person, producing a specific outcome that only the best product could produce.
  • 7
    Before/After Contrast Show the sharpest possible contrast between the reader's current state and their state after using the product. The greater the contrast, the more powerful the desire it generates.
  • 8
    Scaling Up the Promise The fish lure example: don't say "catch more fish." Say "catch so many fish your arms ache from reeling them in." Scale the promise to its emotional maximum.
  • 9
    The Vivid Negative Sometimes intensifying the problem you solve — making the reader feel its full weight — is more powerful than intensifying the benefit. Make the pain vivid before you offer the cure.
  • 10
    Social Proof at Scale Move from "many people" to "327,482 men and women across 47 states." The more specific the social proof, the more credible and motivating it becomes.
  • 11
    The Guarantee as Intensifier The plant food guarantee example: "Your plants will grow or we'll replace them free" — not "satisfaction guaranteed." The specific guarantee intensifies the claim by putting real stakes behind it.
  • 12
    Time Pressure and Scarcity Intensify desire by limiting availability. A scarce or time-limited thing is more valuable than an always-available thing — this is basic human psychology made into copy technique.
  • 13
    The Cumulative List Stack benefit upon benefit, feature upon feature, until the weight of accumulated value becomes irresistible. Each additional element adds to the emotional mass of the offer.

Identification

The deepest technique in the book. Identification means making the reader see themselves — their best self, or their feared self — in your copy. When the reader thinks "that's me," they are already half-sold.

Schwartz distinguishes between two fundamental types of identification that copy can use to reach the reader at the level of self-image:

Character Roles

The reader identifies with a type of person — a character in a narrative or social situation. These are roles defined by circumstance, background, or social position.

  • The struggling young man trying to better himself
  • The mother protecting her family from illness
  • The professional who can't afford to fail
  • The romantic who wants to be more attractive
  • The bridesmaid who is never the bride

Character roles are entered through narrative — through a story that opens in a situation the reader recognizes as their own.

Achievement Roles

The reader identifies with the person they want to become — defined by what they will accomplish, achieve, or be recognized for. These are aspiration-based identities.

  • The man who can fix anything himself
  • The investor who saw opportunities others missed
  • The athlete who achieved what trainers said was impossible
  • The writer whose work is recognized and admired
  • The Marlboro Man — ultimate masculine independence

Achievement roles are entered through aspiration — through an image of mastery that the reader urgently wants to inhabit.

The Piston Ring Example

Schwartz uses the piston ring ad as a master example of character role identification. The ad opens not with the piston ring, not with its benefits, but with a man — a specific type of man — facing a specific type of problem. The reader who shares that type and that problem immediately recognizes himself and reads on.

The piston ring is introduced only after the reader is fully emotionally engaged with the character's situation. By that point, the reader is not buying a piston ring — he is solving his own problem, which happens to be exactly the problem the piston ring solves.

Identification — The Marlboro Tattoo Ads A man's hand, weathered and strong, with a tattoo on the wrist. The cigarette is barely visible. There is no claim, no headline, no promise. Just an image of a specific kind of man — and every man who wants to be that kind of man immediately feels the pull.
Identification — "Blow Some My Way" A cigarette ad directed at women at a time when women smoking was transgressive. The headline doesn't sell smoking — it sells a character: a woman confident, modern, slightly daring enough to ask a man to share his smoke. Women who wanted to be that woman bought the cigarettes.

Gradualization

The architecture of belief. How to bring a completely skeptical prospect to the point of certainty — step by step, claim by claim, proof by proof, without losing them at any transition.

Gradualization is the technique of never asking the reader to make a leap of faith that is larger than the evidence you've provided supports. Each step in the logical chain must be small enough that the reader can take it comfortably — and each step prepares the reader to take the next, slightly larger step.

The TV Repair Manual Ad — Step by Step

Schwartz uses the TV Repair Manual ad as the definitive example of Gradualization in action. The reader begins completely skeptical that they could learn to repair a TV. The copy builds belief through a carefully graduated sequence:

Step 1: Establish that TV repair is a skill, not a gift. It can be learned. Step 2: Show that others just like you have learned it. Step 3: Give a specific, simple example of a repair so easy that the reader thinks "I could do that." Step 4: Gradually escalate to more complex repairs — but by now the reader is already inside the world of believing. Step 5: Introduce the full scope of what mastery looks like — the income, the independence, the satisfaction. Step 6: Present the product as the logical next step for someone who is already, by now, a believer.

— Schwartz's architecture of the TV Repair Manual ad

Three Structural Tools of Gradualization

Inclusion Questions

Questions that the reader answers "yes" to — building a chain of agreement that carries them forward. "Have you ever wished you could...?" "Do you sometimes feel that...?" Each "yes" deepens the reader's investment in the copy and makes the next step easier to take.

Contingency Structures

"If... then..." constructions that connect the reader's current situation to the promised outcome. "If you can follow simple instructions, then you can repair any TV in your home." The conditional form makes the promise feel earned rather than asserted.

Syllogistic Thinking

Leading the reader through a chain of logical steps — each step indisputably true — that arrives inevitably at the conclusion you want them to reach. The reader feels they have reasoned their own way to the purchase decision, rather than being sold to.

Redefinition

Changing how the reader perceives something — the product, the price, the problem, the solution — so that what seemed impossible seems easy, what seemed expensive seems cheap, what seemed complex seems simple.

Redefinition operates on the frame around the offer, not the offer itself. By changing how something is perceived, you change the emotional response to it — without changing a single objective fact.

Three Forms of Redefinition

Simplification

Make a complex or intimidating subject feel manageable. The TV repair manual does this by showing that the "mysterious" television consists of only a handful of interchangeable parts. Once the complexity is stripped away, the reader's fear evaporates. Simplification removes the barrier of perceived difficulty.

Escalation

Upgrade how the reader perceives the value or importance of something. Schwartz uses vocabulary as an example — showing how the ability to use words precisely doesn't just make you sound smarter, it literally gives you more persuasive power in every human interaction: business, romance, friendship, negotiation. The skill is escalated from "vocabulary" to "the master key to all human influence."

Price Reduction

The SA Fire Injectors example: a product that seems expensive when presented as "X dollars" becomes a bargain when redefined through its story — hand-crafted, individually tested, a process that takes hours per unit. The price is objectively the same. But the perceived value has been completely transformed by redefining what went into making it. "Made by hand, one at a time" makes $19.95 feel like a steal for something that should cost $50.

Price Reduction — SA Fire Injectors The copy describes in loving detail the hand-crafting process: each unit individually machined, individually tested, individually inspected. By the time the price is revealed, the reader feels they are getting a handmade precision instrument at a factory price.

Mechanization

Naming and dramatizing the specific mechanism by which your product works. The mechanism converts vague claims into believable, specific, ownable truth.

A claim says: "This product works." A mechanized claim says: "This product works because of the Active Suds Formula, which penetrates dirt molecules and lifts them free of the fabric without scrubbing." The mechanism gives the reader something concrete to believe in — and something the competitor cannot easily copy, because it is now associated specifically with your product.

1

Name It

Give your product's key process a proprietary name. The name creates ownership and makes the mechanism memorable.

2

Describe It

Explain how the mechanism works — in plain language, vividly and specifically. Make the process feel real and demonstrable.

3

Feature It

Use the mechanism as your primary competitive differentiator. Lead with it in headlines, build the body copy around it.

The Rinso Example

Rinso's Active Suds Formula — previously just "soap" — became, through mechanization, a product with a specific named process that customers could believe in. "Active Suds" implied scientific formulation, implied testing, implied superiority over ordinary soap that just... washed. The name made the mechanism real, and the mechanism made the claim believable.

— Schwartz on the Rinso Active Suds campaign

Mechanism Headlines

Once you have named your mechanism, it can lead your headlines at Stage 3 and Stage 4 markets:

Mechanism Headline Examples "FLOATS FAT RIGHT OUT OF YOUR BODY!" (mechanism in action) "THE RINSO ACTIVE SUDS FORMULA LIFTS DIRT WITHOUT SCRUBBING" (mechanism as hero) "NEW 'TURTLE WAX' CREATES MIRROR-BRIGHT FINISH IN SECONDS" (mechanism + outcome)

Concentration

Focusing the reader's entire attention on a single, critical point — usually through the technique of contrast. Show the "before" and the "after" so sharply that the product's value becomes undeniable.

Concentration is the technique of eliminating everything extraneous from the reader's attention and bringing the full weight of the argument to bear on the single most persuasive comparison available. The contrast structure — bad vs. good, wrong vs. right, before vs. after — is concentration's primary tool.

The Spark Plug Structure

Schwartz's classic example: the spark plug ad shows first the worn, fouled, mis-firing spark plug — describes in vivid detail what it does to the engine, to gas mileage, to acceleration, to reliability. Then, in stark contrast, shows the new Champion spark plug firing cleanly, perfectly, every time. The contrast is so sharp that the value of the new plug is self-evident. The reader doesn't need to be told to buy it — they have already arrived at that conclusion through the concentrated force of the contrast.

— Schwartz on the power of concentrated contrast

The Reducing Pill Structure

The same concentration technique applied to a weight-loss product: first, a vivid and uncomfortably accurate description of the reader's current situation — the clothes that don't fit, the mirror that lies, the energy that isn't there. Then the sharp pivot to the new reality made possible by the product. The negative must be as vivid as the positive — concentration requires both poles to be fully developed, not just the positive outcome.

Concentration — The Before/After Pivot "Bad: [14 lines of vivid, specific, uncomfortable description of the problem] Good: [14 lines of equally vivid, specific, emotionally rich description of the solution]" — The contrast ratio should be approximately equal. Neither pole should dominate.

Camouflage

Making your ad not look like an ad. Borrowing the credibility, authority, and attention-holding power of formats the reader already trusts — journalism, personal letters, official reports, celebrity endorsements.

The best camouflage is invisible. When a reader doesn't immediately perceive they are reading an advertisement, they engage with it on different terms — with the openness they bring to a magazine article, a friend's recommendation, or a piece of news. That openness means they are far more likely to be persuaded by what they read.

Three Camouflage Techniques

Format Borrowing

Adopting the visual and structural format of a trusted medium. Schwartz describes how one ad was structured to look like a Wall Street Journal front page — complete with the column format, the editorial tone, the apparent objectivity of news reporting. Readers who would have skimmed past a conventional ad stopped and read every word, because the format told them this was journalism, not advertising.

Phraseology Borrowing

Using the specific vocabulary, sentence structures, and rhetorical patterns of a trusted category — scientific papers, government reports, doctor's advice. When copy sounds like a doctor is speaking, it carries the authority of medicine. When it sounds like a financial analyst is speaking, it carries the credibility of expertise.

Understatement and Deadly Sincerity

The Volkswagen Beetle campaign: "Think Small." "Lemon." These ads were so honest about the car's limitations that readers simply trusted everything else they said. Understatement and deliberate self-deprecation disarm the reader's advertising-detection mechanism completely. When an advertiser tells you something bad about their product, you believe everything good they tell you afterward.

Camouflage — Volkswagen "Lemon" The headline called their own car a lemon. The body copy explained that this particular car had been pulled from the line by inspector Kurt Kroner because the chrome strip on the glove compartment was slightly blemished. It didn't make it past their own quality control. So you won't get a lemon — because they already pulled it. A devastating display of honest advertising that made every claim in every future VW ad completely believable.
Camouflage — "Blow Some My Way" This cigarette ad wasn't camouflaged as journalism — it was camouflaged as a moment of authentic social interaction. A woman, in a real social situation, saying a real thing she might really say. The ad felt like an observed slice of life rather than a crafted advertisement. That feeling of authenticity was the camouflage.
Part Seven

The Final Touches

The master-level refinements that separate a powerful ad from an unforgettable one. These elements operate below the reader's conscious awareness — shaping experience, momentum, and conviction.

Verification

Every important claim should be verifiable — and the reader should feel that verification is possible even if they don't personally verify it. This is different from proof: verification is the reader's sense that the claim could be checked and would hold up.

Placement matters enormously. A specific detail placed immediately after a broad claim acts as instantaneous verification. The specificity of the detail retroactively validates the claim. "You'll lose up to 47 pounds — that's the result reported by the 2,300 people in our first clinical study" — the specific number verifies the claim before the reader can doubt it.

Reinforcement

The principle that 1 + 1 = 10 in advertising. When a claim is made in multiple contexts, through multiple channels, by multiple voices, its persuasive power is not doubled — it is multiplied many times over.

The Avis example: "We're #2 — we try harder." Every element of the Avis campaign reinforced this single truth: the cleaner cars, the friendlier staff, the faster service. Each reinforcement multiplied the persuasive power of every other. Reinforcement is the organizational principle of campaign thinking, not single-ad thinking.

Interweaving

The technique of weaving promise, logic, and emotion together simultaneously throughout the copy — not addressing them separately in sequence.

Amateur copy does: promise, then proof, then emotion. Professional copy interweaves all three continuously — so that the logical proof amplifies the emotional response, which in turn makes the promise feel more real. The three threads, braided together, produce a single overwhelming cord of conviction.

Sensitivity — Knowing the Shift Points

Every piece of copy has natural "shift points" — moments where the reader's emotional state, level of belief, or focus of attention changes. The master copywriter feels these shift points and changes the copy's tone, pace, or approach to match.

When the reader's belief is low, you give proof. When belief is high, you give emotion. When attention is high, you give complex argument. When attention wanes, you use a subhead, a change of pace, a vivid image. Sensitivity is reading the reader's state and responding to it in real time within the copy.

Momentum

Copy that moves. Every sentence should propel the reader into the next sentence. Schwartz discusses two specific tools for creating momentum:

Transition phrases that carry the reader forward: "And there's more..." "But that's not all..." "Here's the part that really surprised me..." These phrases function as engines, pulling the reader through the copy rather than letting them stop and exit.

Incomplete statements that cannot be left unresolved: a question asked at the end of a paragraph that is answered in the next. A dramatic pause marked by a dash. A sentence that begins at the bottom of a column and ends at the top of the next. Incompleteness is discomfort; the reader reads on to relieve it.

Mood — Color Words, Rhythm, Sincerity

The emotional atmosphere of the copy. Mood is the sum of all the tiny choices — word selection, sentence length, punctuation rhythm, paragraph structure — that create a unified emotional experience.

Color words are words that carry emotional charge beyond their denotative meaning: "gleaming," "trembling," "drenched," "blazing." They color the reader's experience of the copy without being overtly emotional themselves.

Rhythm is the pace of the copy — long flowing sentences for building desire, short punchy sentences for emphasis and decision. The rhythm change itself signals the reader to pay heightened attention.

Sincerity — Schwartz's ultimate refinement. The copy that persuades most powerfully is the copy that clearly believes in what it is saying. Sincerity cannot be faked over the course of a long piece of copy. The master copywriter must genuinely believe in the product's ability to fulfill its promise.

The Culmination

The Cardinal Rule

"What you are looking for in this product and this market is the one element that makes them unique — that separates them from all competition — that gives you the right to be heard above all the noise of the marketplace."

— Gene Schwartz, Breakthrough Advertising

Schwartz argues that advertising creativity operates on three distinct levels. Most copywriters operate at the first. The best operate at the third. Understanding the difference is the difference between adequate copy and breakthrough copy.

Level One

Word-Substitute Creativity

Taking a functional statement and replacing words with more vivid or interesting synonyms. "Good" becomes "magnificent." "Works fast" becomes "works in seconds." This is the lowest level of creativity — it improves the presentation of a given idea without changing the idea itself.

Most copywriters spend their entire careers here. It is necessary but not sufficient for breakthrough results.

Level Two

Formula Creativity

Applying a known successful structure to new content. "How to..." headlines, before/after stories, testimonial formats, numbered list formats. These are proven templates that reliably produce workable copy.

Formula creativity is essential to know and essential to transcend. The formulas exist because they work — but they are not enough when the market is saturated with the same formulas from every competitor.

Level Three

Analytical Creativity

Starting from the product and the market — analyzing them with complete rigor — and discovering the one true thing that makes this product uniquely valuable to this market at this moment. Then finding the most powerful way to express that truth.

Analytical creativity cannot be systematized entirely — it requires genuine thought about genuine specifics. But it can be trained through practice with the tools in this book.

"Creativity in this sense is not decoration. It is not finding a clever way to say something obvious. It is finding the one true, unique, powerful thing — and then finding the clearest possible way to say it."
The Final Test

Before running any ad, ask one question: "Is there anything in this ad that is uniquely true about this product for this market — something no competitor could honestly say?" If the answer is no, you have not yet found the breakthrough. Go back to the product. Go back to the market. The answer is there.

About the Author

Eugene M. Schwartz

Gene Schwartz (1927–1995) was one of the most successful and highest-paid copywriters in American history. He was also a serious art collector, a committed bibliophile who owned over 20,000 books, and a student of human psychology whose interest in advertising was ultimately an interest in the hidden forces that shape human desire and decision-making.

He wrote copy for Rodale Press, Boardroom Reports, and dozens of other direct-response publishers. His ads generated hundreds of millions of dollars over a career that spanned four decades. He charged $25,000 per day as a consultant — in the 1970s and 80s — and every client considered it a bargain.

About the Book

The History of Breakthrough Advertising

First published in 1966, Breakthrough Advertising was originally written for professional copywriters and direct-response marketers. It has never been conventionally published in the modern sense — it has been passed hand to hand, photocopied, reprinted privately, and has sold for hundreds of dollars as a used book for decades.

The book's reach extended far beyond professional copywriters. In his preface, Schwartz describes a phenomenon he observed repeatedly over his career: the people who made the most money from his ideas were not trained copywriters. They were entrepreneurs, publishers, inventors, and promoters — ordinary people who read the book and used its principles to build industries from scratch.

"Not one of them was a copywriter by profession. They were housewives, engineers, doctors, truck drivers — people who had a product or service to sell and who learned, from these pages, how to channel the mass desire that was already flowing through their market directly toward what they had to offer."
