🍌 Interactive Exercise

The Comedy Perception Test

One of the first things Steve Kaplan does in his seminars is present variations on a classic scenario. Each version produces a different reaction — and reveals something profound about how we perceive comedy. Click each scenario to explore what it reveals.

A

A man slips on a banana peel.

Basic Physical Comedy. We feel a brief shock, then relief that it's not us. Classic slapstick — the unexpected stumble of ordinary life.
B

A man in a top hat slips on a banana peel.

Comedy with Satisfaction. The hat signals pretension, wealth, or status. Now we don't just laugh — we enjoy it a little more. The universe restoring balance.
C

A man slips on a banana peel — after kicking a dog.

Pure Satisfaction Comedy. He deserved it. This is the oldest form: justice delivered by the universe. We actively want him to fall. We cheer.
D

A man slips on a banana peel — after losing his job.

The Edge of Comedy and Pathos. The laugh is uneasy. We're moving from comedy toward tragedy. His misfortune accumulates beyond what feels "safe" to laugh at.
E

A blind man slips on a banana peel.

Uncomfortable Territory. Most audiences stop laughing here. The lack of agency removes the comic contract. We feel complicit rather than amused.
F

A blind man's dog slips on a banana peel.

Pure Relief Comedy. The laugh returns — fully, joyfully. The dog is fine (dogs always bounce back in our imagination), no one is truly hurt, and the absurdity is complete.
G

A man slips on a banana peel — and dies.

Beyond Comedy: Tragedy. Death ends the comic contract entirely. Comedy requires the possibility of a happy ending. Death closes that door. This is why comedy must protect hope.

The lesson: Comedy is not about what happens — it's about who it happens to, why it happens, and whether we can believe a happy ending is still possible. These seven scenarios contain the entire grammar of comic perception.

🎭 The Foundation

Comedy Defined

Comedy is the art of telling the truth about what it's like to be human.
🎭

Drama

"Helps us dream about what we could be."

😄

Comedy

"Helps us live with who we are."

We are all, every one of us, trying to improve our lives. We want better jobs, better relationships, better health, more recognition. We wake up each morning with a plan — and the universe has a counter-plan. We are simultaneously aware of our own mortality and determined to act as if it doesn't apply to us.

That gap — between what we want to be and what we are, between our expectations and our reality, between our ambitions and our capabilities — is the territory of comedy. Comedy doesn't mock this gap. It celebrates it. It says: this is what it's like to be human. You are not alone.

Drama says: you could be a hero. Comedy says: you don't have to be. Drama presents the ideal. Comedy presents the real. And in that presentation of the real — fumbling, failing, trying again — lies the most profound form of human truth.

This is why comedy matters. This is why it's not "lesser" than drama, not mere entertainment, not a guilty pleasure. Comedy is, at its deepest, an act of compassion — a reminder that all of us are ordinary people struggling against insurmountable odds, and that this struggle is not shameful. It's the most human thing there is.

⚖️ The Formula

The Comic Equation

At the heart of Kaplan's theory is a single, elegant formulation that encompasses all of comedy. Every great comic character, every enduring funny story, fits within it.

Comedy is about an ordinary guy or gal struggling against insurmountable odds without many of the required skills and tools with which to win — yet never giving up hope.

01

Ordinary Guy or Gal

Not a hero, not a special person — a "moke." Someone like us. The moment a character becomes extraordinary, capable, or heroic, we've crossed into drama. Comedy requires someone we recognize as ourselves.

02

Insurmountable Odds

The problem must be genuinely, objectively bigger than the character can handle. Not merely difficult — impossible. If the odds are surmountable, the stakes drop and so does the comedy.

03

Without Required Skills

The comic character lacks the tools, knowledge, or competence needed to succeed. They're flawed, fumbling, and unsuited to the task. The fewer skills they have, the more comic they become.

04

Never Giving Up Hope

The essential ingredient. No matter how many times they fail, how absurd the situation, the comic character keeps trying. Hope is the engine. Without it, comedy collapses into tragedy.

🔧 The Core Curriculum

The 8 Hidden Tools

These are the tools that working comedy professionals use — often without knowing they're using them. Making them conscious transforms how you write, direct, and perform.

01
🏆

Winning

The permission at the heart of all comedy

Comedy gives your character — and by extension, you as a writer or performer — the permission to win. To do whatever the character thinks they need to do in order to win, no matter how stupid or crass or idiotic or undignified it makes them look.

This is a liberating concept. Most of us walk through life worried about how we appear to others. We self-censor, we modulate, we hold back. But a truly comic character has no such filter. They want something, and they're going to do whatever it takes to get it. Their single-minded, often-misguided pursuit of a goal IS the comedy.

Don't try to be funny. Try to win. The comedy will take care of itself.

The practical application: when you're writing a comic scene and it's not working, ask yourself — what does this character want, and are they doing everything in their power to get it? If they're holding back, being reasonable, or acting with dignity, you've found your problem. Let them try to win. Fully, completely, without reservation.

This is also the antidote to "mugging" — the comedian's trap of performing comedy rather than living it. When you're trying to be funny, you're not trying to win. And when you're not trying to win, nothing is funny.

Example — Annie Hall

Alvy Singer is arguing about Marshall McLuhan with a pompous man in a movie line. Unable to win the argument on its merits, he literally produces Marshall McLuhan from behind a movie poster to settle the debate. He wins — impossibly, absurdly — and it's hilarious.

Example — Liar Liar

Fletcher Reede cannot lie for one day. In the bathroom, he attempts to fight himself — and loses. He's trying to win against his own body. The commitment to winning, at any cost, to dignity or logic, IS the joke.

"The character's permission to win is the writer's permission to be funny."
02
🚶

The Non-Hero

The ordinary person who defines comic character

The Non-Hero is an ordinary person who lacks many of the required skills and tools to face their situation — yet never gives up. This is the fundamental unit of comic character. The more skills and competence a character has, the more dramatic they become. The fewer skills, the more comic.

Consider: Woody Allen in a room with twelve armed men. Now consider: Charles Bronson in the same room. Allen is comic; Bronson is dramatic. Not because of personality, but because of capability. The gap between what's required and what the character can deliver is the source of comedy.

The more he knows, the less comic it is. The less he knows, the more comic it becomes.

This is why comedy and drama are not just tonal differences — they're structural differences. The hero of a drama earns their victory through competence. The hero of a comedy stumbles into (or is denied) their victory through incompetence, luck, or sheer persistence.

George Costanza swallows a fly during a meeting. He doesn't know what to do. He tries to pretend it didn't happen. He fails. The comedy comes entirely from his lack of a single usable skill with which to handle the situation with grace.

Non-Hero + Impossible Situation = Comedy

Hero + Impossible Situation = Drama

The Non-Hero Spectrum

The range runs from slightly bumbling (Jim Halpert) to completely inept (Michael Scott) to cosmically outmatched (Inspector Clouseau). All are Non-Heroes. All are comic. Their skill level determines the flavor of comedy.

Key Distinction

The Non-Hero is not stupid. They are often quite intelligent. But they lack the specific skills required for their specific situation. This makes them relatable — we all face situations where we lack the right tools.

03
🔗

Metaphorical Relationships

The hidden relationship beneath the surface

Every great comedy has not just a surface relationship between characters, but a metaphorical relationship — an essential, hidden layer that lies beneath what we see on screen. This metaphorical relationship is often what makes us feel something beyond mere amusement.

Felix and Oscar in The Odd Couple appear to be roommates. But their metaphorical relationship is an old married couple — complete with all the resentment, dependency, tenderness, and power struggles that entails. Every scene plays on both levels simultaneously.

The surface relationship is what they do. The metaphorical relationship is what they mean — to each other, and to us.

Beyond the metaphorical relationship itself, Kaplan identifies two other crucial elements: World View — how each character uniquely and specifically perceives reality (Felix's world is chaos to be organized; Oscar's is order to be resisted) — and Frames & Chapters, the ability to see entire scenes through a single metaphorical lens that gives coherence and depth to comedic action.

When you understand the metaphorical relationship in a comedy, you understand why scenes work, why characters clash in the specific ways they do, and why their conflicts resonate beyond the immediate situation.

Classic Example

Felix & Oscar — Surface: two men sharing an apartment. Metaphorical: an old married couple trapped in a codependent relationship neither can leave.

Three Aspects

1. Metaphorical Relationship — the hidden bond

2. World View — each character's unique lens on reality

3. Frames & Chapters — the metaphorical structure of scenes

"Find the hidden relationship and you find the heart of the comedy."
04
➕

Positive Action

Every action taken in hope of a better outcome

Every action a comic character takes, they do in the hope and belief that it will work and make their lives better. This sounds obvious, but its implications are profound. Positive Action does not mean action performed in a positive way — it means action designed to bring a positive, selfish result for the character.

Kramer's pineapple bark pickup line is ridiculous, absurd, doomed to fail. But Kramer believes, genuinely and completely, that it will work. That belief — that sincere investment in the positive outcome — is what makes it funny. The moment a character knows their action won't work, the comedy deflates.

The character must always believe, in this moment, that what they're doing will get them what they want. Protect the possibility of a happy ending.

This connects directly to hope — the final element of the Comic Equation. As long as characters act in pursuit of a positive outcome, hope is alive. The moment a character gives up or acts nihilistically, the comedic contract breaks. This is also why irony is so often the enemy of comedy: ironic distance destroys the investment in positive action.

For writers, this means: every scene needs a character who sincerely wants something and sincerely believes their action will get it. The more misguided their method, while maintaining their sincere belief, the funnier it becomes.

Example — Seinfeld

Kramer's pineapple bark pickup line: he genuinely, completely believes this will work. The sincerity of his investment makes it comedy. If he were being ironic, it would be nothing.

Example — The Office

Michael Scott confronting a difficult HR situation with complete, sincere confidence that his approach will work — and that everyone will like him afterward. The gap between his certainty and his catastrophic method is the engine of his comedy.

Sincere Investment + Misguided Method = Comic Gold

❤️

Active Emotion

What you actually feel IS the correct emotional line

The emotion that naturally occurs to the performer in the course of genuinely trying to win IS the correct emotional line for the character. Not performed emotion. Not indicated emotion. The real thing that happens when you're actually invested in the outcome.

This is perhaps the most liberating tool for performers. You don't need to manufacture a "comic" emotion or decide in advance how the character "should" feel. You have everything you need to play comedy — you're human. If you actually try to win, actually try to get what the character wants, the emotions that arise from that genuine effort are exactly right.

You have everything you need to play comedy. You're human. Every human feeling in pursuit of a goal is the right feeling for that moment.

The enemy of Active Emotion is performance. When actors "mug" — making faces to signal that what's happening is funny — they've stopped trying to win and started trying to be funny. The result is the opposite of funny. Similarly, "acting funny" — adopting a comic vocal quality, a broad physical style, a wink to the audience — all of these replace genuine emotion with performed emotion.

For writers: watch your parentheticals. Writing (laughs hysterically) or (acts confused) in a script tells the actor what to perform rather than what to want. Instead, write what the character wants and why. Let the actor find the emotion through active pursuit.

For Performers

Don't decide how to feel. Decide what you want. Pursue it genuinely. The feelings that arise from that pursuit — frustration, hope, joy, confusion — are your performance. They're real, so they're funny.

For Writers

Avoid parentheticals that dictate emotion. Instead of (frustrated), write the situation that creates frustration. Trust the actor to feel what's real when they're truly trying to win.

Real pursuit → Real emotion → Real comedy.
Performed emotion → Nothing.
06
〰️

Straight Line / Wavy Line

The comedy isn't the funny thing — it's watching someone watch the funny thing

This is one of Kaplan's most brilliant and counterintuitive insights: comedy isn't watching someone do something funny — it's watching someone watch someone do something funny. The audience's laughter comes not from the absurd event itself, but from their identification with the character who must deal with the absurdity.

The Straight Line character is blind to the problem — often because they are the problem. They proceed with perfect confidence through the chaos they create. Abbott, in "Who's On First?", appears to have all the information; he doesn't understand why Costello is confused. He proceeds with absolute certainty.

The Straight Line creates the problem. The Wavy Line is us on screen, struggling to cope with the problem the Straight Line creates or embodies.

The Wavy Line character sees the problem, knows there's a problem, struggles with it, can't solve it, and can't escape it. Costello, in "Who's On First?", sees perfectly clearly that something is wrong — the names don't make sense, the information doesn't compute. He's our representative. We identify with his confusion, his frustration, his inability to get a straight answer in a world that has gone mad.

The Wavy Line is always our representative on screen. When we watch a comedy, we are the Wavy Line — the person who can see the absurdity but can't stop it. This is why the Wavy Line character is typically more sympathetic and more central to our engagement with the story.

Who's On First? — Abbott & Costello

Abbott (Straight Line): Has all the "facts." Doesn't understand why Costello is confused. Proceeds with maddening certainty.

Costello (Wavy Line): Sees the paradox clearly. Struggles with it. Cannot escape. Is US — our eyes in the scene.

Modern Examples

Straight Line: Michael Scott, David Brent, Frank Drebin — characters blind to the chaos they generate.

Wavy Line: Jim Halpert, Basil Fawlty's staff, anyone in a room with Kramer. They see. They suffer. We laugh with them.

Straight Line creates chaos.
Wavy Line witnesses and endures it.
Audience is the Wavy Line.

Archetypes — Commedia Tonight!

3,000 years of character types, alive on screen today

Comedy archetypes are not clichés — they're the deep structural patterns of human behavior that have made audiences laugh for three millennia. From the ancient Greek theater through the Commedia dell'Arte of Renaissance Italy to the sitcoms of today, the same fundamental types recur because they map onto fundamental human patterns.

The Commedia dell'Arte gave us the definitive vocabulary of comic character. The Zannis were the servant class: Arlecchino (Harlequin), the clever, acrobatic trickster; Scapino, the cowardly schemer; Brighella, the cunning rogue; Pierrot, the lovesick innocent. Each embodies a different flavor of the comic Non-Hero.

Character creates plot. Character creates action. Character creates movement. Know your archetypes and you know why your story moves the way it does.

Pantalone, the lecherous old miser always chasing young women; Marinetta, his battle-axe wife who knows exactly what he's up to; Il Dottore, the pompous academic who speaks in elaborate nonsense; Colombine, the perky, resourceful servant who actually runs the household; Il Capitano, the braggart soldier who is secretly a coward; and The Innamorati, the young lovers who are beautiful, passionate, and completely useless.

These archetypes are alive today: Pantalone is every TV boss who thinks he runs things while actually being managed by Colombine. Il Capitano is every puffed-up character who cowers when actually challenged. The Innamorati are every romantic subplot that's really just in the way of the real comedy.

The Commedia Family Tree

Zannis (servants) → Jerry Lewis, Jim Carrey, Rowan Atkinson

Pantalone → Mr. Burns, Ron Swanson, J. Jonah Jameson

Il Dottore → Frasier Crane, Jeff Winger, Professor Higgins

Il Capitano → Michael Scott, David Brent, Gaston

Colombine → Donna Meagle, Pepper Potts, the actual competent one

3,000 years of laughter.
The same characters.
New masks, same truth.

Comic Premise

The lie that tells the truth

The comic premise is what Kaplan calls "the lie that tells the truth." It's an impossible or highly improbable set of circumstances — something that could never happen in the real world — which then becomes the launching pad for absolutely honest, truthful exploration of human behavior.

The premise itself is the lie: What if you relived the same day over and over? (Groundhog Day). What if a 12-year-old woke up in an adult's body? (Big). What if your whole life was a TV show and you didn't know? (The Truman Show). These things don't happen. They're lies.

Tell ONE big lie. Then, from that premise, develop everything else with complete, rigorous honesty. The premise is the engine; the theme is the rudder.

The power of the premise comes from what it reveals. Groundhog Day isn't about time loops — it's about what it takes for a self-absorbed person to become capable of love. Big isn't about magic — it's about what adults have lost and what children still have. The impossible premise is just the lens through which we see a profound truth.

Rules of the comic premise: Tell only one big lie, then develop everything else honestly. All action must flow from the premise through character. Characters are brought on by Need (what the story requires) and Theme (what the story is about). The premise provides the engine of story; theme provides the direction and meaning.

Great Comic Premises

Groundhog Day: A man relives Feb. 2nd forever → truth about change, love, and becoming better

Big: A boy becomes an adult → truth about what we lose growing up

The Incredibles: Superheroes must hide → truth about extraordinary people in ordinary life

Liar Liar: A lawyer can't lie → truth about how we deceive those we love

❓ Common Questions

The Comedy FAQ

The questions writers, directors, and performers ask most often — answered through Kaplan's framework.

"Funny" is a moment — a joke, a pratfall, a surprising line. It produces a laugh and then it's over. "Comic" is a sustained quality of character and situation that creates a world where laughter is continuously possible. A great comic character isn't funny because they tell jokes — they're funny because of who they are and how they see the world.

You can write a scene full of funny moments that doesn't cohere into comedy. And you can write a scene with no individual jokes that is deeply, continuously comic. The difference is character depth, metaphorical relationship, and truth — the architecture beneath the laugh.

Kaplan identifies four requirements for a joke that works in a script or performance context: It must further the action — if you can remove the joke without affecting the scene, it doesn't belong. It must define character — the joke must be specific to this character's world view; the same line in another character's mouth would fall flat. It must express a unique point of view — it has to say something that could only be said from this character's particular perspective. And it must be compressed — comedy requires economy; every unnecessary word is a leak in the balloon.

A joke that meets all four requirements isn't just funny — it's doing four jobs at once. That's the mark of great comedy writing: every laugh earns its place by advancing the story, deepening character, and expressing truth.

Trying to be funny. This sounds paradoxical, but it's the most important thing to understand. When a writer is trying to write something funny, they're writing toward a result — a laugh — rather than writing toward a character and a truth. The result is jokes that feel forced, characters who exist only to deliver setups, and scenes that have no life beyond their punchlines.

The alternative: write toward what the character wants. Write the truth of who this person is and what they're willing to do to get it. Trust that if you've built a real character in an impossible situation pursuing their goal with complete sincerity, the comedy will emerge organically. Trying to be funny is the enemy of funny.

In practice, every comedy scene benefits from identifying which character is the Straight Line and which is the Wavy Line. The Straight Line is the one who doesn't know there's a problem — who proceeds with total confidence through whatever madness surrounds them. The Wavy Line knows something is wrong and cannot escape or fix it.

The Wavy Line does most of the emotional heavy lifting. They're the audience's representative — we see through them, we feel through them, we suffer through them. This is why Wavy Line characters are often more sympathetic and more central to our engagement. When writing or directing, ask: who is seeing the problem right now? Are they able to do anything about it? If yes, the comedy dissipates. If they're trapped and aware, you have comedy.

Important: characters can switch functions. In one scene, Character A is the Wavy Line dealing with Character B's oblivious Straight Line behavior. In the next scene, their roles might reverse. This dynamic switching is a source of great comic texture.

The gap between expectation and reality is one of the fundamental mechanisms of comedy. We set up an expectation — through character, situation, or genre convention — and then reality fails to meet it. The size and nature of that gap determines the type and intensity of the comedy.

A small gap produces gentle comedy: we expected the party to go smoothly, and it's slightly awkward. A large gap produces farce: we expected the dignified dinner party, and it has completely collapsed into chaos. The gap must be real — genuine expectation met with genuine failure — not telegraphed or performed.

The key is that the expectation must be established honestly. If we never believe the expectation, the gap is invisible and unfunny. Comedy requires us to genuinely think, for a moment, that things might work out — before they spectacularly don't.

Yes — though with a nuance that Kaplan is careful to articulate. What can be taught is not "how to be funny" in some absolute, generic sense. What can be taught is how to look deeply at comedy — to understand its structure, its mechanisms, its character requirements, its relationship to truth.

Comedy, like drama, like music, like painting, has a craft that can be studied and improved. A student of comedy who understands the Non-Hero, the Comic Equation, the tools of Positive Action and Active Emotion and the Straight/Wavy Line dynamic, will be a better comedy writer, director, or performer than they were before — regardless of their natural talent level.

Natural talent still matters, of course. But the history of comedy is full of people who started out unfunny and became masterful through rigorous study, practice, and the willingness to fail, analyze, and try again. This book is proof that the tools of comedy can be articulated — and if they can be articulated, they can be learned.

The difference is deeper than tone or genre. Drama and comedy operate on different structural principles. Drama gives us characters who grow into their potential — who earn their transformation through struggle and emerge genuinely changed and capable. The dramatic hero is on an arc toward competence, toward truth, toward becoming who they were always meant to be.

Comedy gives us characters who struggle to remain themselves in a world that's trying to change them — or who try desperately to change and fail to do so completely. The comic character often ends roughly where they began, slightly wiser but fundamentally the same. The arc of comedy is not transformation — it's survival, adaptation, endurance.

This is also why the same story can be either comedy or drama depending on how it's told: the same man-faces-impossible-odds story becomes comedy when he's incompetent and survives through luck and persistence, and drama when he grows into the competence required to overcome.

Kaplan identifies several secrets specific to sitcom writing that build on the foundational tools: 1. Status — every comic relationship involves a status dynamic, and the best sitcom writing constantly plays with who has status and who doesn't. 2. The B-Story should mirror the A-Story thematically — the best sitcom episodes have two storylines that illuminate the same truth from different angles. 3. Sitcom characters are defined by what they want from every other character — these specific wants create the specific comedy of each relationship. 4. Every act should end with the situation worse than it began — comedy escalates by accumulating problems, not by solving them. 5. The tag is a gift — the brief scene after the main story resolves can say the thing the episode couldn't quite say during the main action. 6. Characters don't change — they learn the lesson of the episode and then forget it by next week, because comedy requires consistent character over transformation. 7. The world of the show is a pressure cooker — sitcom worlds work because the characters can't leave. The relationships are inescapable, which means the comedy is inescapable too.

Steve Kaplan is one of the world's foremost authorities on comedy — not as a performer or stand-up, but as a teacher, theorist, and the man who has spent decades figuring out exactly why things are funny and how to help writers, directors, and performers make them funnier.

He is the co-founder and former artistic director of Manhattan Punch Line, New York City's premier comedy theater, where he worked with and developed some of the most significant comedy talent of the past three decades. The theater was a crucible for comedy in the truest sense: a place where the craft was taken seriously, where funny was understood as something you could get better at.

Among the writers, directors, and performers who have come through Kaplan's workshops and the theater he built are names that have shaped American comedy: David Crane and Marta Kauffman, creators of Friends; Nathan Lane, one of the most accomplished comic actors of his generation; Oliver Platt; and many others whose work has defined comedy on stage and screen.

Kaplan's workshops and seminars have been taken by thousands of writers, directors, actors, and storytellers around the world. He consults for studios, networks, and production companies. His approach is always the same: rigorous, practical, deeply grounded in the history and theory of comedy, and immediately applicable to the work.

The Hidden Tools of Comedy is the culmination of this work — the distillation of decades of teaching, observing, and refining what it means to be funny on purpose. It is both a theory of comedy and a practical toolkit, written with the clarity and wit you'd expect from someone who has spent a career studying exactly why things are funny and how to make them funnier.
