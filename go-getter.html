Legacy

Two classics. One philosophy.

Two slim books, written decades apart, share the same electrifying conviction: the rarest quality on earth is a person who receives a task and simply does it—without excuses, without endless questions, without waiting to be rescued. Together they have influenced CEOs, generals, entrepreneurs, and ordinary people in search of an extraordinary life.

1921
The Parable
The Go-Getter
Peter B. Kyne  ·  San Francisco

Bill Peck, a young WWI veteran with one arm and a limp, refuses to accept refusal. He talks his way into a job at Ricks Logging & Lumbering, out-sells every territory he touches—then faces a cryptic Sunday errand: find a blue vase and deliver it by 7:45 that evening. He doesn't know it's a test. He doesn't know it's nearly impossible. He only knows one thing: it shall be done.

1899
The Essay
A Message to Garcia
Elbert Hubbard  ·  East Aurora, NY

During the Spanish-American War, Lieutenant Rowan was handed a letter and told to deliver it to General Garcia—somewhere in the Cuban jungle. He asked no questions. He took the letter, sealed it in oilskin over his heart, landed by night off an open boat, disappeared into the jungle, and three weeks later emerged on the other side having delivered it. Hubbard's meditation on that act became a global phenomenon.

Dramatis Personae

Meet the cast

Kyne populated his slim parable with vivid, archetypal figures. Each one teaches us something about leadership, loyalty, skepticism, and what it truly means to earn trust.

🎖️
The Go-Getter
Bill Peck

WWI veteran. One arm (lost in France), a slightly shortened leg, early TB—and the most compelling smile Cappy Ricks had ever seen. Before the war, a lumber salesman. After: a man who had been trained, by a great brigadier, to say three words to every obstacle.

"All I ask is fighting room and I'll hack my way into Mr. Skinner's heart."
🪄
The Wise Tester
Cappy Ricks

President emeritus of the Ricks interests—logging, lumber, and Pacific shipping. Nominally retired. Actually the most active mind in the room. He sees men before they see themselves. He invented the Degree of the Blue Vase because he knows that experience alone is not enough: courage is what counts.

"The go-getters of this world are under thirty years of age."
📋
The Skeptic
Mr. Skinner

General manager of the Ricks Logging & Lumbering Company. Efficient, cold, practical—and convinced that sentiment has no place in business. He turned Peck away without a second look. He is not a villain; he is simply the ceiling that every Peck eventually rises through.

"There is no sentiment in business."
⚔️
The Soul Behind the Motto
The Brigadier

Never named. Never fully described. And yet the most powerful presence in the book. He gave his brigade a motto. He once failed to buy a blue vase—and threw a cobblestone through a shop window to get it. Cappy let him go to his destiny. Twenty years later, his motto walked through Cappy's door wearing Bill Peck's face.

"It shall be done." — Brigade motto
🌊
The Doer
Lt. Andrew Rowan

Real historical figure. In 1898, handed a letter for General Garcia somewhere in the Cuban jungle, he did not ask "Where is he?" He strapped the letter over his heart and vanished into a hostile country. Three weeks later the letter was delivered. Hubbard spent 1,800 words celebrating that silence.

He took the letter. He did not ask "Where is he at?"
🚢
The Son-in-Law
Captain Matt Peasley

President of the Blue Star Navigation Company. Good-natured, loyal—but he too, like Skinner, tells Peck the company is full. He later refuses to lend Peck the $2,000 for the vase on a Sunday night. Not unkind. Not a go-getter.

"Go home and to bed and forget it."
The Narrative

The story, chapter by chapter

The Go-Getter moves briskly through four chapters. Each phase reveals a new layer of Peck's character— and Cappy's shrewd, patient genius for finding the people who carry the world forward.

Chapter I
Cappy's problem—and an unexpected visitor

Cappy Ricks—president emeritus of a Pacific Coast lumber and shipping empire—is exasperated. His Shanghai office manager has absconded with the bank account. His two deputies, Skinner and Matt Peasley, can't agree on a replacement. Cappy decides the next man must be tested before he's trusted. He's mid-thought when a one-armed veteran walks through the door without an appointment and says: "I've called for my job."

"I've called for my job." — not "I'd like a job," but "my job."
Chapter II
The sell—and the first obstacle

Peck has already had his business cards engraved. He began planning this job a week ago. He sells Cappy on himself in minutes, then gracefully wins over Skinner's reluctant compliance. Cappy assigns him Andrews' territory—plus the stock nobody else can move: "skunk spruce," the worst lumber in the yard. Peck doesn't flinch. "Can you drive nails in it?" "Yes." "Then I can sell it."

He had business cards engraved before he had the job. That is preparation.
Chapter III & IV
Two months on the road—and the test is set

From Salt Lake City to Texas, Peck sends orders flying in. He opens new accounts nobody else could crack. He sells five carloads of skunk spruce. Skinner grudgingly raises his salary. Cappy watches, satisfied— then tells Skinner: "I think I shall give him the thirty-third degree. I'm going to have him deliver a blue vase." Skinner smiles his swordfish smile and starts arranging the obstacles.

Only 2 of 15 candidates have ever delivered the vase. The degree is real.
Chapter V
The Blue Vase — and the verdict

On Sunday afternoon, Skinner—supposedly ill in bed—routes Cappy's call to Peck. Cappy asks for one small errand: find a blue vase on Sutter Street and deliver it to his train by 7:45 pm. Every detail is wrong. The street is wrong. The shop is closed. The owner's name is spelled three ways. The vase costs $2,000 cash. Nobody will help. The train leaves without him. At 2 a.m. Bill Peck flags the train from the middle of the tracks and delivers the vase.

"What you carried in was a $10,000-a-year job as our Shanghai manager."
The Ultimate Test

The Degree of the Blue Vase

Cappy Ricks has tested fifteen candidates with this challenge. Only two ever delivered the vase. The test is designed to be impossible: deliberately wrong information, deliberately stacked obstacles, deliberately timed to fail.


The point is not the vase. The point is whether a person—when alone, exhausted, out of resources, and out of time—will find another way or simply stop. Click each obstacle below to see how Peck refused to let it end his mission.

0 of 12 obstacles overcome

"Bill, old boy, it was cruel—damnably cruel, but I had a big job for you and I had to find out a lot of things about you before I entrusted you with that job."

At 2 a.m., standing in the doorway of Cappy's train compartment in his nightshirt, Cappy stared at Bill Peck as if he were a ghost. The vase cost fifteen cents. It came from a five-and-dime store. What Bill Peck actually carried in was a $10,000-a-year job as Oriental manager. Cappy has given this test to fifteen men. Bill is only the third to deliver the vase.

The Philosophy

Six principles of the go-getter

The Go-Getter is not a self-help checklist. It's a lived philosophy—demonstrated through action, not words. These six principles emerge from Peck's conduct and Cappy's commentary. Hover each card to go deeper.

↕ Hover to flip

01
🎯
Unconditional Commitment
The motto is not a feeling—it's a command.
01 — Unconditional Commitment
"It shall be done" is not an expression of confidence. It's a vow made before the outcome is known. The brigadier didn't say "I'll try." He said it shall be done—and then found a way to make it true. Peck inherited this: when Cappy gives the order, the how is Peck's problem to solve, not Cappy's.
"In the army the commanding general's request is always tantamount to an order."
02
🚪
Refuse the First No
Skinner said no. Matt said no. Cappy said yes.
02 — Refuse the First No
Peck was turned down by Skinner. Then by Matt Peasley. Most people stop there— and those people stay where they are. Peck went over their heads to the founder, not out of disrespect but out of certainty. He knew he had value. He refused to let someone else's fear or indifference be the final word on his future.
"He sized you up just like that, Skinner. He declined to permit you to block him."
03
🔧
Resourcefulness Under Pressure
When the obvious path closes, find another.
03 — Resourcefulness Under Pressure
No cash? Use the ring. No time? Call the pilot. Train left? Flag it down. Peck never waited for permission to think creatively. Every obstacle he hit was an engineering problem, not a stop sign. This is not luck—it's the product of a mind trained to always ask: what else can I try?
"You told me what to do, but you did not insult my intelligence by telling me how to do it."
04
🛡️
Loyalty to the Mission
Not to the boss—to what the boss sent you to do.
04 — Loyalty to the Mission
When Matt Peasley told Peck to go home and forget the vase, Peck refused—not to defy Matt, but because he felt he owed Cappy better than failure. His loyalty wasn't to rank or convenience. It was to completion. He would rather flag a train at 1 a.m. than live with not having tried hard enough.
"I wouldn't disappoint him for my right arm. He's been a dead game sport with me."
05
⚡
Initiative Without Being Asked
He had cards printed before he had the job.
05 — Initiative Without Being Asked
Peck planned his job at Ricks a week before he applied. He had engraved business cards ready. "By the Twelve Ragged Apostles! When did you first make up your mind to go to work for us?" "A week ago." You can't engrave cards in half an hour—Cappy knew. That level of pre-commitment signals something beyond ambition. It signals conviction.
"He was a go-getter. He carried his objective." — Cappy on Peck
06
🌱
Resilience Through Setbacks
His leg hurt. His stump hurt. He delivered anyway.
06 — Resilience Through Setbacks
Peck's tears in the train compartment are not weakness—they are the mark of someone who has given everything. He was exhausted, hungry (skipped both meals), in physical pain, and had been manipulated. And yet he still delivered. The brigadier's lesson: "Private Peck will do his utmost to recover—and as a starter he will smile."
"Sir, it shall be done." — Peck in the field hospital, told to smile.
Elbert Hubbard, 1899

A Message to Garcia

Hubbard's essay began as a dinner-table story and grew into one of the most widely distributed pieces of writing in history—over 40 million copies given to soldiers, workers, and students across the world. Its premise is simple and devastating:


Lieutenant Andrew Rowan was given a letter and told to deliver it to Garcia. He didn't ask where Garcia was. He didn't ask which route. He didn't ask what would happen if he failed. He took the letter and went. Hubbard spent the rest of the essay asking why that is so extraordinarily rare—and what it costs the world that it is.

The questions the average man would have asked Rowan:

❓"Who is Garcia?"
❓"Where is he at?"
❓"Which encyclopedia?"
❓"Was I hired for that?"
❓"What do you want to know for?"
❓"Is there any hurry?"
❓"Can't you look it up yourself?"
❓"What's the matter with Charlie doing it?"

The Hidden Thread

The circular legend

The most beautiful structural detail of The Go-Getter is a secret most readers miss on first reading. The blue vase test was invented by Cappy decades ago—and the brigadier who gave Peck his unbreakable motto was once tested by that very same vase. Trace the thread:

🏭
Decades earlier — Cappy invents the test

Cappy Ricks creates the Degree of the Blue Vase as the ultimate screen for high-trust positions. Only those who deliver it—despite impossible obstacles—are fit for the $10,000 Shanghai post. Over the years, fifteen candidates attempt it.

The Test Is Born
⚔️
The brigadier takes the test — and smashes a window

A young candidate—later to become Bill Peck's general—is given the blue vase errand. When he cannot buy the vase legally, he throws a cobblestone through the shop window, grabs the vase, and runs a mile and a half before police catch him. It costs Cappy money to square the case. He lets the man go to his destiny.

The Brigadier Delivers
🎖️
The war — "It shall be done" is born in the trenches

The brigadier leads his brigade with a single, irreducible motto. He visits Peck in the field hospital after Peck is wounded, tells him he isn't half dead, and orders him to smile. The motto becomes the spine of Peck's entire character.

A Soul Is Transmitted
🔵
Bill Peck delivers the blue vase — for the brigadier

Twenty-odd years after the brigadier threw his cobblestone, Bill Peck walks into Cappy's office carrying the brigadier's motto. He cannot know that he is completing a circle. When he says "It shall be done," he is channeling a man Cappy knew well—a man who once stood where Peck now stands. The soul of a great leader travels through time through the people it shaped.

The Circle Closes
✨
The revelation — Cappy recognizes the ghost

When Peck names his idol—the brigadier—awe fills Cappy's voice. He whispers: "By the Twelve Ragged Apostles!" He understands everything. He was the one who set the brigadier free. And the brigadier's spirit walked back in through his own door. "His soul goes marching on," Cappy says softly.

Modern Application

The philosophy, applied today

The world of 1921 is gone. Cappy's logging empire, Pacific steamships, and Shanghai trading posts are historical artifacts. But the human behaviors that make someone irreplaceable—or replaceable— haven't changed one molecule.

💼
Early Career
Volunteer for the task no one else will touch

Peck asked for the skunk spruce—the stock nobody could move. He turned it into a demonstration of capability. The thankless, unglamorous project in your organization is your blue vase. Take it.

🔑
Interviews & Job Seeking
Prepare as if you already have the job

Peck had engraved business cards before his interview. Research the company so deeply you can speak to specific problems. Bring solutions. Show that you've already started— the question is whether they're smart enough to let you finish.

🗺️
Project Execution
Own the outcome, not the steps

Cappy didn't tell Peck how to find the vase. He told him to deliver it. When you own an outcome, you stop asking for permission at each obstacle. You find the next route—ring, pilot, torch, train—until the objective is carried.

👥
Leadership
The esprit de corps filters from the top

"An organization is what its commanding officer is—neither better nor worse." If you lead a team, your attitude toward obstacles is the attitude your team will adopt. Your standards are the ceiling they aim for. Your words become their inner voice.

⏰
Time & Priorities
"Count that day lost…"

Peck returned from lunch at 1 p.m. to earn half a day's pay on day one. He skipped lunch and dinner during the vase hunt without complaint. Productivity isn't about grinding—it's about the unwillingness to let a day pass without forward movement.

🏆
Long-Term Career
Be the person Cappy is always looking for

Every organization has its Cappy Ricks—the person who truly controls opportunity and is searching desperately for someone to trust with it. They are not impressed by credentials. They are watching for the one person who shows up, says "it shall be done," and then actually does it.
