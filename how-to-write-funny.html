Chapter 1

Introduction: The Craft of Comedy

Writing humor that's genuinely funny — gut-busting, milk-out-of-the-nose funny — is one of the most difficult literary crafts. Here's why, and what you can do about it.

When you get pulled into a good piece of humor writing, something magical happens. A string of words ignites a spark that sends outlandish images and funny ideas racing into your brain, culminating in an explosion of laughter. Most of us have no idea what's making us laugh. We can't articulate it.

There aren't many great humor writers in the world. You could count on one hand the ones who've made you laugh out loud. It's a one-in-a-million writer who can elicit sustained, hardy laughs from total strangers with nothing more than words on a page.

⚡ Why Horror is Easy

Vampires, ghosts, blood, screaming — pre-vetted spooky clichés sit waiting on the horror tool shelf. You could write a decent one over a weekend.

💧 Why Sadness is Easy

Write about a pet that dies, or a couple splitting up. Separate characters who are meant to be together. Writing a tearjerker is like pushing a button.

But writing something funny? That's something else entirely. All the go-to ideas have been done to death: banana-peel slipping, mothers-in-law, three-things-walk-into-a-bar… How do you create laughs from thin air?

✦ The Good News

Professional comedy writers have a very good idea of what's going to work. It's their job, and they do it consistently — using special tools locked in the subconscious mind. This guide describes those tools and explains how to use them. Comedy writing is a craft. It can be learned. It doesn't have to take decades.

Humor-Writing Tip #1

Concept is King

The core concept you're writing about has to be funny — it's the most important part. The greatest writing in the world will not save a bad concept. But a great concept written barely adequately will be met with great success. Express your concept in a single line. That's your "joke." If the concept makes readers laugh, they'll read on. If it doesn't, they won't.

"To paraphrase E. B. White, comedy is like a frog — once you start dissecting it, it's not funny. And dissecting comedy is exactly what we're going to do."
Chapter 2

Your Brain's Comedy Engine

Access both hemispheres of your brain to eliminate writer's block and tap an endless reserve of comedy ideas.

Productive humor writers churn out enormous amounts of material. They write in journals and notebooks. They're an unstoppable force of nature. With practice and positive reinforcement, they get very good at it. You can too — if you solve one of two core problems.

🤡

The Clown

The right brain — creative, subjective, outside the box, nonjudgmental. The Clown writes down every idea, no matter how stupid it seems. Overconfident writers are too Clown-heavy: they love being silly, but their material falls apart on the page without editorial structure.

✏️

The Editor

The left brain — logical, objective, organized, analytical. Most writers are too much Editor. They question every choice and judge every idea before it can shine. The Editor must evaluate raw material and craft it into superb humor — but not kill everything at birth.

Just like we need both sides of our brain to function in the world, humor writers need to balance both sides of their comedy brain. The back-and-forth reliance on these two very different halves is like doing mental calisthenics. The more you do it, the more adroit you'll become.

Humor-Writing Tip #2

Quantity Is the Key to Quality

No writer cranks out pure gold in a single draft. The best comedy writers write dozens — sometimes hundreds — of jokes, then carefully select only the best. They make it seem easy because they never show you all the bad jokes they throw away. Write more to have better material to choose from.

Developing Your Inner Clown

📓 Exercise 1: Morning Pages

Write for a half hour every day, without stopping, no matter what you're writing — and no matter how bad you think it is. Don't stop to think, don't correct typos. Just keep writing until the timer beeps. This primes the pump and clears the gunk from your brain so the good stuff can flow.

🗒️ Exercise 2: The Notebook

Always keep a little notebook with you. Write down every idea you have, especially ones you find amusing. These little observations are the crude oil of the humor-writing business. Failing to save them is like letting oil from your well spill out all over the sand.

Once a week, flip through your notebook and save any ideas you still find amusing — this is your Editor at work. If you reject every idea, you still have some rebalancing to do.

Chapter 2 Action Steps

  1. Buy a little notebook and keep it with you at all times. Write down any thought or observation that strikes you as amusing — or even merely interesting.
  2. Every day for at least two weeks, do the Morning Pages: write without stopping for half an hour. Love and accept all of it, even the garbage.
  3. At the end of each week, go through your Morning Pages and notebook. Save anything remotely interesting to a "short list" — your bank of potential comedy ideas.
Chapter 3

The Humor Writer's Biggest Problem

It's not "how do I come up with ideas?" It's something more fundamental — and more controllable than most writers realize.

Let's say you churn out brilliant, funny prose. Then what? Who's going to read it? Your mom, probably. How are you going to find an audience?

⚠️ The Scale Problem

There's more writing published online every day than has ever been published in the history of human civilization — and that number doubles the next day. Most people don't like to read. They'd rather do anything else. In our culture of easy entertainment and instant gratification, reading is akin to homework.

Humor-Writing Tip #3

Make It Accessible

Accessible writing is easy to find and easy to read. It's written clearly and simply to appeal to as many readers as possible, covering subject matter that's understandable to as many readers as possible. Because the audience for the written word is so small, the more accessible your writing is, the bigger its potential audience. Don't make readers work even harder to find or understand your writing.

Humor-Writing Tip #4

Make It Short

Anything worth saying is worth saying briefly. Sometimes cutting a word or two can make a line twice as funny, or turn an unfunny line into a funny one. By trimming your writing, you force yourself to get at the heart of what you have to say, and say it in a way readers can more easily digest. (Mark Twain apologized for a letter's lengthiness by explaining he didn't have time to make it short.)

The Seven Humor Media

One secret trick: steal attention-getting traits from other humor media to make your prose more appealing. Just as a plant needs creative ways to attract bees, your writing needs creative ways to attract readers.

📝

Prose

Undivided attention, deep intimacy, longest-lasting. Weakness: small audience, perceived as intellectual work.

📺

TV / Web Video

World's most accessible and popular. People are addicted. Expensive to produce, but words-only web video can be very affordable.

🎬

Movies

Immersive storytelling format. Requires dramatic structure beyond comedic structure for anything longer than a sketch.

🎙️

Audio / Podcast

Next most intimate after prose. Uniquely accessible — people listen while commuting or exercising.

🎭

Stage

Live performance energy is unmatched. The human element is a critical tool almost all humor media exploit.

🖼️

Visual

Still images with no words. Internet memes master this — catchy images with brief text generating massive awareness.

🎨

Street Art

Audiences entertained when not expecting it are the best audiences. Surprise and delight are virtually impossible to achieve in any other medium.

💡 Key Insight: Steal Strengths from Other Media

Add a surprising image to your writing. Pair it with video. Write through a character voice. Include humor in unexpected places. The Onion began as a free street newspaper — the street art medium helped introduce it to new readers who were absolutely delighted to find humor in an unexpected place.

  2. Make a list of ideas for overcoming prose's weaknesses in your own writing — what strengths of other media could you employ to help readers find your work?
Chapter 4

How to Get Laughs

There are different kinds of laughs. Understanding them — and what causes the rarest, most healing kind — is the foundation of comedy writing.

📊 The Anatomy of Laughter

90%+ — Nervous laughter. People greasing social wheels, breaking tension. Empty, non-healing.

~9% — Pure, hardy laughter in response to real-life situations (the funny thing the dog did, the non-injurious fall). Good for you, but can't be engineered — only happens by accident.

~1% — Laughter in response to consciously crafted comedy. Most of this comes from performance, social contracts, and laugh tracks.

.00001% — Laughter generated by nothing but the written word, with no performer, no sound, no laugh track. This is what we're after.

The Essential Ingredient: Surprise

Humor can exist without relatability, without timing, without tragedy, without taboo-breaking. Humor cannot exist without surprise. Even when something predictable happens that makes us laugh, that event must unfold in a surprising way to be funny.

Humor-Writing Tip #5

Avoid Clichés

If you've heard a joke or funny phrase before, don't repeat it. Also avoid topics that have been joked about endlessly. There's no surprise with clichés because we've seen the joke before. Only fresh ideas, unique perspectives, and original thoughts can surprise — and only fresh, unique, and original writing can get laughs.

🚫 Comedy Clichés to Avoid

airline food quality "That's what she said" men hogging the remote "I just threw up a little" the "surfer dude" character mothers-in-law "You should see the other guy" white people can't jump game show buzzer sounds "I could tell you, but..."

The Five Categories of Written Humor

Written humor exists in a spectrum from basic to sophisticated. Only one category provides maximum surprise potential and the largest possible audience.

← Basic / Narrow AppealSophisticated / Broad Appeal →
Level 1
Kid Stuff
Basic incongruity, simple discordance. Pratfalls, talking animals, simple puns. Funny to young children and those with that sensibility. Seeds from which more sophisticated humor grows.
Level 2
Conversational
Prose written with the author's personality laid bare — "on the nose," no disguise. The writer's inherent character, but not a comedic construct. Dave Barry, early David Sedaris.
Level 3
Snark
Conversational + smart-alecky sarcasm. The Know-It-All in transparent form. Ubiquitous on blogs and social media. Relies heavily on borrowed clichés. Effective when done well and without clichés.
Level 4
Formulaic Humor
Multiple layers of humor. The 11 Funny Filters used skillfully. Written by professional comedy writers. Often assumes other character voices and formats. National Lampoon, Garrison Keillor, Spy Magazine.
★ Level 5
Satire
Formulaic Humor plus one secret ingredient: Subtext. The laughs are more satisfying, more memorable. Can appeal to all readers — from the least to most sophisticated. The Onion, Kurt Vonnegut, Swift, Twain.
Humor-Writing Tip #6

Proofread

Get your spelling, grammar, and syntax right. Make a solid effort to ensure no typos slip through. Your work might be just as funny as the competition's, but if you have misspellings and bad punctuation and they don't, guess who gets the job.

  2. Write 10 one-sentence jokes using no clichés.
Chapter 5

Subtext: The Magic Ingredient

The secret ingredient that separates Satire from all other humor categories — and makes laughs more satisfying and memorable.

In any sophisticated writing, there's more there than just the literal words. Something else is being communicated clearly to any reasonably intelligent reader — but it's never stated outright. That's Subtext.

When you read a joke, you add two and two in your mind and expose the Subtext the writer has hidden. It's this exposure that surprises you and causes you to laugh. If you "got" a joke, you successfully uncovered its Subtext. If you didn't get it, you couldn't decipher it.

🚇 The Subway Metaphor

Subtext is like a subway. It travels underground. If it traveled at street level, pedestrians, cars, and everything else would get plowed through as it crashed through everything in sight. This havoc is exactly what happens to your humor writing when you state your Subtext overtly. Never state your Subtext.

Humor-Writing Tip #7

Have Something to Say

To be a writer, the first thing you need is something to say. Without that, why are you writing? By regularly sifting through your notebook and Morning Pages, you'll eventually find something worthy of being communicated to others that you can get passionate about.

Humor-Writing Tip #8

Know What Joke You're Telling

Always be in control of the message by using a clear, intentional Subtext. Never leave a joke open to interpretation. When you ask, "What is my joke really saying?" — you need a specific answer. You need to know what your Subtext is and how you're revealing it. If you don't know what you're saying, you have almost no chance that readers will laugh at it.

Subtext in Action

Subtext can usually be stated in a simple sentence: subject, verb, object. It must be irreducible — no hidden meaning in itself. It must be a flat, on-the-nose opinion.

"Don't forget: Tomorrow is Bring Your Daughter To Tears Day."
Subtext
The idea of Take Your Daughter to Work Day is sad — in our society, girls (not boys) need this extra exposure to the workplace.
— Dan Guterman
"Couldn't get tickets to the Daytona 500, so I just stayed home and set a box of Tide on fire."
Subtext
NASCAR is a pathetic, too-heavily-sponsored sport, and it's a twisted fact that many people watch it for the accidents.
— Dan Guterman
"Fun Fact: If you stretched out your intestines they would reach all the way to the cabin in the woods you were murdered in."
Subtext
It's a little unsettling when people point out how long our intestines are.
— Dan Guterman
Humor-Writing Tip #9

Comfort the Afflicted, Afflict the Comfortable

If the target of your Satire is the downtrodden — the homeless, or victims of a tragedy — it will come across as mean-spirited, and audiences won't find it funny. You can make a joke about anything, but the target must deserve ridicule. The best targets are "the comfortable": the status quo, entrenched power, or any authority, no matter how low-level.

📌 Powerful Subtext Examples from Literature

  • War of the Worlds (H.G. Wells): "Humanity's arrogance as the dominant species may be unwarranted."
  • 1984 (Orwell): "Totalitarianism destroys souls."
  • Animal Farm (Orwell): "Power corrupts."
  • Huckleberry Finn (Twain): "Racism is wrong."
  • The Onion (daily): "Our government is incompetent." / "Americans don't care about the world, they care about snacks."
"Comedy equals tragedy plus time." — Steve Allen
"Humor is about one thing: life's nightmare hellscape of unrelenting horror." — Todd Hanson, head writer, The Onion

  2. Write 10 different Subtext ideas that aren't necessarily funny — just opinions or value judgments like "We're destroying our planet" or "People without a legitimate handicap shouldn't ride mobility scooters in grocery stores."
Chapter 6

The 11 Funny Filters

Every joke ever written uses one or more of these 11 fundamental building blocks. Master them and you can generate laughs reliably, on command.

These are called "Funny Filters" because the humor writer starts with Subtext (which is not usually humorous) and filters it through one or more of these tools so it comes out the other end as a joke. That discovery of the hidden message is what causes the laugh.

Humor-Writing Tip #10

Heighten Contrast

Humor often involves contrasting two things — the straight character and the jokester, two ends of an ironic situation, a fake world and the real world. A common flaw in unsuccessful humor is that the contrast isn't heightened enough. By simply pushing contrast to its greatest possible extreme, a lot of comedy writing becomes instantly funnier.

1
🔄
Irony
What It Is

Extreme Opposites — when the literal meaning is the opposite of the intended meaning.

How to Use It

Write the polar opposite of your Subtext with absolute conviction. Play it straight. Think "9 and 3" on a steering wheel — maximum contrast leverage.

"Alcoholic Father Disappointed In Pothead Son" — The Onion
"It's Not A Crack House, It's A Crack Home" — The Onion Note: Irony ≠ Sarcasm. Sarcasm is "Irony light" — delivered with attitude that exposes Subtext too knowingly. Irony pretends to adopt the opposite opinion with total conviction.
2
🎭
Character
What It Is

A comedic character with 1–3 clear traits acts (or is acted upon) in accordance with those traits.

How to Use It

Create a simply drawn, two-dimensional character (not a realistic dramatic character). Establish their 1–3 traits, then have them act on those traits. This is the engine behind virtually all performance-based comedy.

"I don't get no respect. The other night I said to the bartender, 'surprise me.' He showed me a naked picture of my wife." — Rodney Dangerfield Key Archetypes: The Dummy, Slob, Snob, Know-It-All, Everyman, Manchild, Klutz, Lothario, Nerd, Robot/Straight Man, Naif, Bumbling Authority, Trickster.
3
💥
Shock
What It Is

Anything shocking — sex, violence, swearing, or a gross-out. Anything inappropriate to mention in mixed company.

How to Use It

In extreme moderation, and always with Subtext. Think of it as a dash of cayenne pepper. The more shocking the humor, the more astute the Subtext needs to be. As a main course, its audience is limited to 14-year-old boys.

"You can't fight City Hall, but you can goddamn sure blow it up." — George Carlin Subtext: We all feel powerless and enraged by government incompetence — even possible to understand the rage of a terrorist, because we've all felt it.
4
📏
Hyperbole
What It Is

Exaggeration so absurd it goes beyond the bounds of science or reason — not just "a lot" but to the point where physical laws are violated.

How to Use It

Exaggerate your Subtext — in any direction (big, small, positive, negative). Start with an opinion, then push it past the breaking point of reality. This is the filter that feels most like "real joke-writing."

"The last time I gave a urine sample it had an olive in it." — Rodney Dangerfield (Subtext: I drink too much)
"NRA Calls For Teachers to Keep Loaded Gun Pointed At Class for Entire School Day" — The Onion Hyperbole allows you to reach ridiculous extremes by exaggerating so far beyond reality that you're suddenly in an impossible reality.
5
🔤
Wordplay
What It Is

Any use of words beyond their standard meanings — double/triple meanings, made-up words, puns, word switches, repetition, funny sounds.

How to Use It

Only with great Subtext. A wordplay joke without Subtext ("How did the frog die? He croaked.") is just a groaner. The difference between a groaner and a sophisticated Wordplay joke: the wordplay must serve astute Subtext.

"I put a dollar in a change machine. Nothing changed." — George Carlin (Subtext: Societal change is difficult)
"Fritolaysia Cuts Off Chiplomatic Relations With Snakistan" — The Onion (Mike DiCenzo) Best used in titles and headlines. Avoid: alliteration, acronyms, Tom Swifties as primary devices.
6
👁️
Reference
What It Is

A relatable observation — readers have seen, heard, or experienced the thing being referenced. Makes them say "oh, yeah, I've experienced that!"

How to Use It

Observe and write down things you notice that others might recognize. Use the "did you ever notice…?" mindset to find unique observations about day-to-day life, culture, or the human condition — in a way no one has articulated before.

Grade D: In-jokes (too narrow). Grade C: Call-backs. Grade B: Current events. Grade B+: Emerging speech patterns/clichés. Grade A: Universal observations about life pointed out for the first time.

"Men don't care what's on TV. They only care what else is on TV." — Jerry Seinfeld Jerry Seinfeld is the master of Reference. He begins bits with References, then escalates using Hyperbole, Wordplay, Character, or other Filters.
7
🤪
Madcap
What It Is

The literary equivalent of physical humor — crazy made-up words, descriptions of slapstick, pratfalls, funny physical action, absurdist references, and seemingly random non sequiturs.

How to Use It

Make sure it serves (and even symbolizes) your Subtext. Works as the main thrust of a joke but also as a very effective garnish. Without Subtext, you have silliness for silliness's sake. Monty Python did this best.

"Secretary Of Agriculture Attends Diplomatic Meeting With Foreign Cabbage" — The Onion
Subtext: Americans couldn't care less what the Secretary of Agriculture does, so he may as well be meeting with a head of cabbage. Warning: Avoid classic Madcap clichés — banana peels, rubber chickens, Groucho glasses. These stopped being funny decades ago.
8
🎭
Parody
What It Is

Making fun of another entertainment or information product by mimicking its format, voice, or anything that calls it to mind for your reader.

How to Use It

Use verisimilitude — make your Parody look or sound as much like the real thing as possible. Mirror the form or format, not the content. Play it straight; don't wink at the reader. Readers will often laugh before reading a word, just from recognizing the format.

The Onion (parodies news websites), The Diary of a Wimpy Kid (parodies a diary), Ian Frazier's "Coyote v. Acme" (parodies legal briefs + Road Runner cartoons). The closer the resemblance to the original, the better. Verisimilitude is everything in Parody. Going halfway is not good enough.
9
🔗
Analogy
What It Is

Comparing two very different things and finding their similarities. The more dissimilar the two things, the better. You must find many points of comparison.

How to Use It

Keep one side of the Analogy "behind the curtain" — only allude to it — while the other is laid bare. Never break character to reveal the secret half. Each time you make a connection the reader recognizes, that's a joke beat.

George Orwell's Animal Farm: animals on a farm (revealed) vs. the Russian Revolution (secret). Power corrupts (Subtext).
"Al Gore Places Infant Son In Rocket To Escape Dying Planet" — The Onion (Analogy to Superman's origin) The hidden half of the Analogy must be discovered by the reader. If you state it, the joke collapses.
10
🔭
Misplaced Focus
What It Is

A pretend lack of awareness of an obvious fact, brought to light by focusing intently on the wrong thing — something just to the side of your Subtext, far less important.

How to Use It

Think of something related to your Subtext but far less important. Draw readers' attention to it, indirectly causing them to think of your Subtext. Best with Subtext that will elicit strong opinions. It stirs righteous indignation.

Jonathan Swift's "A Modest Proposal": focuses on eating Ireland's poor children (wrong thing) instead of solving child poverty (real issue).
"Secondhand Smoke Linked to Secondhand Coolness" — The Onion
"Mother of dead teen has no one to update her to the new iOS." — student joke It's like saying "Don't think of a Pink Elephant" — the glaring omission makes the real issue impossible to ignore.
11
🪞
Metahumor
What It Is

Making fun of other humor or the idea of humor itself — using humor as the subject or target of the joke. Stepping back and commenting on the act of comedy.

How to Use It

Type A: Mocks the concept of humor itself (most sophisticated, most esoteric). Type B: Intellectually deconstructs comedy with no emotion. Type C: Openly derides well-respected comedy media/clichés. Type D: Mocks unsophisticated humor (easy laughs, less daring).

"Viagra Giving Hope To Thousands Of Struggling Stand-Up Comedians" — The Onion
Steve Martin referring to his own act as "hilarious comedy jokes."
Marlin in Finding Nemo who is a terrible joke-teller — and is a clown fish. Antihumor is a subset: the absence of humor becomes funny when the audience is expecting something funny and gets nothing instead.
Humor-Writing Tip #11

Use Verisimilitude

If you're copying something else in form (a pattern of speech, a character, another work of entertainment), make it as similar to the thing you're imitating as possible. If it's a senator character, he should talk like real senators talk. If it's a parody of an advertisement, it should sound as much like a real advertisement as possible. Be consistent, and never "break voice."

Humor-Writing Tip #13

Put the Funny Part Last

A joke usually works best when the reveal — the funny word, the funny detail, the funniest part — comes last. Often, when assessing a joke, you'll notice the funny part is at the beginning or middle. Try moving it as close to the end as possible. That often makes it funnier.

Humor-Writing Tip #14

There's Only One Rule in Comedy

If they laugh, it's funny. When you feel overwhelmed by all the dos and don'ts, step away from the keyboard and forget about it for a while. Do something fun. When you come back to writing, remember: readers just want to have fun. They want to laugh. If you can make them laugh — no matter how you're doing it — you're doing it right.

Chapter 6 Action Step

  1. Every day for 10 days, write 10 funny one-liners using each of the 11 Funny Filters — one filter per day. Day 1: 10 ironic jokes. Day 2: 10 character jokes. And so on. Put yourself in Clown mode. They may not be good. That's okay. This is practice.
Chapter 7

Using the Funny Filters

Three practical methods for generating funny material — whether you start with an idea, a half-formed joke, or nothing at all.

These methods may seem clunky and mechanical at first. Comedy writing is a slog. But with practice they'll start to feel like instinct, or at least a well-honed skill.

The core formula is the same across all three methods: Subtext filtered through one or more Funny Filters. That's what creates a joke.

Humor-Writing Tip #15

One Impossible Thing at a Time

When you create a joke, you create a comedy universe in which one key idea is askew from reality. You can only have one "crazy" idea or one impossible thing per joke. If you give readers two, they'll end up confused. If we're going to laugh easily and freely, one comedic concept at a time is all we can handle.

Method 1

Filtering

Start with Subtext, then filter it through one or more Funny Filters to make a joke. The most mechanical, reverse-engineered method.

1
Find an opinion or observation in your notebook or Morning Pages that could serve as Subtext. It must have a clear subject-verb-object structure.
2
Put on your Clown hat. Try filtering the Subtext through each of the 11 Funny Filters one by one — see what funny things happen to it.
3
When multiple Funny Filters start piling up on an idea, something good is usually happening — continue in that vein.
4
Let ideas sit for a few days. Come back and assess them with your Editor hat — then use Method 2 (Finessing) to polish them.
Method 2

Finessing

Start with a joke — or something resembling one — that came to you in a flash of inspiration, then refine it using your awareness of Subtext and the Funny Filters.

1
Put on your Editor hat. Find a joke or funny idea from your notebook or short list.
2
Identify the Subtext — what is this joke really saying? Know exactly what joke you're telling and what you're communicating.
3
Heighten contrast as much as possible. Is the funny part last? Could you add a Funny Filter without adding words?
4
Check all 18 Humor-Writing Tips. Cut every unnecessary word. Proof for spelling and grammar.
Method 3

Divining

No ideas and no notes — use the Funny Filters by themselves to drum up something funny out of nothing.

1
Go into pure Clown mode. Pick a Funny Filter — any one.
2
Brainstorm using that filter's inherent logic: for Irony, think of opposites; for Character, think of famous people reduced to 1–3 traits; for Hyperbole, think of opinions and push them past reason; etc.
3
Cycle through all 11 Funny Filters, generating at least one attempt with each. Quantity over quality here.
4
Let the new jokes sit for a few days, then assess them with a more objective Editor eye.
Humor-Writing Tip #12

Don't Fall In Love

You may think you have the greatest idea in the world, but don't fall in love with it. You may have to change it or cut it based on feedback. Amateur humor writers often have one idea they're very proud of, making them immune to constructive feedback. Professional humor writers come up with tons of ideas and are prepared to discard any that don't connect. As Sir Arthur Quiller-Couch said: "Murder your darlings."

Humor-Writing Tip #16

Keep It Simple

Don't overload a joke with too much exposition, information, or a confusing rubric. Just use a Funny Filter to give a twist to your one subtextual message, and state it in the clearest, simplest way possible. Anything more and you'll only confuse readers.

💡 Your Sense of Humor

Every writer has comedy preferences. Some Funny Filters will come naturally to you; others will be a struggle. Feel free to play to your strengths — that's what gives you your unique voice. But practice using all 11 Filters, especially the difficult ones. When you can write any kind of joke using any Filter, you increase your potential readership. Each reader has their own preferences, so the more Filters you use, the more readers you'll reach.

Chapter 7 Action Steps

  1. Write 10 one-liners or headlines using the Filtering method — work from observations and non-joke notes in your notebook or Morning Pages.
  2. Go through your jokes from the Chapter 6 Action Step and run them through the Finessing process to make sure they're as good as they can be.
  3. Write 10 one-liners using the Divining method, cycling through each of the 11 Funny Filters.
Chapter 8

Process Overview

Master this simple system to become a prolific, reliable humor writer — one who generates great material consistently, not just occasionally.

All good humor writing starts with an opinion. A writer needs something to say. This opinion doesn't have to be funny — it just has to be something you have a feeling about. The humor writer generates this idea and is compelled to express it to readers.

📝

Daily: Write Without Stopping

Do the Morning Pages exercise every day. Write in your notebook when an idea strikes. These two activities develop your Clown brain and prime the pump of creativity until it overflows.

🔍

Weekly: Curate Your Short List

1–2 times a week, go through your notebook and Morning Pages. If you see anything remotely interesting or potentially funny, move it to a short list — your bank of potential comedy ideas. Only a single-digit percentile of what you write will make it here, and that's fine.

Daily: Write Humor (3 Methods)

Spend a few minutes every day actually trying to write humor using Filtering, Finessing, or Divining. Run Subtext through Funny Filters. Polish existing jokes. Brainstorm from scratch.

✅

Ongoing: Apply the 18 Tips

Check jokes against all the Humor-Writing Tips: Is it a cliché? Does it comfort the afflicted instead of afflicting the comfortable? Is the contrast heightened? Is the funny part last? Can any words be cut?

📣

Eventually: Get It in Front of Readers

Sift through your best jokes and decide what media each is best suited for — tweet, one-liner, article, story, screenplay? Get your work in front of readers and find out what they think. Incorporate feedback into your next piece.

Humor-Writing Tip #18

Ruffle Some Feathers

In Satire, if someone's not offended, you're probably not doing it right. The best humor has a little sense of danger or mischievousness, as if the writer is getting away with saying something that shouldn't be allowed — often at the expense of a humorless authority or comfortable target. When a deserving target gets angry at something you wrote, consider that a badge of honor and a sign of success.

The Master Formula

✦ The Core Equation

Astute Subtext + One or More Funny Filters = A Joke That Works

The Funny Filters are the processing mechanism. They filter your raw, bitter opinion so readers can't taste it directly — they experience only the sweet, refined humor. Like feeding an aspirin to a child: you don't give a plain, dry, bitter pill. You crunch it up and put it in a spoonful of jelly. That's what the Funny Filters do for Subtext.

All 18 Humor-Writing Tips at a Glance

#1 Concept is King

The funny idea is the most important part. A great concept even barely executed will succeed. Great writing cannot save a bad concept.

#2 Quantity = Quality

Write dozens of jokes to find the gold. Never show the bad ones you throw away. Write more to have better material to choose from.

#3 Make It Accessible

Easy to find, easy to read. Appeal to as many readers as possible. Don't make readers work hard.

#4 Make It Short

Brevity is the soul of wit. Cutting a word or two can double the laughs. Use simpler, shorter words.

#5 Avoid Clichés

If you've heard it before, don't repeat it. Avoid overused topics, phrases, and joke patterns.

#6 Proofread

Spelling, grammar, syntax. No typos. Your competition won't have them.

#7 Have Something to Say

To be a writer, you need something to communicate. Without that, why are you writing?

#8 Know Your Joke

Be in control of your Subtext. Know exactly what joke you're telling and ensure your reader knows too.

#9 Afflict the Comfortable

Never target the downtrodden. The best targets are the status quo, entrenched power, any authority.

#10 Heighten Contrast

Push contrast to its greatest possible extreme. Many jokes fail simply because contrast isn't heightened enough.

#11 Verisimilitude

If imitating something, make it as similar to the real thing as possible. Never break voice.

#12 Don't Fall in Love

Be prepared to discard any idea. Generate tons of them. Murder your darlings.

#13 Funny Part Last

The reveal, the funny word, the punchline — move it as close to the end as possible.

#14 One Rule: They Laugh

If they laugh, it's funny. Don't get overwhelmed. Readers just want to have fun.

#15 One Impossible Thing

Only one "crazy" idea per joke. Two impossible things confuses readers and kills the laugh.

#16 Keep It Simple

Don't overload with exposition. One Funny Filter + one Subtext = one joke. State it as clearly as possible.

#17 (Quantity again)

No writer produces only winners. The trick is to keep spewing junk and recognize the things that look like gold.

#18 Ruffle Feathers

If no one's offended, you're probably not doing it right. Danger and mischievousness are signs of success.
