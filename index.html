Introduction

The Vision Thing

Since 1976, Steve Jobs spoke his mind — to the delight of his advocates and the dismay of his detractors — in every venue imaginable: press releases, public appearances, keynote addresses, and interviews with print and electronic media across four decades.

“I'm always keeping my eyes open for the next big opportunity.”

Jobs occupied a unique position in modern business history. His peers and critics alike recognized the scope of his impact:

CEO of the Decade — Fortune World's Best-Performing CEO — Harvard Business Review Person of the Decade — Wall Street Journal Person of the Year — Financial Times

On August 24, 2011, Jobs announced his resignation as Apple's CEO, asking the board to execute the company's succession plan and name Timothy Cook as his successor. On October 5, 2011 — one day after Apple's new CEO held his first media event — the Apple board announced that Steve Jobs, age 56, had passed away.

Apple's board released a statement: “Steve's brilliance, passion and energy were the source of countless innovations that enrich and improve all of our lives. The world is immeasurably better because of Steve.”

What follows is a curated collection of his most powerful and enduring words — searchable by topic, organized by theme, and presented alongside the milestones of an extraordinary life.

Key Themes

Eight Pillars of His Philosophy

Jobs’s worldview crystallized around a handful of ideas he returned to again and again throughout his career.

◆

Design

For Jobs, design was never about aesthetics alone — it was about function, soul, and the invisible architecture of experience. He believed great design required deep understanding, passionate commitment, and the willingness to peel back every layer until the elegant solution emerged.

Design is the fundamental soul of a man-made creation that ends up expressing itself in successive outer layers of the product or service.

💡

Innovation

Jobs understood that true innovation was not about R&D budgets or committee decisions — it was about vision, taste, and the courage to say no to 1,000 good ideas in service of one great one. He distinguished leaders from followers by their willingness to invent, not imitate.

Innovation has nothing to do with how many R&D dollars you have. It’s about the people you have, how you’re led, and how much you get it.

👥

People

Jobs believed recruiting was “the heart and soul” of what Apple did. He sought people who wanted to make a dent in the universe, who thrived under the pressure of impossible standards, and who understood that the work was bigger than any individual on the team.

All we are is our ideas, or people. That’s what keeps us going to work in the morning.

🧠

Philosophy

Deeply influenced by Zen Buddhism, Jobs approached life as a beginner — open, unencumbered by expert assumptions. He treasured experience over intellectual abstraction, prioritized what mattered over what impressed, and measured success not by wealth but by meaning.

The heaviness of being successful was replaced by the lightness of being a beginner again, less sure about everything.

💻

Technology

Jobs saw technology not as an end but as a tool — a bicycle for the mind. He insisted that Apple own the entire stack: hardware, software, and the operating system, so that the user experience could be seamless, accountable, and sublime.

What a computer is to me is the most remarkable tool that we have ever come up with. It’s the equivalent of a bicycle for our minds.

🌟

Creativity

Jobs insisted that creativity was an act of synthesis — connecting dots across disciplines, stealing great ideas shamelessly, and refusing to separate art from engineering. He celebrated musicians who coded, scientists who painted, and the blurry intersection where both lived.

Good artists copy, great artists steal. We have always been shameless about stealing great ideas.

🎯

Leadership

His model of leadership was demanding, intentional, and occasionally uncomfortable. He believed a leader’s job was not to be easy on people but to make them better — to create the conditions in which excellence was not only expected but inevitable.

My job is not to be easy on people. My job is to make them better.

📈

Simplicity

Simplicity was not a style for Jobs — it was a discipline. He pruned Apple’s product line from dozens of models to four. He killed features, programs, and good ideas relentlessly. He believed that the hardest work was not adding complexity but removing it.

That’s been one of my mantras — focus and simplicity. Simple can be harder than complex.

End of an Era

Steve Jobs’s resignation letter as CEO of Apple — a document that closed one of the most remarkable chapters in business history.

AUGUST 24, 2011

To the Apple Board of Directors and the Apple Community

I have always said if there ever came a day when I could no longer meet my duties and expectations as Apple’s CEO, I would be the first to let you know. Unfortunately, that day has come.

I hereby resign as CEO of Apple. I would like to serve, if the Board sees fit, as Chairman of the Board, director and Apple employee.

As far as my successor goes, I strongly recommend that we execute our succession plan and name Tim Cook as CEO of Apple.

I believe Apple’s brightest and most innovative days are ahead of it. And I look forward to watching and contributing to its success in a new role.

I have made some of the best friends of my life at Apple, and I thank you for all the many years of being able to work alongside you.

— Steve
