The Second City — A Timeline
DECEMBER 1959

The Second City opens in a converted Chinese laundry in Chicago's Old Town neighborhood, founded by Paul Sills, Bernie Sahlins, and Howard Alk.

1961

"Family Reunion" — one of the first scenes to blend personal truth with political comedy, breaking boundaries of American entertainment.

1976

SCTV launches in Canada — a landmark in co-created sketch comedy launching careers of John Candy, Martin Short, Eugene Levy, and Catherine O'Hara.

1989

Second City Comedy Marketing Group (later Second City Works) founded — bringing improv tools to corporate clients worldwide.

1992

Andrew Alexander launches the Second City Outreach Program following the LA riots, committing the company to radical diversity on and off stage.

1996

Tina Fey joins the first gender-equal cast at Second City Chicago in Citizen Gates — a watershed moment for the institution.

2009

Rod Blagojevich Superstar — a live embodiment of Yes, And in action: written in two weeks, produced on a frozen budget, sold out entirely on announcement.

2014

Yes, And published — bringing five decades of improv wisdom to business, leadership, and life beyond the stage.

Introduction

We Have Cool Jobs.
Sometimes We Hate Them.

Everyone does. But the authors of this book have come to understand a central truth: they tend to hate their jobs a lot less than other people — and they've identified exactly why.

"We are at our happiest and most successful when we are working as improvisers. When we are fiercely following the elements of improvisation, we generate ideas both quickly and efficiently; we're more engaged with our coworkers; our interactions with clients become richer or more long-standing; we weather rough storms with more aplomb, and we don't work burdened by a fear of failure."

— Kelly Leonard & Tom Yorton

Practicing improvisation is like yoga for your professional development — a solid, strengthening workout that improves emotional intelligence, teaches you to pivot out of tight spaces, and helps you become both a more compelling leader and a more collaborative follower.

Google's hiring criteria — "the ability to process on the fly," a willingness "to relinquish power," ease with "creating space for others to contribute," and individuals who can "learn how to learn from failure" — are the qualities of an improviser. And they can be learned.

"This work will change your life."

— Anne Libera, former Artistic Director, Second City Training Center
The Core Framework

Seven Elements of Improvisation

These seven elements are at the heart of The Second City's success and have contributed to the careers of some of the most brilliant performers of our time — and to the culture of organizations as diverse as Nissan, Motorola, Google, Nike, and McDonald's.

ELEMENT · 01 ✦

Yes, And

The bedrock of all improvisation. Creative breakthroughs occur in environments where ideas are not just fully explored, but heightened and stretched to levels that might seem absurd at first. Work cultures that embrace Yes, And are more inventive, quicker to solve problems, and have more engaged employees.

ELEMENT · 02 ◎

Ensemble

Stars are born out of ensembles. The preeminent focus of everything — in sales teams, executive boards, retail staff. The ensemble's main enemies: the need to be right, the need to steal focus, and the need to appear in control when the evidence says otherwise.

ELEMENT · 03 ⟳

Co-Creation

Dialogues push stories further than monologues. Half a century of work shows the sum of co-creation is greater than its parts. In our increasingly connected world, co-creation is fast becoming a fact of life — yet rarely taught where it is needed most.

ELEMENT · 04 ◈

Authenticity

"Dare to offend." The business or organization that takes itself too seriously and doesn't know how to question its own beliefs is at a strong competitive disadvantage. Comedy and irreverence are lubricants that encourage people to reconsider long-standing beliefs.

ELEMENT · 05 ✕

Failure

Counterintuitively, failure is something improvisers actually practice. The biggest threat to creativity is fear, especially the fear of failure. By deflating the negative power of failure, you erode fear and allow creativity to flourish.

ELEMENT · 06 ⇄

Follow the Follower

A principle that gives the group flexibility to allow any member to assume leadership as long as their expertise is needed, then shuffle the hierarchy when needs change. Leadership is about understanding status, not maintaining it.

ELEMENT · 07 ◉

Listening

Deep listening is essential to improvisation — and critical in selling, evaluating, brainstorming, and leading. The difference between listening to understand and listening while waiting to respond is the difference between art and noise.

Element 01 — Deep Dive

Yes, And:
How to Make Something Out of Nothing

No matter what title is on your business card, professional success requires the ability to create something out of nothing. Marketing campaigns. New products. Trusted partnerships. Better processes. Thoughtful responses to customer complaints. The people who do this extraordinarily well approach every challenge with Yes, And.

NO / YES, BUT (Kills momentum)

"Wow, I've never seen so many stars in the sky."

"I can't see a single star… it's broad daylight."

→ Scene stops dead. Actor scrambles. Audience loses interest.

YES, AND (Builds momentum)

"Wow, I've never seen so many stars in the sky."

"I know. Things look so different up here on the moon."

→ Scene explodes with possibility. Partners both have something to build on.

The Anti-Patterns of Yes, And

Enemies of Yes, And often say "yes" with their mouths while saying "no" with their actions.

Getting to No by Numbers

If team consensus isn't what you wanted, just add more and more voices until you get the no you were looking for.

Yes, And I'm Overstepping

Using "Yes, And" as a refuge to stick your nose into other departments when you were never invited.

Yes, And… Go Hang Yourself

Watching someone head toward disaster and simply Yes, And-ing their idea so you can't be blamed.

No, But Yes, And It's Mine

Saying no emphatically to an idea, then adopting it and collecting the credit for brilliant thinking.

They'll Forget About It

Saying Yes, And to shut people up, knowing they'll eventually forget they brought up the idea.

The Power of Neither

Maintaining power by never taking a stand. No one can say you made a bad decision when you make none at all.

"Yes, And showed me that you're not going to love every idea, but it helps to love it for at least a little while."

— Senior participant, Farmers Insurance leadership workshop

Yes, And in the Wild: Technology Examples

Wikipedia — Content is user-generated, meaning anyone can create, edit, or add to what is already written. It operates on an Explore and Heighten model: something is offered, then built upon, deepened, sometimes redirected. Yes, And in its purest digital form.

Linux — Developers use and modify existing code and make it do cool new things. Now the leading OS for servers and mainframes, with more than 90% of the world's fastest supercomputers running some version. The most powerful, benevolent, and useful expression of Yes, And the world has ever seen.

How to Build an Ensemble

The word team implies competition. Ensemble carries no such baggage — it is a thing unto itself, an entity that is only its true self when its members are performing as one.

TEAM

  • Implies competition, an external foe
  • Starters and bench players — clear hierarchy
  • Some members matter, some simply do not
  • Defined by role in the contest

ENSEMBLE ✦

  • All parts considered only in relation to the whole
  • First, second, third chair — all on stage
  • Every member plays in almost every movement
  • Defined by how members perform as one

The Three Practices That Build Ensemble

Be in the Moment

Don't dwell on previous mistakes or even previous successes. There is time for reflection and time for planning — both distinct from the time for creation and innovation.

Give and Take

Research shows: people who practice give and take enjoy greater professional success. Engineers who gave far more than they received had the highest productivity and fewest mistakes.

Surrender the Need to Be Right

The need to be right is one of the biggest barriers to creative ensembles. Create environments where people agree to try Yes, And for even just one day — watch what happens.

"Basketball, unlike football with its prescribed routes, is an improvisational game, similar to jazz. If someone drops a note, someone else must step into the vacuum and drive the beat that sustains the team."

— Phil Jackson, on the Chicago Bulls dynasty

Diversity Is Not Optional

Like-minded individuals from like-minded backgrounds produce like-minded results. The world demands a diversity of approaches to real-world problems. When Second City committed to diverse casting in 1992, it didn't just become more ethical — it became more powerful. Keegan-Michael Key's role as a Pakistani cab driver navigating post-9/11 America could only have existed in a truly diverse ensemble.

Three Keys to In-House Co-Creation

🔍

Find the Idea

Not your idea. When ensembles co-create, ideas belong to the group. The most robust creative fount comes from those willing to cede control.

🤝

Cede Control

Gets more difficult the higher you rise. But the best creatives close their mouths and listen to all ensemble members, letting ideas grow and morph.

🛡

Eradicate Fear

Every blocker to good co-creation stems from fear. Fear doesn't inspire elegant thinking — it inspires knee-jerk, path-of-least-resistance ideas.

The Four Faces of Fear in Co-Creation

❓

Asking Questions

Questions push creative responsibility entirely onto your partner. "Why are we here?" gives nothing to build on.

💢

Aggression

When we're afraid, we lash out. Aggression masquerading as boldness kills every scene and every meeting.

📢

Yelling

Making yourself louder than anyone else, or bulldozing through interactions. Management-by-volume destroys memory, morale, and collaboration.

💬

Waffling

"I don't know." "Maybe." Waffling is a delaying tactic — too afraid to leap into the unknown. In business, not deciding is almost worse than deciding wrong.

"The human brain is hardwired to fill in the gaps of incomplete ideas — and people respond positively when invited to contribute to something not yet fully formed. The closure principle: a door opener."

— Neuroscience & improvisation
Element 04 — Deep Dive

Change Is Hard:
Comedy and Improvisation Make It Easier

Before you can get people to change their behavior, they have to change their attitudes. Before they can change their attitudes, they have to understand there's a situation that needs to be viewed differently. Comedy defuses the tension. Improvisation builds the skills.

The Three Essential Elements of Comedy

1

Recognition

The mere mention of a shared truth — an adjoining suburb, a familiar type — elicits response. Comedy's first hook.

2

Pain

You can't have comedy without something just a little uncomfortable — tension, surprise, incongruity, cognitive dissonance.

3

Distance

Truth and pain alone make tragedy. Distance — in time, space, or shared trust — creates the safe context for laughter.

The Respect / Revere Distinction

RESPECT (Enables Change)

Like the rules of the road — constantly keeping us from crashing into each other. Respect demands that you consider the other. It allows for dialogue between individuals who may think differently, creating a path to understanding and change.

REVERE (Blocks Change)

Turns respect into something so perfect it can't be touched. Makes idols of individuals and institutions. Speaks at us rather than with us. Reverence is the enemy of change.

The line between respect and reverence shows up everywhere: Worker and Boss, Constituent and Representative, Student and Teacher. When respect becomes reverence, the person of lower status loses the ability to call out what needs calling out — and change becomes impossible.

"Innovators question facts. Albert Einstein respected the fundamental laws of physics but didn't revere Newton so much that he couldn't prove new relationships. Steve Jobs respected what technology could do but wasn't so reverential to the status quo that he couldn't improve it for everyone."

Element 05 — Deep Dive

Using Failure

We fail a lot. Sometimes spectacularly. We do it on our stages, in our conference rooms, on the road, when we hire and fire people, and in our training centers. We failed fifty-five years ago, we fail today, and we'll certainly fail tomorrow. That's what happens when you understand that staying relevant means constantly challenging and reinventing yourself.

Famous Failures from The Second City

The Royster Oyster

A hamburger with an oyster on top. Dozens of customers were hospitalized with food poisoning. Menu item removed.

Second City Cleveland

Opened without advertising, group sales, or promotion. Received a cease-and-desist from Riverdance. Audiences booed the political satire. Closed after 19 months.

Invisible Rails

An evening of completely serious, non-comedic improvisation. Del Close walked out the moment he found out. Nobody laughed. Nobody came.

Second City Montreal

Announced three times over twenty years. Zero theatres ever opened in Montreal.

The Six Ways The Second City Chooses to Fail

FAIL IN PUBLIC

Where there's a chance of public failure, there's also a chance of public acclaim. Focus on that — and take the leap.

FAIL TOGETHER

"Always take care of your partner." Failure's sting is shared. Risk is distributed. The ensemble makes individual courage possible.

FAIL FAST

When a scene fails, acknowledge it with a quick joke and move on. Speed prevents failure from becoming an anchor.

FAIL FREE OF JUDGMENT

Producers need an invitation to enter rehearsals. No surprise "gotcha" visits. The creative process changes when supervisors arrive too soon.

FAIL WITH CONFIDENCE

Unshakable faith that approach, talent, and experience will carry you through. Ensemble members go into every challenge confident they'll succeed.

FAIL INCREMENTALLY

"Bring a brick, not a cathedral." No individual idea is critical because it's really only a bridge to better ideas. The only true failure is withholding contribution.

"Success is not final, failure is not fatal; it is the courage to continue that counts."

— Winston Churchill
Element 06 — Deep Dive

Follow the Follower

In the late 1950s, management consultant Peter Drucker introduced the concept of the knowledge worker and proposed flat organizational structures. Around the same time, Viola Spolin proposed "Follow the Follower" — a principle that gives any member of an ensemble the ability to assume leadership for as long as their expertise is needed, then shuffle the hierarchy when needs change.

They were both right. And The Second City had known it for years already.

"The leaders who work most effectively never say I. And that's not because they have trained themselves not to say I. They don't think I. They think we; they think team. They understand their job is to make the team function. They accept responsibility and don't sidestep it: We gets the credit. This is what creates trust."
— PETER DRUCKER, 1974

What Great Leadership Actually Looks Like

🏗

Build the Right Sandbox

Set parameters. Hire the right people. Then get out of the way. The creative process changes when supervisors get involved too soon.

👂

Talk to Your Audience

After years of declining readership, the Chicago Tribune started sending reporters to bars. More than 100 readers showed up the first night.

🔄

Know When to Lead, Follow, or Leave

Leadership today is about letting go of old stereotypes. If you have to demand respect, you never had it.

🎯

Read the Room — Constantly

Notice when someone sinks in their chair. Bring them back. Let everyone know they're being heard. Keep the bricks coming.

"A fundamental principle of improv is listening and accepting any initiation that's made on the stage. Similarly, I want my managers to listen and respond to their employees' perceptions, not ignore them. When they deny there's an issue and reflexively defend the status quo, it creates misery."

— Dick Costolo, CEO of Twitter & Second City alumnus

Women and Collaborative Leadership

A 2011 study of 7,280 leaders found that while men hold more top positions, women were rated more positively in 12 of 15 leadership effectiveness categories — including Collaboration, Inspiring Others, Developing Others, Championing Change, and Communicating Powerfully.

These are the qualities of the best improvisers. Moving from command-and-control to a collaborative model plays to strengths that improvisation specifically develops — in everyone, regardless of gender.

LISTENING TO RESPOND ✕

The default mode for most of the world. While the other person talks, you're already formulating your counterpoint. You hear the beginning; you miss the end. You disagree when you don't actually disagree. You build things nobody asked for.

LISTENING TO UNDERSTAND ✓

Enriches and broadens perspective. Turns off the judgment part of your brain. Keeps you in the moment, not looking backward or jumping ahead. Allows you to hear what someone is saying and what they are not saying.

Listening for Intent

Words are only one way we communicate. Paul Ekman identified seven universal micro expressions — anger, fear, sadness, disgust, contempt, surprise, happiness — that appear for only a fraction of a second and reveal the gap between what we say and how we truly feel.

Interestingly, Ekman was at the University of Chicago in the early 1950s at the same time as Mike Nichols and Elaine May — practicing Viola Spolin's improv games. The connection between deep listening and emotional intelligence runs back to the very roots of The Second City.

"If you listen, if you truly listen, you'll realize how easy it is to connect with people you thought you disagreed with. That's the freedom you get with improv."

— Betsy Myers, COO of Barack Obama's 2008 presidential campaign

Practical Application

The Second City Improv Exercises

These exercises can be brought into your team to practice the seven elements of improvisation. Click any exercise to see the setup.

01
Exposure
Focus: Overcoming self-consciousness

Divide your group into two lines, facing each other, about ten feet apart. Have them stand looking at each other. Once there is noticeable discomfort, have them complete a counting task (e.g., bricks on the wall). The fidgeting stops — everyone concentrates on the task. Point: Focus can rid us of fear and being in our heads.

02
One-Word Story
Focus: Yes, And / collective storytelling

Gather 6–10 people in a circle and tell an original story — each person contributes only one word at a time. The most logical next word wins. Type A personalities discover what it means to bring a brick instead of a cathedral. The story will take hilarious and unexpected twists.

03
Talk Without I
Focus: Ensemble / other-directedness

Pair participants and instruct them to have a conversation about anything — without using the word "I." Debriefing reveals how we filter everything through our own perspective. Teaches participants to frame things in ways more accessible to others.

04
Mirror
Focus: Ensemble / sustained focus

Split into pairs facing each other. One person leads with small movements; the other mirrors every action. Switch. Then try mirroring with no assigned leader. Point: Interesting doesn't matter — sustained focus does. The foundation of ensemble awareness.

05
Give and Take
Focus: Ensemble / meeting dynamics

Group spreads out. Practice giving focus to someone with a physical cue (eye contact, point, touch). Then practice taking focus. Then do both — give, take, give back. When it works seamlessly, focus flows like a jazz ensemble. When it doesn't, everyone sees why meetings fail.

06
Parts of a Whole
Focus: Ensemble / ceding control

Suggest an animal or object. Participants silently organize their bodies into that shape. Progress to more complex scenarios: shoppers in a grocery store, fish in an aquarium. Teaches that individuals must be willing to play their part for the group to succeed.

07
Take That Back
Focus: Change / adaptability

Two people have a conversation. When the leader claps, the person speaking must replace their last sentence with something different and continue from there. Teaches that we are more resourceful than we think — and that teammates can adapt on the fly remarkably well.

08
Thank You Statues
Focus: Co-creation / trust

One person steps into the center of a circle and strikes a pose. Another taps them out, assumes their own pose, and the first person says "thank you." Speed up. Then remove tap-outs — just keep adding poses to build a statue. Teaches the visceral feeling of support and the risk of initiating ideas.

09
Follow the Follower
Focus: Leadership / ensemble dynamics

Sit in a circle. Everyone makes sounds and movements — but simultaneously mimics others. No assigned leader or follower. The result should look like a school of fish: no individual identifiable as the initiator. When it works, the group functions as one organism.

10
Last Word Response
Focus: Listening / full attention

Pairs have a conversation where each response must begin with the last word spoken by the partner. Forces listening all the way to the end of a thought — not halfway. Reveals how rarely we do this in practice, and how much we miss as a result.

11
Gibberish Games
Focus: Listening / intent over words

Two people speak entirely in gibberish. A third "translates" for the group — not with jokes, but genuinely listening to body language, tone, and energy. Reveals how much communication happens beyond words. Builds cognitive empathy.

12
String of Pearls
Focus: Leadership / storytelling as a team

Group stands in a line. First person gets opening sentence. Last person gets closing sentence. Everyone in between improvises a line that logically moves the story from one to the other. Forces people to consider not just their own contribution, but how it connects to what came before and after.

Improv in the World

Case Studies

Real-world applications of Second City improv principles across industries.

MAJOR LEAGUE BASEBALL

Teaching Life Skills to Rookies

Every January, Second City actors travel to Lansdowne Conference Center in Virginia to help ~100 MLB rookies navigate wealth, fame, media, and organized crime — through custom comedy vignettes and improv workshops. Lectures don't work for 20-year-olds brimming with testosterone. Comedy does.

FARMERS INSURANCE

Empathetic Claims Reps at Scale

For years, Farmers has worked with Second City to help claims reps become more effective and empathetic communicators during times of stress. Programs touch 10,000+ people, win industry awards, and lift customer satisfaction — because they're built on ensemble brainstorming, not top-down approval.

CLOROX

Live Co-Creation on Twitter

The Clorox Ick Awards: a live, improvised award show on Twitter hosted by Rachel Dratch, celebrating parents' messiest moments. Result: #Ickies became a trending topic, generating 160M+ social media impressions from 16,800+ tweets in 4 hours. Audiences respond when you let them in on the act.

LYRIC OPERA CHICAGO

Second City Guide to the Opera

Renée Fleming heard her own voice in a Second City show. Within weeks she was brainstorming with Second City producers. A partnership that seemed impossible — 3,563 seats vs. 300, opera vs. improv — produced a sold-out gala with Patrick Stewart, standing ovations, and national press coverage.

NORWEGIAN CRUISE LINE

Making Fun of Everything That Could Go Wrong

Second City discovered that the most powerful thing they could do on cruise ships was satirize the cruise experience itself — tiny cabins, long buffet lines, the terrifying flush. Norwegian never flinched. Today 6 ships, 450 actors/directors employed. Power lives in addressing the elephant in the room.

U.S. DEPARTMENT OF EDUCATION

Finding Common Ground Through Comedy

School board presidents, superintendents, and teachers' union leaders — historically adversarial — were brought together through live satirical sketches about Finland's "perfect" education system. Shared laughter became shared truth. The conference was less contentious and more productive than any previous convening.

CHICAGO TRIBUNE

Trib Nation: Co-Creating Journalism

After declining readership, editor Gerry Kern invited readers to a bar to talk face-to-face with reporters. More than 100 showed up. Trib Nation and Chicago Live (with Second City's theatrical support) gave the Tribune its first two-way dialogue with readers in decades.

TWITTER

Running a Company Like an Improv Group

CEO Dick Costolo trained in improv comedy in Chicago before building Twitter. He applies improvisational listening principles to how he manages: "Managers have to be open to accepting any kind of initiation. When they deny there's an issue and reflexively defend the status quo, it creates misery."
