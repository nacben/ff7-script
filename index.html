Introduction

What is psychology?

Among all the sciences, psychology is perhaps the most mysterious to the general public, and the most prone to misconceptions. For some, it conjures up images of people in white coats conducting laboratory experiments on rats. Others may imagine a man with a middle-European accent psychoanalyzing a patient on a couch.

The word comes from the ancient Greek psyche, meaning "soul" or "mind," and logia, "study" or "account." Today, it most accurately describes the science of mind and behavior — a bridge between philosophy and physiology that examines the mental processes underlying thought, speech, and action.

"Psychology has a long past, but only a short history." — Hermann Ebbinghaus

Though humans have wondered about the mind for millennia, psychology only became a scientific discipline in 1879, when Wilhelm Wundt opened the world's first experimental psychology laboratory in Leipzig. In barely 150 years, it has blossomed into one of the most diverse and influential of all sciences.

This book traces that blossoming through six great movements — each a different lens on what it means to think, feel, learn, and become.

Part One

Philosophical Roots

Long before psychology had a name, philosophers wrestled with the same questions: What is the mind? Where does it live? Why do we feel what we feel?

c. 129 — c. 201 CE

Galen

Humorism

"The four temperaments of personality."

Building on Hippocrates, Galen proposed that an imbalance of bodily fluids — blood, phlegm, yellow bile, and black bile — produced the sanguine, phlegmatic, choleric, and melancholic temperaments. His doctrines dominated Western medicine for over 1,400 years.

1596 — 1650

René Descartes

Dualism

"There is a reasoning soul in this machine."

Descartes split mind from body, locating the soul in the pineal gland, where it could direct "animal spirits" through the nerves. His dualism shaped centuries of thought about consciousness, free will, and the brain.

1756 — 1819

Abbé Faria

Hypnosis

"Dormez!"

Where Mesmer used magnets, Faria revealed the truth: hypnosis is the power of suggestion alone. His "lucid sleep" became the foundation of modern hypnotism.

1813 — 1855

Søren Kierkegaard

Existentialism

"Be that self which one truly is."

The Danish philosopher argued that despair arises when we deny our true self. His existentialism would later inspire psychologists like R.D. Laing, Rollo May, and Carl Rogers.

1822 — 1911

Francis Galton

Bio-psychology

"Personality is composed of nature and nurture."

Darwin's cousin coined the famous dichotomy. He pioneered statistical methods, the first survey by questionnaire, and the controversial idea of eugenics — for which his legacy remains deeply contested.

1832 — 1920

Wilhelm Wundt

Experimental Psychology

"The beginnings of the mental life date from the beginnings of life."

Founder of the first experimental psychology lab (Leipzig, 1879), Wundt insisted that consciousness — inner experience — was a proper object of scientific study, measurable through introspection and reaction time.

1842 — 1910

William James

Functionalism

"We know the meaning of 'consciousness' so long as no one asks us to define it."

America's father of psychology described the mind as a "stream of consciousness" — fluid, personal, ever-changing. His pragmatic philosophy redirected the field from speculation to function.

1850 — 1909

Hermann Ebbinghaus

Memory Studies

"24 hours after learning something, we forget two-thirds of it."

Using nonsense syllables and brutal self-experimentation, Ebbinghaus discovered the "forgetting curve" — and proved memory could be studied with the rigor of any natural science.

1857 — 1911

Alfred Binet

Intelligence Theory

"The intelligence of an individual is not a fixed quantity."

Commissioned to identify children needing extra help in school, Binet built the first practical intelligence test — while warning that no single number could capture a person's mind.

Part Two

Behaviorism

If the mind cannot be observed, then psychology — the behaviorists declared — must study only what can be measured: behavior itself.

Ivan Pavlov · 1849–1936

The sight of tasty food makes a hungry man's mouth water.

While studying digestion in dogs, the Russian physiologist Ivan Pavlov noticed something stranger than salivation: his dogs began drooling at the mere sound of their keeper's footsteps. He had stumbled onto classical conditioning — the ability to forge an automatic, learned response by pairing a neutral stimulus with a meaningful one.

Pavlov never thought of himself as a psychologist. But his bells, metronomes, and salivating dogs would launch the dominant school of American psychology for the next half century.

🔔 + 🍖 → 💧
Unconditioned stimulus + neutral stimulus
= conditioned response
THE PUZZLE BOX
A hungry cat trapped inside
→ flails, paws, scrambles
→ accidentally trips the latch
→ escapes & eats

Each attempt: faster.
Profitless acts stamped out.
Rewarded acts stamped in.
Edward Thorndike · 1874–1949

Profitless acts are stamped out.

Working in a basement at Harvard with cats and "puzzle boxes," Thorndike formulated his Law of Effect: behaviors followed by satisfying outcomes are repeated; those followed by frustration die away. It was the first formal statement of an idea that would dominate learning theory for a century.

Thorndike's connectionism foreshadowed Skinner, his CAVD tests shaped intelligence measurement, and his rigorous experimental method helped pull psychology out of the armchair and into the laboratory.

John B. Watson · 1878–1958

Anyone, regardless of their nature, can be trained to be anything.

In 1913, Watson issued the "behaviorist manifesto," declaring that psychology must abandon the unobservable mind and concentrate solely on behavior. Seven years later, his ethically dubious "Little Albert" experiment conditioned an 11-month-old baby to fear a white rat — and, by extension, all furry things.

Watson's boast that he could train any healthy infant to become "doctor, lawyer, artist, merchant-chief, and yes, even beggar-man and thief" remains one of the most extreme statements of environmental determinism ever made.

"Give me a dozen healthy infants…
and I'll guarantee to take any one
at random and train him to become
any type of specialist I might select."
— Watson, Behaviorism (1924)
THE SKINNER BOX
Rat presses lever
→ food pellet drops
→ rat presses again

Positive reinforcement
shapes behavior over time —
no preceding stimulus required.
B.F. Skinner · 1904–1990

Behavior is shaped by positive and negative reinforcement.

Where Pavlov saw stimulus → response, Skinner saw action → consequence. His operant conditioning argued that we learn not because something precedes our behavior, but because something follows it. Reward what you want repeated. Withhold reward for what you don't.

An inveterate "gadget man," Skinner invented teaching machines, pigeon-guided missiles, and even a temperature-controlled crib for his daughter — sparking decades of rumors. His "radical behaviorism" dismissed free will as illusion, declaring that all behavior is shaped by genetic and environmental history.

Imprinting

Konrad Lorenz & the Goslings

1903–1989 · Ethology

Lorenz discovered that newly hatched birds bond with the first moving object they see — even, famously, his own Wellington boots. Imprinting, he showed, happens only during a "critical period" and cannot be undone.

"Imprinting cannot be forgotten."
Latent Learning

Edward Tolman & the Maze

1886–1959 · Cognitive Behaviorism

Tolman's rats learned the layout of mazes before any reward appeared, building "cognitive maps" they could deploy once food was on offer. Conditioning, he argued, was not the whole story.

"That great God-given maze which is our human world."
Desensitization

Joseph Wolpe & War Neurosis

1915–1997 · Behavior Therapy

Treating WWII veterans, Wolpe found that talking through trauma wasn't working. He devised reciprocal inhibition: pair the feared image with deep relaxation, and the fear dissolves.

"Stop imagining the scene and relax."
Part Three

Psychotherapy

If behaviorism studied the surface, psychotherapy plumbed the depths — the unconscious, the soul, the buried wound. From Freud's couch to Rogers' empathy, this is the long conversation between healer and human.

1856 — 1939

Sigmund Freud

Psychoanalysis

"The unconscious is the true psychical reality."

The Viennese neurologist gave us the unconscious, the id–ego–superego, dream analysis, repression, the talking cure, and a vocabulary the entire 20th century would speak. Whether you accept his theories or reject them, you cannot escape them.

1870 — 1937

Alfred Adler

Individual Psychology

"The neurotic carries a feeling of inferiority with him constantly."

A founding member of Freud's circle who broke away, Adler argued that we are driven not by sex but by the universal childhood experience of feeling small — and the lifelong striving to overcome it.

1875 — 1961

Carl Jung

Analytical Psychology

"The collective unconscious is made up of archetypes."

Jung saw beneath the personal unconscious a deeper layer — a shared inheritance of symbols, the Shadow, the Anima, the Wise Old Man — drawn from myths recurring across every culture.

1882 — 1960

Melanie Klein

Object Relations

"The struggle between the life and death instincts persists throughout life."

A pioneer of child psychoanalysis, Klein revealed the dramatic emotional life of infants — already torn, she argued, between love and hate from the earliest months.

1885 — 1952

Karen Horney

Neo-Freudian

"The tyranny of the shoulds."

Horney named the internalized voice that drives neurosis: I should be perfect. I should be loved. I should never fail. Therapy, she argued, must liberate us from these impossible demands.

1893 — 1970

Fritz Perls

Gestalt Therapy

"Truth can be tolerated only if you discover it yourself."

Co-founder of Gestalt therapy, Perls emphasized present awareness, personal responsibility, and the dethroning of "shoulds." Reality, he insisted, is built moment by moment from our own perceptions.

1900 — 1980

Erich Fromm

Humanistic Psychoanalysis

"Man's main task is to give birth to himself."

Drawing from Marx, Freud, and Zen alike, Fromm asked why humans fear freedom. His answer reshaped how we think about love, work, and the courage it takes to become oneself.

1902 — 1987

Carl Rogers

Humanistic / Person-Centered

"The good life is a process, not a state of being."

Rogers replaced the all-knowing analyst with the empathic companion. His radical idea — that people, given unconditional positive regard, will heal themselves — transformed counseling forever.

1908 — 1970

Abraham Maslow

Humanistic

"What a man can be, he must be."

Maslow's hierarchy of needs — from food and safety up to self-actualization and transcendence — refocused psychology on flourishing rather than illness, and inspired the entire humanistic movement.

1905 — 1997

Viktor Frankl

Logotherapy

"Suffering ceases to be suffering at the moment it finds a meaning."

Survivor of Auschwitz, Frankl founded a therapy built on the unconquerable human freedom to choose meaning — even in the worst circumstances imaginable.

1913 — 2007

Albert Ellis

REBT

"Rational beliefs create healthy emotional consequences."

Long before cognitive therapy had a name, Ellis was teaching patients to dispute their irrational beliefs. Events do not disturb us; our interpretations of them do.

1927 — 1989

R.D. Laing

Anti-Psychiatry

"Madness need not be all breakdown. It may also be break-through."

Laing dared to listen to schizophrenic patients — and heard not nonsense but coded distress, a desperate communication from an impossible situation. His radical empathy transformed psychiatric care.

Abraham Maslow · 1943

The Hierarchy of Needs

Maslow proposed that human motivation climbs a ladder. The bottom rungs — food, water, safety — must be secured before we can attend to belonging, esteem, or growth. At the summit lies self-actualization: becoming what one is uniquely meant to be.

Later in life, Maslow added a higher tier still: self-transcendence — devoting oneself to something beyond the self, whether other people, an ideal, or the universe entire.

Though contested by modern researchers, the pyramid endures because it captures something true: that human flourishing has structure, and that we cannot ask people to seek meaning before we have helped them eat.

Part Four

Cognitive Psychology

The Calculating Brain. By the 1950s, computers gave psychologists a new metaphor: the mind as information processor. The "cognitive revolution" reopened doors behaviorism had nailed shut.

Insight Learning

Wolfgang Köhler & the Chimpanzees

1887–1967 · Gestalt

Watching apes solve problems on Tenerife, Köhler saw something behaviorism couldn't explain: the moment of aha! Pause. Survey. Insight. Then action. Animals could think — not just react.

"Instinct is a dynamic pattern."
Memory

Bluma Zeigarnik & the Waiters

1901–1988 · Gestalt / Memory

Waiters could recall unpaid orders perfectly — and forgot them the moment the bill was settled. The Zeigarnik effect: interrupted tasks linger in memory; completed ones fade.

"Interruption greatly improves a task's chances of being remembered."
Cognitive Dissonance

Leon Festinger & the Doomsday Cult

1919–1989 · Social Cognition

When prophecies failed and the world refused to end, true believers didn't abandon faith — they doubled down. Festinger showed how we twist reality to protect our convictions.

"A man with conviction is a hard man to change."
Working Memory

George Armitage Miller & the Magical Seven

1920–2012 · Cognitive Psychology

The mind, Miller showed, can juggle about seven items at once — give or take two. To remember more, we must "chunk": group raw bits into meaningful units.

"The magical number 7, plus or minus 2."
Cognitive Therapy

Aaron Beck & the Surface of Thought

1921–2021 · CBT

Where Freud probed the deep unconscious, Beck listened to the automatic, surface-level thoughts depressed patients reported — and showed that changing those thoughts could change everything.

"There's more to the surface than meets the eye."
Selective Attention

Donald Broadbent & the Air Traffic Controllers

1926–1993 · Attention

Why can pilots only handle one radio voice at a time? Broadbent's filter model gave us the first scientific theory of attention — and helped redesign cockpits, control rooms, and modern life.

"We can listen to only one voice at once."
Memory Reliability

Elizabeth Loftus & the Lost in the Mall

1944– · Memory

Loftus implanted entirely false childhood memories — of being lost in a shopping mall — in adult participants. Her work overturned decades of legal and therapeutic assumptions about "recovered memory."

"What we believe with all our hearts is not necessarily the truth."
Positive Psychology

Mihály Csíkszentmihályi & Flow

1934–2021 · Positive Psychology

When skill meets challenge, time disappears and self dissolves. Csíkszentmihályi called this state flow — the closest most of us come to everyday ecstasy.

"Ecstasy is a step into an alternative reality."
Memory's Failings

Daniel Schacter & the Seven Sins

1952– · Cognitive Neuroscience

Transience. Absent-mindedness. Blocking. Misattribution. Suggestibility. Bias. Persistence. Schacter's seven "sins" aren't flaws — they're the necessary costs of a memory system that mostly works brilliantly.

"The seven sins of memory."
Part Five

Social Psychology

Being in a world of others. The most disturbing — and important — findings in psychology have come from studying not what individuals do alone, but what they do together.

Solomon Asch · 1951

How strong is the urge toward social conformity?

Asch showed simple line-matching tasks to groups in which everyone but one person was a confederate giving deliberately wrong answers. Despite the obvious correct response, 75% of participants conformed to the group at least once, and 32% conformed consistently.

Most knew they were wrong. They went along anyway — to fit in, to not stand out, to not be foolish. The line experiment revealed the quiet, daily, near-universal capitulation of perception to peer pressure.

Which matches the reference line?
Everyone in the room says the wrong one.
What do you say?
65%
of participants administered
the maximum 450-volt shock
Psychiatrists predicted fewer than 0.1%.
Stanley Milgram · 1961

People do what they are told to do.

In a Yale basement, ordinary people — postal workers, salesmen, teachers — pressed switches they believed were delivering excruciating, perhaps lethal shocks to a stranger, because a man in a lab coat told them to continue.

Milgram had set out to test whether something about Germans had enabled the Holocaust. What he found was something more terrifying: it wasn't about Germans. The capacity to obey unspeakable orders is in nearly all of us, waiting for the right authority.

Philip Zimbardo · 1971

What happens when you put good people in an evil place?

Zimbardo recruited 24 healthy, mentally-screened college students, flipped a coin to make half "guards" and half "prisoners," and locked them in a mock prison in the Stanford psychology basement.

The experiment was scheduled to run two weeks. It was shut down after six days. Guards were systematically degrading prisoners. Prisoners were breaking down. Even Zimbardo — playing the "warden" — had lost moral perspective.

The lesson: it isn't the individual disposition that produces cruelty. It is the situation. And we are all far more susceptible to situations than we like to believe.

1890 — 1947

Kurt Lewin

Field Theory

"You cannot understand a system until you try to change it."

The father of social psychology mapped behavior as the product of an entire "life space" — a force field of person and environment in dynamic interaction.

1922 — 1982

Erving Goffman

Dramaturgy

"Life is a dramatically enacted thing."

Goffman saw all of social life as theater: front stage and back stage, costumes and scripts, the careful management of impression.

1923 — 2008

Robert Zajonc

Familiarity

"The more you see it, the more you like it."

The "mere exposure effect": we develop preference for things we've simply encountered before — even when we don't consciously remember encountering them.

1929 —

Melvin Lerner

Just-World Hypothesis

"We believe people get what they deserve."

The terrifying corollary: we blame victims, not because they deserve it, but because we cannot bear the thought that misfortune might be random — and might come for us.

1932 —

Elliot Aronson

Social Cognition

"People who do crazy things are not necessarily crazy."

Ordinary people can be moved by situational pressures to commit extraordinarily cruel acts — and afterward, will reshape their beliefs to justify what they have done.

1942 — 1989

Ignacio Martín-Baró

Liberation Psychology

"Trauma must be understood in terms of the relationship between the individual and society."

Murdered by a Salvadoran death squad for his work, Martín-Baró insisted that psychology must address the suffering caused by injustice and oppression — not just heal individuals, but help liberate societies.

Part Six

Developmental Psychology

From infant to adult. How does a person come to be? Through stages, through attachments, through language, through play — through countless invisible negotiations with the world.

Jean Piaget · 1896–1980

The goal of education is to create men and women who are capable of doing new things.

Piaget overturned the prevailing assumption that children were just small adults with less knowledge. They are, he argued, qualitatively different thinkers, progressing through distinct stages — sensorimotor, pre-operational, concrete operational, formal operational — each with its own logic.

Children build knowledge by acting on the world. They are not empty vessels to be filled but active scientists, constantly testing hypotheses. The role of the educator is not to lecture, but to set the stage.

PIAGET'S STAGES
0–2 yrs · Sensorimotor — learn through senses
2–7 yrs · Pre-operational — symbols & language
7–11 yrs · Concrete operational — logic with objects
11+ yrs · Formal operational — abstract reasoning
HARLOW'S MONKEYS, 1959
Wire mother with milk · or ·
cloth mother with no food?

The infants chose comfort.
Attachment, Harlow proved,
is more than food.
John Bowlby, Harry Harlow & Mary Ainsworth

Early emotional bonds are an integral part of human nature.

Bowlby argued that infants are biologically programmed to form an attachment to a caregiver — a survival imperative as basic as feeding. Disrupted attachment in the first years, he warned, leaves wounds that may never fully heal.

Harlow's monkeys proved the point in stark terms: given a choice between a cold wire mother who provided milk and a soft cloth mother who did not, infants clung to comfort. Mary Ainsworth's "Strange Situation" studies later identified secure, avoidant, and resistant patterns of attachment that shape relationships across the lifespan.

1896 — 1934

Lev Vygotsky

Sociocultural Theory

"We become ourselves through others."

Where Piaget saw the lone child explorer, Vygotsky saw a child embedded in a culture, learning through interaction with parents, teachers, and peers. The "zone of proximal development" defines what a learner can do with help — and will soon do alone.

1902 — 1994

Erik Erikson

Psychosocial Development

"Anything that grows has a ground plan."

Erikson mapped life as eight psychosocial stages, each with its own central conflict: trust vs. mistrust, intimacy vs. isolation, ego-integrity vs. despair. He coined the phrase "identity crisis."

1925 — 2021

Albert Bandura

Social Learning Theory

"Most human behavior is learned through modeling."

In the famous Bobo doll experiments, children who watched adults beat an inflatable clown went on to imitate the violence — and invent new forms of it. We learn, Bandura showed, by watching.

1927 — 1987

Lawrence Kohlberg

Moral Development

"Morality develops in six stages."

From obeying to avoid punishment, through obeying for social approval, to acting on universal principles of justice — Kohlberg charted the long path from "won't get caught" to conscience.

1928 —

Noam Chomsky

Nativism

"The language organ grows like any other body organ."

Chomsky's review of Skinner's Verbal Behavior helped end behaviorism's reign. Language, he argued, cannot be learned by reinforcement alone — children must come pre-wired with the grammar of all possible human languages.

1958 —

Simon Baron-Cohen

Theory of Mind

"Autism is an extreme form of the male brain."

Baron-Cohen distinguishes "empathizing" brains, attuned to others' feelings, from "systematizing" brains, attuned to how things work. Autism, he proposes, sits at the far systematizing end of the spectrum.

Part Seven

Psychology of Difference

Personality and Intelligence. What makes us individuals? Why are no two minds the same? And how — carefully, with humility — might we begin to measure such things?

1897 — 1967

Gordon Allport

Trait Theory

"Did Robinson Crusoe lack personality traits before the advent of Friday?"

Allport pioneered the systematic study of personality, identifying cardinal, central, and secondary traits. Personality, he argued, isn't a costume we wear — it is shaped in and revealed through relationships.

1897 — 1987

J.P. Guilford

Creativity

"Name as many uses as you can think of for a toothpick."

Guilford distinguished convergent thinking (one right answer) from divergent thinking (many possibilities). Standard IQ tests, he argued, captured only the first — and missed the wellspring of creativity entirely.

1905 — 1998

Raymond Cattell

Intelligence Theory

"General intelligence consists of both fluid and crystallized intelligence."

Fluid intelligence — raw reasoning — peaks in young adulthood and declines. Crystallized intelligence — accumulated knowledge — keeps growing for a lifetime. Cattell helped psychology measure them apart.

1916 — 1997

Hans J. Eysenck

Personality

"There is an association between insanity and genius."

Eysenck mapped personality along three superfactors — Psychoticism, Extraversion, Neuroticism — and proposed that creative genius shares a key cognitive feature with psychosis: over-inclusive thinking.

1917 — 1998

David C. McClelland

Need Theory

"Three key motivations drive performance."

Achievement. Power. Affiliation. McClelland argued these three unconscious drives — not IQ, not credentials — best predict whether a person will thrive in a given role.

1927 —

Nico Frijda

Emotion Theory

"Emotion is an essentially unconscious process."

Emotions, Frijda showed, arise before we know it — preparing the body for action. Feelings come second: our conscious interpretation of what emotion has already done to us.

1930 —

Walter Mischel

Person × Situation

"Behavior without environmental cues would be absurdly chaotic."

Mischel's "marshmallow test" — could a four-year-old resist one treat now for two later? — predicted achievement decades on. But he also showed that trait scores alone barely predict behavior. Context is everything.

1932 —

David Rosenhan

Antipsychiatry

"We cannot distinguish the sane from the insane in psychiatric hospitals."

Eight sane volunteers feigned a single symptom to gain admission to psychiatric hospitals. All were diagnosed; all stayed an average of 19 days; the diagnoses followed them out the door. Rosenhan exposed psychiatric labels as institutional artifacts.

Psychology has questioned deeply held beliefs, unearthed unsettling truths, and provided startling insights into the mysterious world of the human mind.

— Adapted from the Introduction

Reference

A short glossary

Key terms drawn from across the book — the vocabulary of mind.

Archetypes
Jung's universal symbolic patterns within the collective unconscious — the Mother, the Shadow, the Wise Old Man — that organize experience across cultures.
Attachment
The emotionally significant bond, especially between infant and caregiver, from which security and exploration both flow.
Behaviorism
The psychological approach that takes observable behavior — not the inner mind — as its only legitimate subject of study.
Classical Conditioning
Pavlovian learning: a neutral stimulus, paired with a meaningful one, comes to trigger the same response.
Cognitive Dissonance
The mental discomfort of holding inconsistent beliefs — and the lengths we go to resolve it.
Collective Unconscious
Jung's deepest layer of mind: shared, inherited symbols and patterns common to all humans.
Cognitive Therapy
Beck's evidence-based approach: change the thoughts, and the feelings often follow.
Ego / Id / Superego
Freud's three parts of the psyche: the rational mediator, the primal desires, and the moral judge.
Flow
Csíkszentmihályi's state of total absorption in an activity that perfectly balances skill and challenge.
Free Association
The psychoanalytic technique of speaking whatever comes to mind, in hopes of bypassing conscious censorship.
Gestalt
"The whole is greater than the sum of its parts." A school emphasizing organized perception and meaning.
Imprinting
Lorenz's term for the rapid, irreversible attachment formed by young animals to a parent figure during a critical period.
Intelligence Quotient (IQ)
A numeric measure of intelligence, originally derived by dividing mental age by chronological age.
Operant Conditioning
Skinner's principle: behavior is shaped by its consequences. Reward what you want; ignore what you don't.
Personality Trait
A stable disposition to think, feel, and act in characteristic ways across situations.
Psychoanalysis
Freud's set of theories and clinical methods for exploring unconscious processes through dialogue.
Repression
The unconscious banishment of unbearable thoughts or memories from awareness — a Freudian defense mechanism.
Schema
A mental framework that organizes knowledge and guides expectation, central to Piaget's theory of cognitive development.
Self-Actualization
Maslow's summit: the realization of one's fullest unique potential as a human being.
Stream of Consciousness
William James's image of the mind as a flowing, ever-changing river of thought, sensation, and memory.
Unconditional Positive Regard
Rogers's therapeutic stance: accept the person fully, without conditions, simply because they are human.
Unconscious
The vast part of the psyche operating beneath awareness — repository, in Freud's view, of repressed wishes; in Jung's, of inherited archetypes.
Zeigarnik Effect
The tendency to remember interrupted tasks better than completed ones.
Zone of Proximal Development
