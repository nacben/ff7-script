What is philosophy?

Philosophy is not the preserve of brilliant but eccentric thinkers. It is what every one of us does in those quiet moments when we stop and wonder what life and the universe are really about. We are inquisitive creatures, gifted with reason — and whenever we reason, we philosophize.

The earliest philosophers, in ancient Greece, China, and India, were not satisfied with the explanations supplied by religion and custom. They sought rational answers, and — just as importantly — they argued with each other. Ideas were sharpened in debate, refined in dialogue, and passed down through schools of thought that still shape how we think today.

Philosophy is less a body of answers than a way of asking. To follow its long argument is to learn how to think.

700 BCE — 250 CE

The Ancient World

✦

Where it begins. In a handful of Greek city-states, in the courts of warring Chinese kingdoms, and beneath a bodhi tree in northern India, a new kind of question is being asked — not what do the gods say? but what can reason show?

Pre-Socratic · Miletus

Thales of Miletus

c. 624 – 546 BCE

Everything is made of water.

The first philosopher we know by name. Thales abandoned myth and sought a single underlying substance from which all things are formed. Water, he reasoned, takes every form — solid, liquid, vapor — and is essential to life. His real revolution was not the answer, but the question itself: a natural cause for everything.

Branch · Metaphysics · Monism

Daoism · China

Laozi

c. 6th century BCE

The Dao that can be told is not the eternal Dao.

The Way (Dao) is the source of all things and lies beyond words. To live virtuously is to act in harmony with nature through wu wei — effortless action, free from striving and ambition. A philosophy of stillness, balance, and the wisdom of yielding.

Tradition · Chinese · Daoism

Mathematics & Mysticism

Pythagoras

c. 570 – 495 BCE

Number is the ruler of forms and ideas.

Behind the changing surfaces of the world, Pythagoras saw the unchanging structure of mathematics. Strings divided in simple ratios make harmonious sounds; the same proportions, he believed, govern the cosmos itself. He fused science with religion in a community devoted to the purity of number.

Branch · Metaphysics · Pythagoreanism

Buddhism · India

Siddhartha Gautama — The Buddha

c. 563 – 483 BCE

Happy is he who has overcome his ego.

Born a prince, he discovered that neither luxury nor extreme asceticism brings peace. Through reasoning, he arrived at the Four Noble Truths: that suffering is universal, that its root is craving and attachment, that craving can be ended, and that the path is the Eightfold Way — right understanding, intention, speech, action, livelihood, effort, mindfulness, and concentration. The self we cling to is, in truth, no fixed thing at all.

Tradition · Eastern · Buddhism

Confucianism · China

Confucius

551 – 479 BCE

Hold faithfulness and sincerity as first principles.

In a time of political chaos, Confucius taught that a stable society rests on cultivated virtue (de), respect for ritual, and right relationships — sovereign and subject, parent and child, friend and friend. Lead by example, he insisted, and the world will follow. A Golden Rule in negative form: do not impose on others what you would not choose for yourself.

Tradition · Chinese · Confucianism

Flux & Change

Heraclitus

c. 535 – 475 BCE

Everything is flux.

You cannot step into the same river twice. For Heraclitus, reality is constant change, and the universe is held together by the tension of opposites — day and night, hot and cold, life and death — all governed by an underlying logos or cosmic reason.

Branch · Metaphysics · Monism

Eleatic School

Parmenides

c. 515 – 445 BCE

All is one.

The diametrical opposite of Heraclitus. Reasoning that what exists cannot come from what does not exist, Parmenides concluded that reality must be one, eternal, and unchanging — and that the change our senses report must be an illusion. The first philosopher to trust reason above experience.

Branch · Metaphysics · Monism

The Sophists

Protagoras

c. 490 – 420 BCE

Man is the measure of all things.

Truth, beauty, justice — none of these, Protagoras argued, exist absolutely; each is relative to the person who perceives it. A radical relativism that placed the human being, not the cosmos or the gods, at the center of philosophy.

Branch · Ethics · Relativism

Atomism

Democritus & Leucippus

c. 460 – 371 BCE

Nothing exists except atoms and empty space.

A startlingly modern vision: the universe consists of indivisible particles moving in a void, combining and recombining to form everything we see. Their materialist atomism would lie dormant for two thousand years before re-emerging in modern science.

Branch · Metaphysics · Atomism

Athens · Founder of Western Philosophy

Socrates

469 – 399 BCE

The unexamined life is not worth living.

He wrote nothing, founded no school, claimed no doctrine — only that he knew that he knew nothing. By relentless questioning in the streets of Athens, Socrates exposed the unexamined assumptions beneath everyday concepts of justice, virtue, and beauty. His dialectical method — drawing truth out of others through dialogue — became the foundation of Western thought. Sentenced to death for corrupting the young, he drank the hemlock without flinching, and made his death an argument for the life he had lived.

Branch · Epistemology · Dialectical Method

The Academy · Athens

Plato

c. 427 – 347 BCE

Earthly knowledge is but shadow.

Imagine prisoners chained in a cave, watching shadows flicker on the wall, mistaking them for reality. So we live, said Plato. The changing things our senses report are only imperfect copies of eternal, perfect Forms — Justice, Beauty, the Good — accessible only through reason. The soul, immortal, once dwelt among them, and learning is a kind of remembering. From his Academy flowed two thousand years of idealist philosophy.

Branch · Epistemology · Rationalism

The Lyceum · Athens

Aristotle

384 – 322 BCE

Truth resides in the world around us.

Plato's greatest pupil — and his greatest critic. Where Plato looked up to a realm of perfect Forms, Aristotle looked carefully at the world: classifying animals, mapping the heavens, building the first formal system of logic. Each thing has its own nature, its purpose (telos), its way of flourishing. To live well is to fulfill what we essentially are: rational, social, ethical beings. His empirical, teleological vision shaped science and theology for two millennia.

Branch · Epistemology · Empiricism

The Garden · Athens

Epicurus

341 – 270 BCE

Death is nothing to us.

The goal of life is tranquility — peace of mind, freedom from fear and pain. The greatest fear is of death, but death is simply the end of sensation: where it is, we are not. Live wisely, justly, and well, and you cannot live unhappily.

Branch · Ethics · Epicureanism

The Cynics

Diogenes of Sinope

c. 404 – 323 BCE

He has the most who is most content with the least.

Living in an abandoned tub, owning nothing, scorning convention with theatrical glee, Diogenes embodied his philosophy. The good life, he insisted, requires not riches but freedom from desire — and the courage to be shameless before a society that mistakes custom for virtue.

Branch · Ethics · Cynicism

Stoicism

Zeno of Citium

c. 332 – 265 BCE

The goal of life is living in agreement with nature.

The cosmos is governed by a rational order. We cannot control what happens to us, but we can control how we meet it. By accepting what we cannot change and exercising virtue in what we can, we live in harmony with the whole. The philosophy that shaped Rome.

Branch · Ethics · Stoicism

Wonder is very much the affection of a philosopher; for there is no other beginning of philosophy than this. — Plato
250 — 1500

The Medieval World

✦

Athens fades; the cathedrals rise. For a thousand years, philosophy in Christendom, the Islamic world, and Jewish thought wrestles with the same problem: how to reconcile the inheritance of Greek reason with the demands of revealed faith.

Christian Platonism

St. Augustine of Hippo

354 – 430

God is not the parent of evils.

If God is good, why is there evil? Augustine answers: evil is not a thing at all but a lack — an absence of the good, as blindness is an absence of sight. And it exists because God gave us free will, and rational creatures must be free to choose, even badly.

Branch · Ethics · Christian Platonism

Late Roman

Boethius

c. 480 – 525

God foresees our free thoughts and actions.

Awaiting execution in prison, Boethius wrestled with a question: if God knows what we will do, are we still free? His answer: God does not live in the flow of time as we do, but in an eternal present. He sees our future as we see our present — without determining it.

Branch · Epistemology · Christian Platonism

Islamic Aristotelianism

Avicenna (Ibn Sînâ)

980 – 1037

The soul is distinct from the body.

Imagine a man created floating in mid-air, blindfolded, sensing nothing. He would still be certain of one thing: that he is. From this "Flying Man" thought-experiment — a remarkable anticipation of Descartes — Avicenna argued that the self is something other than the body it inhabits.

Branch · Metaphysics · Arabic Aristotelianism

The Ontological Argument

St. Anselm

1033 – 1109

Just by thinking about God we can know he exists.

God is "that than which nothing greater can be thought." But a being that exists in reality is greater than one that exists only in the mind. Therefore — runs Anselm's astonishing proof — God must exist. Centuries of philosophers have argued whether this clever move really succeeds.

Branch · Philosophy of Religion

Islamic Philosophy

Averroes (Ibn Rushd)

1126 – 1198

Philosophy and religion are not incompatible.

Sacred texts speak in poetry for those who cannot reason philosophically — but the educated have a duty to interpret them in light of demonstrative truth. Where the surface of scripture conflicts with reason, look deeper. Averroes' commentaries on Aristotle would reshape Christian Europe.

Branch · Arabic Aristotelianism

Jewish Philosophy

Moses Maimonides

1135 – 1204

God has no attributes.

To say God is "good" or "powerful" treats God as one kind of being among others. But God is beyond all categories. We can only say what God is not — a "negative theology" that protects the divine from being shrunk to our scale.

Branch · Philosophy of Religion

Sufism · Persia

Jalal ad-Din Rumi

1207 – 1273

Don't grieve. Anything you lose comes round in another form.

The universe is a constant flow of life through changing forms. To die in one form is to be reborn in another, and to know this is to lose the fear of loss. Rumi's mystical poetry made the inner journey to God a matter of love, music, and dance.

Tradition · Islamic · Sufism

Scholasticism · The Synthesis

Thomas Aquinas

c. 1225 – 1274

The universe has not always existed.

Aristotle taught that the universe is eternal. The Bible teaches that it had a beginning. Aquinas, the great synthesizer, refused to dismiss either. The universe, he concluded, did have a beginning — but only because God chose to create it that way; God could just as well have created an eternal one. Faith and reason are not enemies. Both come from the same source, and they cannot, rightly understood, contradict.

Branch · Metaphysics · Christian Aristotelianism

German Mysticism

Nikolaus von Kues

1401 – 1464

God is the not-other.

God lies before all things, before even the possibility of things — and so cannot be described by any concept, attribute, or name we might apply to creation. The closest we can come is to call God the Not-Other: the source through which everything else is.

Branch · Philosophy of Religion

Renaissance Humanism

Desiderius Erasmus

1466 – 1536

To know nothing is the happiest life.

A witty satire on the corruption of the Church and the pretensions of intellectual theology. True religion, said Erasmus, is a matter of simple, heartfelt faith — and a touch of saving folly, which knows it does not know.

Branch · Philosophy of Religion · Humanism

I am myself and my circumstances. — José Ortega y Gasset
1500 — 1750

Renaissance & the Age of Reason

✦

The Church loses its monopoly on truth. Science replaces dogma, humanity replaces God at the center of the picture, and philosophy splits into two rival schools — those who would build knowledge from reason alone, and those who would build it from experience.

Renaissance · Florence

Niccolò Machiavelli

1469 – 1527

The end justifies the means.

The first modern political realist. A successful prince, Machiavelli observed, cannot afford the luxury of Christian morality — power has its own logic. Better to be feared than loved, and to do whatever stability requires. A coldly clear-eyed look at how politics actually works.

Branch · Political Philosophy · Realism

Renaissance Humanism

Michel de Montaigne

1533 – 1592

Fame and tranquillity can never be bedfellows.

Tranquility, said Montaigne, depends on detachment from the opinion of others. To chase glory is to surrender our peace to the crowd. His essays — the genre is his invention — turn the mind on itself and ask, gently and skeptically: what do I really know?

Branch · Ethics · Humanism

Scientific Revolution · England

Francis Bacon

1561 – 1626

Knowledge is power.

Science should not be the pursuit of personal glory but the improvement of human life. Bacon set out a new method — observation, experiment, induction — and warned against the "idols" of the mind: the prejudices and pet theories that distort what we see.

Branch · Philosophy of Science · Empiricism

Physicalism · England

Thomas Hobbes

1588 – 1679

Man is a machine.

Everything in the universe is body in motion — including us. Mind, soul, will: all are mechanisms. In our natural state, life is "solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short," which is why we surrender our freedom to a sovereign through a social contract. The price of order is obedience.

Branch · Metaphysics · Physicalism

The Father of Modern Philosophy · France

René Descartes

1596 – 1650

I think, therefore I am.

Descartes resolved to doubt everything that could possibly be doubted — the evidence of his senses, the existence of the world, even mathematics — in case some malicious demon were deceiving him. Only one belief survived: that he was doubting, which meant he existed as a thinking thing. From this single, unshakeable point — the cogito — he set out to rebuild knowledge on the foundations of reason alone. Modern philosophy begins here.

Branch · Epistemology · Rationalism

Jansenism · France

Blaise Pascal

1623 – 1662

Imagination decides everything.

Reason is not master in its own house. The imagination — the way we are moved by appearances, dress, manner, and habit — sways us more than logic ever does. A mathematician's wager on faith: the potential gain of belief outweighs the loss, even if the odds are uncertain.

Branch · Philosophy of Mind · Voluntarism

Rationalism · Netherlands

Benedictus Spinoza

1632 – 1677

God is the cause of all things, which are in him.

There is only one substance, and it has infinite attributes. We call it God, or Nature — they are the same. Everything that exists is a mode of this single substance, and the more we understand it, the more we understand ourselves as part of an eternal whole.

Branch · Metaphysics · Monism

Empiricism · England

John Locke

1632 – 1704

No man's knowledge here can go beyond his experience.

The mind at birth, Locke argued, is a blank slate — a tabula rasa — on which experience writes. We have no innate ideas, no inborn knowledge of God, mathematics, or morality. All comes from the senses, refined by reflection. From this empiricist starting point, Locke built a philosophy of natural rights, religious toleration, and government by consent — the philosophical backbone of modern liberal democracy.

Branch · Epistemology · Empiricism

Rationalism · Germany

Gottfried Leibniz

1646 – 1716

There are two kinds of truths: truths of reasoning and truths of fact.

Mathematical truths are necessary — their denial is contradictory. Truths about the world are contingent — they could be otherwise. The universe, Leibniz believed, is composed of countless simple substances called monads, each mirroring the whole in its own way. And ours is the best of all possible worlds.

Branch · Epistemology · Rationalism

Idealism · Ireland

George Berkeley

1685 – 1753

To be is to be perceived.

If all we ever experience are ideas in our minds, what reason have we to believe that anything else exists? For Berkeley, there are no material objects — only perceiving minds and the ideas they perceive. And the world keeps existing when no one is looking because it is always perceived by God.

Branch · Metaphysics · Idealism

Doubt is not a pleasant condition, but certainty is absurd. — Voltaire
1750 — 1900

The Age of Revolution

✦

Old orders crumble. The American and French revolutions overturn kings; the Industrial Revolution overturns society itself. Philosophers ask new questions about liberty, equality, history, and the human mind — and German Idealism reshapes the map of thought.

The Enlightenment · France

Voltaire

1694 – 1778

Doubt is not a pleasant condition, but certainty is absurd.

The wittiest weapon of the French Enlightenment. Voltaire used satire and acid prose to ridicule superstition, intolerance, and arbitrary power. Almost every "fact" of history has been revised; almost every dogma will be. Doubt everything — and defend, even for those you disagree with, the freedom to say it.

Branch · Epistemology · Scepticism

Empiricism's High Point · Scotland

David Hume

1711 – 1776

Custom is the great guide of human life.

Why do we believe the sun will rise tomorrow? Not because reason shows it must, but because it always has — and habit forms in us the expectation. Hume's devastating analysis of cause and effect showed that we never actually see necessary connection in the world; we only see one thing follow another. What we call knowledge of nature is, at root, custom. He woke Kant from his "dogmatic slumbers" and remains the most genial and unsettling of sceptics.

Branch · Epistemology · Empiricism

The Social Contract · Geneva

Jean-Jacques Rousseau

1712 – 1778

Man was born free, yet everywhere he is in chains.

In our natural state, Rousseau imagined, human beings are innocent and free, moved by compassion and self-love. It is civilization that has corrupted us — by introducing property, inequality, and law that protects the rich from the poor. The remedy is not to go back to the forest, but forward to a new social contract in which all citizens together form the General Will, and rule is by the people for the people. The slogan of the French Revolution.

Branch · Political Philosophy

Economics · Scotland

Adam Smith

1723 – 1790

Man is an animal that makes bargains.

No dog, Smith observed, has ever been seen trading bones with another. Trade and the division of labor are uniquely human, and when individuals pursue their own interests in a free market, an "invisible hand" produces the wealth of nations. The founder of modern economics — but also a moralist who worried about what repetitive labor does to the worker.

Branch · Political Philosophy · Economics

Transcendental Idealism · Königsberg

Immanuel Kant

1724 – 1804

There are two worlds: our bodies and the external world.

Kant's "Copernican revolution": we do not conform to the world; the world, as we know it, conforms to us. Space, time, and the basic categories of cause and substance are not features of reality in itself — they are the frame our minds impose on experience. The world as we know it (phenomena) is shaped by us; the world in itself (noumena) lies forever beyond our grasp. From his ethics: act only on principles you could will to be universal law.

Branch · Metaphysics · Transcendental Idealism

Conservatism · Britain

Edmund Burke

1729 – 1797

Society is indeed a contract.

But not the kind we sign and tear up at will. Society, said Burke, is a partnership between the living, the dead, and those yet to be born. Tradition carries the slow accumulated wisdom of generations. Reform — yes; revolution — no. The intellectual father of modern conservatism.

Branch · Political Philosophy

Utilitarianism · England

Jeremy Bentham

1748 – 1832

The greatest happiness for the greatest number.

A simple and radical principle for ethics and law: every action, every policy, should be judged by how much pleasure it produces and how much pain it removes — for everyone affected, counted equally. The poet matters no more, in this reckoning, than the pin-maker.

Branch · Ethics · Utilitarianism

Feminism · England

Mary Wollstonecraft

1759 – 1797

Mind has no gender.

Give women the same education as men, Wollstonecraft argued, and they will display the same virtues, the same reason, the same capabilities. The apparent weakness of women is not nature's doing but society's. A founding text of feminist thought.

Branch · Political Philosophy · Feminism

German Idealism

Georg Hegel

1770 – 1831

Reality is a historical process.

History is not a chaos of accidents but the unfolding of Spirit (Geist) coming to know itself. Every idea contains its own contradiction (antithesis), and from the clash a richer synthesis emerges, which becomes the next thesis. This is the dialectic — the engine of thought, of history, and ultimately of reality itself. Hegel's grand vision would shape Marx, the existentialists, and almost every continental philosopher who came after.

Branch · Metaphysics · Idealism

Pessimism · Germany

Arthur Schopenhauer

1788 – 1860

Every man takes the limits of his own field of vision for the limits of the world.

Behind the world of appearance, said Schopenhauer, there is only blind Will — a hungry, restless striving that drives everything and is satisfied by nothing. Life oscillates between pain and boredom. The only relief lies in art, compassion, and the renunciation of desire — a Western echo of the Buddha's insight.

Branch · Metaphysics · Idealism

Existentialism's First Voice · Denmark

Søren Kierkegaard

1813 – 1855

Anxiety is the dizziness of freedom.

Stand at the edge of a cliff. There are two fears: that you might fall — and the giddier, stranger fear that you might jump. That second fear is the truth of being human. We are radically free, and the realization of it makes us tremble. Faith, for Kierkegaard, is not the conclusion of an argument but a leap into the dark, taken alone.

Branch · Metaphysics · Existentialism

Communism · Germany

Karl Marx

1818 – 1883

The history of all hitherto existing society is the history of class struggles.

The motor of history, Marx argued, is not Spirit or ideas but economics — the conflict between those who own the means of production and those who must sell their labor to survive. Under capitalism, the worker is alienated from his work, his fellows, and himself. The system contains the contradictions that must eventually destroy it. A philosophy that, for better and for terrible worse, would remake the twentieth century.

Branch · Political Philosophy · Communism

Civil Disobedience · America

Henry David Thoreau

1817 – 1862

Must the citizen ever resign his conscience to the legislator?

When the law commands you to do what your conscience forbids, Thoreau answered: disobey. Refuse, peacefully and openly, and accept the consequences. An essay that would inspire Gandhi, Martin Luther King, Jr., and every non-violent movement since.

Branch · Political Philosophy

Liberalism · England

John Stuart Mill

1806 – 1873

Over his own body and mind, the individual is sovereign.

Mill softened Bentham's utilitarianism with a regard for the quality, not just quantity, of pleasures — better, he wrote, to be Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied. And he set out the "harm principle" that still anchors liberal thought: the state may restrict individual freedom only to prevent harm to others. A lifelong campaigner, too, for the equality of women.

Branch · Political Philosophy · Utilitarianism

Pragmatism · America

William James

1842 – 1910

Act as if what you do makes a difference. It does.

Truth, said James, is what works. An idea is true insofar as it guides us reliably through experience; belief is not a passive mirroring of reality but an active participant in making the world. Pragmatism — practical, plural, democratic — America's first original contribution to philosophy.

Branch · Epistemology · Pragmatism

God is dead. God remains dead. And we have killed him. — Friedrich Nietzsche
1900 — 1950

The Modern World

✦

Two world wars, the death of God, the rise of physics and psychoanalysis. Philosophy splits: in Britain and America, the cool precision of logical and linguistic analysis; on the Continent, the passionate search for meaning in a universe without one.

Existentialism's Prophet · Germany

Friedrich Nietzsche

1844 – 1900

Man is something to be surpassed.

Christianity is finished. So is the philosophy that propped it up. The values we have inherited — humility, pity, the otherworldly — are weapons turned against life. Nietzsche called for a revaluation of all values, and for the Übermensch, the human being who creates meaning out of nothing rather than receiving it from on high. To affirm this life, in all its terror and joy, eternally — that is his measure of greatness.

Branch · Ethics · Existentialism

Semiotics · Switzerland

Ferdinand de Saussure

1857 – 1913

Every message is made of signs.

Language is not a list of names for things. It is a system of signs, each defined by its difference from the others. The relationship between a word and what it means is arbitrary — and meaning lives not in the world but in the structure of language itself. The starting point of modern linguistics and structuralism.

Branch · Philosophy of Language

Phenomenology · Germany

Edmund Husserl

1859 – 1938

Experience by itself is not science.

To put philosophy on truly secure foundations, Husserl proposed, we must bracket out every assumption — including the assumption that an external world exists — and describe experience purely as it appears. This is phenomenology, and from it would grow a whole continental tradition.

Branch · Ontology · Phenomenology

Vitalism · France

Henri Bergson

1859 – 1941

Intuition goes in the very direction of life.

Time, for Bergson, is not the clock-time of physics but duration — the felt, unbroken flow of inner experience. The intellect chops the world into pieces; intuition grasps its living movement. Reality is not a sequence of states but a creative becoming.

Branch · Epistemology · Vitalism

Pragmatism · America

John Dewey

1859 – 1952

We only think when we are confronted with problems.

Philosophy is not the contemplation of eternal truths from a spectator's seat. It is the practical activity of beings trying to live well in a changing world. Thought begins in difficulty, and succeeds insofar as it helps us act. A philosophy of democracy, education, and engaged citizenship.

Branch · Epistemology · Pragmatism

Analytic Philosophy · Britain

Bertrand Russell

1872 – 1970

The road to happiness lies in an organized diminution of work.

A mathematician, logician, pacifist, and social radical. Russell helped found the analytic tradition by applying mathematical logic to language, and he campaigned tirelessly against war and for the kind of life — leisured, curious, kind — that he thought civilization owed its people.

Branch · Ethics · Analytic Philosophy

Spain

José Ortega y Gasset

1883 – 1955

Life is a series of collisions with the future.

We are not separate from our circumstances; we are made by them and remake them through our choices. To live is to project ourselves into possibilities — and to collide, again and again, with a world that resists.

Branch · Ontology · Existentialism

The Limits of Language · Vienna

Ludwig Wittgenstein

1889 – 1951

The limits of my language are the limits of my world.

The young Wittgenstein believed that language pictures the world, and that anything which cannot be expressed in clear propositions is, strictly, nonsense. Most of philosophy, he concluded, falls into that category — and we should pass over it in silence. The older Wittgenstein then changed his mind: language is not a single mirror but a wild forest of "language games," and meaning is use, embedded in forms of life. Twice the most influential philosopher of his age.

Branch · Philosophy of Language · Logic

Being and Time · Germany

Martin Heidegger

1889 – 1976

We are ourselves the entities to be analyzed.

What does it mean to be? Twenty-five centuries of metaphysics, said Heidegger, have forgotten the question. To recover it, we must look not at abstract being-in-general but at the being for whom being is an issue — ourselves. We are thrown into a world, immersed in projects, and bounded by the one unsurpassable horizon: death. To live authentically is to face this finitude and choose accordingly.

Branch · Ontology · Phenomenology

Philosophy of Science · Vienna / London

Karl Popper

1902 – 1994

A scientific statement must be falsifiable.

No number of observations can ever prove a scientific theory — but a single contrary observation can refute it. What distinguishes science from pseudo-science is not certainty but the courage to be wrong. A theory that cannot in principle be falsified is not a scientific theory at all.

Branch · Philosophy of Science

Critical Theory · Frankfurt

Theodor Adorno

1903 – 1969

Intelligence is a moral category.

To act well requires both feeling and understanding. The mass culture industry of the modern age erodes both — replacing intelligence with distraction, and emotion with stereotype. We do not just lose pleasure; we lose the capacity for moral judgment itself.

Branch · Ethics · Frankfurt School

Existentialism · France

Jean-Paul Sartre

1905 – 1980

Existence precedes essence.

A paper-knife is made for a purpose; its essence comes before its existence. Human beings are not. We come into the world without predetermined nature or meaning — and then we have to make ourselves through our choices. This freedom is total, terrifying, and inescapable. Sartre called us "condemned to be free": responsible not only for who we are but, by example, for the image of humanity we project.

Branch · Ethics · Existentialism

Political Philosophy · Germany / America

Hannah Arendt

1906 – 1975

The banality of evil.

Watching Adolf Eichmann on trial in Jerusalem, Arendt was struck not by monstrousness but by ordinariness. The architects of the Holocaust were not demons; they were bureaucrats who had stopped thinking. Evil, she warned, is rarely radical — it is most dangerous when it is dull, dutiful, and unreflective.

Branch · Ethics · Existentialism

Ethics of the Other · France

Emmanuel Levinas

1906 – 1995

Reason lives in language.

Before any spoken word, the face of another human being addresses us and calls us to responsibility. Ethics is not a system we deduce; it is the primary fact of human existence, prior to philosophy itself.

Branch · Ethics · Phenomenology

Perception · France

Maurice Merleau-Ponty

1908 – 1961

In order to see the world we must break with our familiar acceptance of it.

We are not minds peering out from the windows of bodies. We are embodied beings, perceiving with our whole flesh, woven into the world we perceive. To philosophize is to look freshly at ordinary experience — and discover how strange it is.

Existential Feminism · France

Simone de Beauvoir

1908 – 1986

Man is defined as a human being and woman as a female.

One is not born, but rather becomes, a woman. The standard measure of "humanity," de Beauvoir showed, has been silently masculine — and woman has been defined throughout history as the Other against this standard. To be truly free, women must reject both this casting and the passivity it teaches, and live the authentic existence available to any conscious being.

Philosophy of Language · America

Willard Van Orman Quine

1908 – 2000

Language is a social art.

Imagine trying to translate the word "gavagai" used by speakers of an unknown language while pointing at a rabbit. Does it mean rabbit? Rabbit-stage? Set of rabbit-parts? You can never be sure. Meaning is never a private link between words and things — it is shaped by the whole social practice of using a language.

Liberty · Latvia / Britain

Isaiah Berlin

1909 – 1997

The fundamental sense of freedom is freedom from chains.

Berlin distinguished negative liberty — freedom from interference — from positive liberty: the freedom to be one's own master, to realize one's purposes. Both matter; but the dream of forcing people to be free, of imposing positive liberty from above, has been the great tragedy of modern politics.

Deep Ecology · Norway

Arne Naess

1912 – 2009

Think like a mountain.

Naess called for a deep ecology that sees human beings not as masters of nature but as members of it. To think like a mountain is to identify our self with the whole biosphere — and to recognize the intrinsic worth of every living thing.

The Absurd · Algeria / France

Albert Camus

1913 – 1960

Life will be lived all the better if it has no meaning.

Sisyphus was condemned by the gods to roll a boulder up a hill, watch it roll back down, and begin again, forever. Camus chose him as a hero. The universe is silent; it gives us no meaning. The honest response is neither suicide nor false hope but revolt: to embrace the absurdity, refuse the consolations, and live — fully, lucidly, defiantly. We must imagine Sisyphus happy.

Semiotics · France

Roland Barthes

1915 – 1980

Language is a skin.

Even our most apparently neutral language carries the trembling of desire. To speak is to address someone — even if only a phantom — and to brush our words against another. A literary philosopher of love, myth, and the small signs of everyday life.

Philosophy of Science · America

Thomas Kuhn

1922 – 1996

Normal science does not aim at novelties of fact or theory.

Science, Kuhn argued, does not advance by steady accumulation. It works for long stretches within a "paradigm" — and then, when anomalies pile up, undergoes a revolution that replaces the paradigm entirely. The word has become inescapable; the idea has remade our picture of how knowledge grows.

Justice · America

John Rawls

1921 – 2002

The principles of justice are chosen behind a veil of ignorance.

Imagine you must design the rules of a society without knowing in advance who you will be in it — rich or poor, talented or not, your race, your sex, your beliefs. Behind this "veil of ignorance," what rules would you rationally choose? Whatever they are, Rawls argued, those are the rules of a just society — fair because no one is allowed to skew them in their own favor.

Anarchism of Method · Austria

Paul Feyerabend

1924 – 1994

Anything goes.

Look at how science has actually progressed, said Feyerabend, and you find no single method — only a glorious anarchy of approaches, guesses, and rule-breaking. Insistence on one official "scientific method" hinders the inquiry it pretends to protect.

Postmodernism · France

Jean-François Lyotard

1924 – 1998

Knowledge is produced to be sold.

In the age of the computer, knowledge has been transformed into information — stored, traded, commodified. The grand narratives that once organized our understanding — Progress, Reason, History — have lost their hold. To be postmodern, Lyotard wrote, is to have incredulity toward such meta-narratives.

Post-Colonial Thought · Martinique

Frantz Fanon

1925 – 1961

For the black man, there is only one destiny. And it is white.

A psychiatrist as well as a philosopher, Fanon dissected the inner damage of colonialism: how the colonized are taught to measure themselves against a culture that despises them, and to aspire to an existence that is forever closed. Liberation, for Fanon, must be psychological as well as political — and must end in the recognition of full and equal humanity for all.

Power and Discourse · France

Michel Foucault

1926 – 1984

Man is an invention of recent date.

The categories we take as natural and eternal — "madness," "sexuality," even "man" himself — are, Foucault argued, historical products, shaped by structures of power and knowledge. His "archaeologies" of the human sciences expose how our most basic concepts of self are constructed, and may, like a face drawn in sand, one day be erased.

Linguistics & Politics · America

Noam Chomsky

1928 –

If we choose, we can live in a world of comforting illusion.

The state's most powerful tool is not force but the manufacture of consent. Chomsky asks one demanding question of his readers: apply to your own government the same standards you would apply to others. The principle of universality — and a relentless test of public rhetoric against actual behavior.

Critical Theory · Germany

Jürgen Habermas

1929 –

Society is dependent upon a criticism of its own traditions.

A healthy democracy needs a "public sphere" — independent of state and market — where citizens can reason together, justify themselves to each other, and bring tradition under examination. Where that sphere is captured by money or power, democracy withers.

Deconstruction · France

Jacques Derrida

1930 – 2004

There is nothing outside of the text.

Every text — every utterance, every system of thought — contains gaps, contradictions, and silences. Meaning is never fully present; it is always deferred, defined by what is excluded as much as by what is said. To read deconstructively is to follow these threads patiently, opening texts up to the questions they could not quite admit they were asking.

Neopragmatism · America

Richard Rorty

1931 – 2007

There is nothing deep down inside us except what we have put there ourselves.

Philosophy has long imagined the mind as a mirror trying to reflect Reality. Rorty asked us to give up the picture. Knowledge is not a matter of mirroring but of conversation — of what our community lets us say. Without absolute foundations we still have plenty: loyalty to one another, hope for a better future, and solidarity in the dark.

Feminism · France

Luce Irigaray

1932 –

Every desire has a relation to madness.

Beneath even the soberest-sounding philosophy, Irigaray argued, a desire simmers — and the dominant philosophical tradition has taken male desire and male perspective as if they were universal. The task is to find authentically female ways of speaking, thinking, and being.

Post-Colonial Theory · Palestine / America

Edward Said

1935 – 2003

Every empire tells itself and the world that it is unlike all other empires.

Empires plunder, dominate, and exploit — but always in the name of civilization, progress, or freedom. Said's Orientalism exposed how scholarly knowledge has served the imperial project, and called us to be wary of every state that claims its interventions abroad are uniquely benevolent.

Feminism · France

Hélène Cixous

1937 –

Thought has always worked by opposition.

Culture/nature, day/night, head/heart, active/passive — Western thought pairs the world in oppositions, and one side is always coded male and dominant, the other female and subordinate. Feminist writing, Cixous urged, must dig under these structures and undo them.

Sage Philosophy · Kenya

Henry Odera Oruka

1944 – 1995

Philosophy is not only a written enterprise.

Africa has been mistakenly assumed to lack philosophical traditions because so much of its wisdom is oral. Oruka traveled into Kenyan villages, recorded conversations with acknowledged sages, and showed that systematic, critical thought lives there too — and deserves a place in the global story of philosophy.

Animal Ethics · Australia

Peter Singer

1946 –

In suffering, the animals are our equals.

Pain is pain, whoever feels it. If we count human suffering in our moral arithmetic, consistency demands that we count animal suffering too. Singer's utilitarian case for the rights of non-human animals launched a global movement.

Marxism · Slovenia

Slavoj Žižek

1949 –

All the best Marxist analyses are always analyses of a failure.

The political left is endlessly fascinated by what would have happened, if only — the moment of revolution, the road not taken, the utopia that almost was. But to mourn failures, Žižek warns, is also a way of not having to test our ideas against the world. A provocateur who insists that we keep asking, seriously, what another kind of society would actually look like.
