Chapters 33–35
The core idea

A game is a problem-solving activity, approached with a playful attitude.

That definition — arrived at after a careful chase through toys, play, fun, and games — sets the agenda for everything that follows. Game designers don't really make games. They make experiences. Games are the machines that produce them.

We cannot create experiences directly. We create artifacts — rule sets, game boards, computer programs — and cross our fingers that the experience that takes place during interaction is something the player will enjoy. Jesse Schell
Section 01 · Chapters 1–6

Foundations

Everything begins with the designer. Before mechanics or technology, before pitches or playtests, there is a person who decides to make a thing — and an audience whose experience that thing exists to serve.

Chapter 1 — In the Beginning, There Is the Designer

If you want to become a game designer, the answer is almost embarrassingly simple: design games. Start now. Don't even finish this sentence. Go.

For many would-be designers, the trouble isn't knowing what to do — it's a crisis of confidence. They feel stuck in a catch-22: only game designers can design games, but you only become a game designer by designing them. The way out is just as simple. Say these words:

I am a game designer.

Say them out loud. Say them like a chant. You may not feel like one yet — but as we'll see, people become what they pretend to be. Doubt will plague you forever. When you're a novice, you'll think you don't know what you're doing. When you're seasoned, you'll think you've lost your touch. The work continues regardless.

If you aren't dropping, you aren't learning. And if you aren't learning, you aren't a juggler.A saying among jugglers

What skills does a game designer need?

In short — all of them. Almost anything you can be good at can become useful: animation, anthropology, architecture, brainstorming, business, cinematography, communication, creative writing, economics, engineering, games of every kind, history, management, mathematics, music, psychology, public speaking, sound design, technical writing, visual arts.

No one masters all of these. But the more of them you are comfortable working with, however imperfectly, the better off you will be — for growth only happens when we exceed our limits.

The most important skill

Of all those skills, one stands above the rest. It sounds so simple that many people miss it. It isn't creativity. It isn't critical thinking. It isn't even communication.

It is listening.

Not the surface kind. Deep listening. Listening that puts you off-balance, that accepts the possibility of upset. The kind described in Hesse's Siddhartha: "To listen with a silent heart, with a waiting, open soul. Without passion, without desire, without judgment, without rebuke."

Designers must listen to five things:

  • The team — they collectively hold the skills you don't
  • The audience — they decide whether your game succeeded
  • The game — you must hear what is wrong with it as a mechanic listens to an engine
  • The client — they are paying, and they need to be understood
  • The self — the hardest of all, and the source of your true creativity
The secret of the gifted

There are two gifts. The minor gift is innate aptitude — design comes easily. The major gift is love of the work itself. People with only the minor gift rarely produce great things. People with the major gift will keep at it, and their skills will grow to match or exceed those of the naturally gifted. The only way to find out if you have it is to start down the path and see if it makes your heart sing.

Chapter 2 — The Designer Creates an Experience

A game designer's goal is not to design games. It is to create experiences. Games themselves are just artifacts — clumps of cardboard or bags of bits — worthless until played. What we care about is what happens in the mind of the player.

This is hard territory because experiences are too familiar. We can't see them, hold them, or share them. No two people have the same experience of the same thing. And yet, creating experiences is all we really do.

The game is not the experience. The game enables the experience, but it is not the experience.Jesse Schell

Three approaches to chasing rainbows

  • Psychology — the science of the mechanisms of the mind. Useful, though most modern psychology avoids the subjective experience we care about most.
  • Anthropology — especially cultural anthropology, which immerses itself in the lives of its subjects. We can do the same with our players.
  • Design — every other design discipline, from architecture to music, has lessons for us.

Essential experience

You can never replicate a real experience exactly. What you can do is capture its essence. A snowball-fight game doesn't need real snow — it needs the feel of cold, the chaos, the laughter, the friendly threat. Identify what makes the experience matter, then find as many ways as possible to deliver that essence through your game.

LENS #1
The Lens of Emotion

People may forget what you said, but they'll never forget how you made them feel. — Maya Angelou

  • What emotions would I like my player to experience? Why?
  • What emotions are players having when they play now? Why?
  • How can I bridge the gap between the two?
LENS #2
The Lens of Essential Experience

Stop thinking about your game. Start thinking about the experience of the player.

  • What experience do I want the player to have?
  • What is essential to that experience?
  • How can my game capture that essence?

Chapter 3 — The Experience Takes Place in a Venue

Every play experience happens somewhere. Technologies come and go — arcade, PC, console, handheld, mobile, tablet — but the venues in our lives are far more stable. Looking past the platform of the moment to the place where play actually happens reveals patterns that survive technological churn.

Private venues

  • The Hearth — once a fireplace, now a TV. A place where families gather. Good for games people can play together or enjoy watching.
  • The Workbench — a quiet, private place for focused, intense, often solitary work. PC gaming, MMOs, and VR live here.
  • The Reading Nook — a comfortable place to "lean back." Tablets thrive here, with games that are simpler and more relaxing.

Public venues

  • The Theater — many people focused on one performance. Games struggle here because interactivity dilutes with audience size.
  • The Arena — purpose-built fields of play, often paired with theaters. Physical sports' natural home; e-sports increasingly imitate it.
  • The Museum — a place we go to encounter the exotic. Arcades, zoos, and gallery installations all share this DNA.

Half-private, half-public

  • The Gaming Table — the intimate face-to-face venue of board, card, and dice games. So engrossing that the sandwich and the sushi roll were both invented by table-gamers who couldn't bear to stop.
  • The Playground — improvisational play in any open space. Largely the domain of children, mostly untouched by the videogame industry.
  • Anywhere — crosswords, Sudoku, and the entire smartphone market. Tiny bites, simple interfaces, played in spare moments.
LENS #3
The Lens of the Venue
  • What type of venue best suits the game I'm trying to create?
  • Does my venue have special properties that will influence my game?
  • What elements of my game are in harmony with my venue? What elements are not?

Chapter 4 — The Experience Rises Out of a Game

What is a game, exactly? Defining it is tricky. Schell's working definitions, distilled over many pages of careful analysis:

The Definitions

Fun is pleasure with surprises.

Play is manipulation that indulges curiosity.

A toy is an object you play with.

A good toy is an object that is fun to play with.

A game is a problem-solving activity, approached with a playful attitude.

LENS #4
The Lens of Surprise

Surprise is so basic that we forget about it. It is at the root of humor, strategy, and problem solving.

  • What will surprise players when they play my game?
  • Does the story have surprises? Do the rules? The artwork? The technology?
  • Do your rules let players surprise each other? Themselves?
LENS #5
The Lens of Fun
  • What parts of my game are fun? Why?
  • What parts need to be more fun?
LENS #6
The Lens of Curiosity

Think about the player's true motivations — not just the goals your game sets, but the reasons the player wants to achieve them.

  • What questions does my game put into the player's mind?
  • What am I doing to make them care about these questions?
  • What can I do to make them invent even more questions?
LENS #7
The Lens of Endogenous Value

A game's success hinges on the players' willingness to pretend it is important.

  • What is valuable to the players in my game?
  • How can I make it more valuable to them?
  • What is the relationship between value in the game and the players' motivations?
LENS #8
The Lens of Problem Solving
  • What problems does my game ask the player to solve?
  • Are there hidden problems that arise as part of gameplay?
  • How can my game generate new problems so players keep coming back?

Chapter 5 — The Game Consists of Elements

To do design, you must learn to see past the skin of a game (what players experience) and into its skeleton (the elements that produce that experience). Schell organizes those elements into four categories — the Elemental Tetrad:

Aesthetics

How the game looks, sounds, feels. The most visible quadrant; the most directly connected to player experience.

Story

The sequence of events that unfolds in your game — linear or branching, scripted or emergent.

Mechanics

The procedures and rules. What makes a game a game. Goals, constraints, and consequences.

Technology

The medium itself — paper, plastic chits, polygons. What enables and constrains everything else.

↑ More visible to players · less visible to players ↓

None of these elements is more important than the others. Each one powerfully influences each of the others. A change in one ripples into the rest. You must develop the rare double vision that sees both skin and skeleton at once — the experience and the elements that produce it.

LENS #9
The Lens of the Elemental Tetrad
  • Is my game design using elements of all four types?
  • Could my design be improved by enhancing elements in one or more of the categories?
  • Are the four elements in harmony, reinforcing each other and working together toward a common theme?
LENS #10
The Lens of Holographic Design

See everything in your game at once — the four elements and the player experience and how they interrelate.

  • What elements of the game make the experience enjoyable?
  • What elements detract from the experience?
  • How can I change game elements to improve the experience?

Chapter 6 — The Elements Support a Theme

To write a mighty book, you must choose a mighty theme.Herman Melville

The strongest games have a clear theme that every element reinforces. Two steps:

  1. Figure out what your theme is.
  2. Use every means possible to reinforce it.

Themes come in two flavors. Experience-based themes deliver an essential feeling ("the fantasy of being a pirate"). Truth-based themes express something we deeply believe ("love is more important than life"). The most powerful games combine both.

When the Disney VR team built Pirates of the Caribbean: Battle for the Buccaneer Gold, the breakthrough was realizing the ride wasn't about pirates — it was about being a pirate. Once they had that theme, every decision flowed from it: the shape of the CAVE, the metal cannons, the swaying motion platform, the ten-speaker audio, even the bags of fishing nets to hold purses. The strongest games are themed all the way down.

LENS #11
The Lens of Unification
  • What is my theme?
  • Am I using every means possible to reinforce that theme?
LENS #12
The Lens of Resonance

Look for hidden power. Resonant themes are buried inside us — and shake us when something causes them to ring.

  • What is it about my game that feels powerful and special?
  • When I describe my game to people, what ideas get them really excited?
  • If I had no constraints of any kind, what would this game be like?
  • I have certain instincts about how this game should be. What is driving those instincts?

Section 02 · Chapters 7–8

Process — Ideas & Iteration

The actual practice of game design: where ideas come from, why prototypes are throwaway, and the unbreakable Rule of the Loop.

Chapter 7 — The Game Begins with an Idea

You may worry that the method you used to design games — think of an idea, try it out, keep changing it until it's good — isn't the "real" method. Relax. That is the real method. The question is only how to do each step well.

Infinite inspiration

The most original work doesn't come from copying other people in your field. It comes from looking everywhere else — at ballet, at flocks of geese, at paper punch machines, at the messy life around you — and finding inspiration in things that nobody else thought to translate into game form.

LENS #13
The Lens of Infinite Inspiration

When you know how to listen, everybody is the guru. Stop looking at your game and at games like it. Look everywhere else.

  • What is an experience I have had in my life that I would want to share with others?
  • In what small way can I capture the essence of that experience and put it into my game?

State the problem

Don't fall in love with your solution. Fall in love with your problem.Anonymous

Most designers jump to solutions too fast. A clear problem statement opens a broader creative space, gives you a measurement of quality (does this solve the problem?), and makes collaboration easier. Notice that "how can I make a browser game teenagers will like?" is constraining — perhaps the question is really "how can I make a browser experience teenagers will like?"

LENS #14
The Lens of the Problem Statement
  • What problem, or problems, am I really trying to solve?
  • Have I been making assumptions about this game that have nothing to do with its true purpose?
  • Is a game really the best solution? Why?
  • How will I be able to tell if the problem is solved?

Your silent partner

Most good ideas don't arrive through reasoning. They surface from the subconscious — the same place dreams come from. Schell argues you should treat your subconscious like a separate person and learn to collaborate with it: pay attention to its hints, record its ideas, manage its appetites, and (above all) sleep. Most good ideas come on the other side of rest.

Five rules for your subconscious

1. Pay attention — if you ignore its suggestions, they stop coming. 2. Record your ideas — until they're written, they clutter your mind. 3. Manage its appetites judiciously — basic needs satisfied, it does its best work. 4. Sleep — the committee of sleep solves problems daylight can't. 5. Don't push too hard — looming over your subconscious slows it down.

Sixteen brainstorming tips (the short version)

  1. Write things down. Memory is terrible. Externalize.
  2. Pen or keyboard, whatever works. Unlined paper invites circles and arrows.
  3. Sketch. Even bad drawings spark ideas you wouldn't have had.
  4. Bring toys. Visual and tactile stimulation triggers creativity.
  5. Change your perspective. Stand on the chair. Go somewhere new.
  6. Immerse yourself. Become an anthropologist of your target audience.
  7. Crack jokes. Humor unlocks perspectives logic locks shut.
  8. Spare no expense. Don't let cheap materials inhibit million-dollar ideas.
  9. Use the walls. Big paper, sticky notes — get ideas out of your head and onto a surface you can walk around.
  10. The space remembers. Spatial memory is strong; lean on it.
  11. Write everything. Stupid ideas clear the way for good ones.
  12. Number your lists. Numbered things acquire dignity.
  13. Destroy your assumptions. List what you've assumed and ask: what if it weren't true?
  14. Mix and match categories. Lists of mechanics × story × aesthetic × tech yield surprising hybrids.
  15. Talk to yourself. Saying things aloud makes them real.
  16. Find a partner. Two minds bounce. Four is a meeting.

Chapter 8 — The Game Improves Through Iteration

Once you make a decision, the universe conspires to make it happen.Emerson

Pick an idea. Commit. The moment you commit, flaws and benefits become clear that weren't before. Be ready to reverse the decision when you realize it's wrong — ideas are paper cups, not china — but pick, and pick fast.

The eight filters

A finished design must pass through all eight. Change anything, and you have to re-run the whole gauntlet:

  1. Artistic impulse — does it feel right?
  2. Demographics — will the intended audience like it?
  3. Experience design — is it well-crafted under many lenses?
  4. Innovation — is it novel enough?
  5. Business and marketing — will it be profitable?
  6. Engineering — can it be built?
  7. Social / community — does it meet the social goals?
  8. Playtesting — do real players enjoy it?
LENS #15
The Lens of the Eight Filters

A design is only done when it can pass all eight tests without requiring change.

The Rule of the Loop

The Rule of the Loop

The more times you test and improve your design, the better your game will be. There are no exceptions.

This is an absolute truth, not a perspective. Faced with a long-term project, you must answer two questions:

  1. How can I make every loop count? → Assess risks; mitigate them.
  2. How can I loop as fast as possible? → Build many rough prototypes.

From waterfall to agile

The waterfall model was a fantasy of orderly steps. It violated the Rule of the Loop. Barry Boehm's spiral model (1986) put iteration at the center, and the 2001 Agile Manifesto codified it: working software over comprehensive documentation; responding to change over following a plan.

Risk mitigation by prototyping

Before writing code, list the risks: the unfun mechanic, the engine limit, the schedule risk, the publisher curveball. Then build the smallest possible prototype that tells you whether each risk is real. Cheap prototypes look ugly on purpose — polished ones hide problems and lull you into false security.

LENS #16
The Lens of Risk Mitigation

Because the sage always confronts difficulties, he never experiences them. — Tao Te Ching

  • What could keep this game from being great?
  • How can we stop that from happening?

Ten prototyping tips

  1. Answer a question. Every prototype exists to answer one.
  2. Forget quality. Polish wastes time and hides defects.
  3. Don't get attached. Plan to throw it away — you will anyway.
  4. Prioritize prototypes. Biggest risks first.
  5. Parallelize. Artists, engineers, designers can each prototype simultaneously.
  6. It doesn't have to be digital. Paper prototypes spot problems faster.
  7. It doesn't have to be interactive. Animatics and sketches can answer questions too.
  8. Pick a fast-loop engine. Languages that let you change code while the game runs are gold.
  9. Build the toy first. Make sure the core interaction is fun before designing a game around it.
  10. Seize opportunities for more loops. Schedule surprises can be a gift.
LENS #17
The Lens of the Toy

Stop thinking about whether your game is fun to play. Start thinking about whether it is fun to play with.

  • If my game had no goal, would it be fun at all?
  • When people see my game, do they want to start interacting with it even before they know what to do?

How much is enough?

The work is never finished, only abandoned. You must loop enough times to be proud, before you spend the whole budget. Mark Cerny's "Method" observes that preproduction continues until you have two publishable levels — at which point the project is ~30% spent, and the rest can finally be scheduled with confidence.

Two useful rules of thumb

The Plan-to-Cut Rule: design so that if 50% of your budget vanished, you'd still have a shippable game.

The 50% Rule: all core gameplay should be fully playable at the halfway point in your schedule. Plan to spend the second half polishing.

LENS #18
The Lens of Passion
  • Am I filled with blinding passion about how great this game will be?
  • If I've lost the passion, can I find it again?
  • If the passion isn't coming back, shouldn't I be doing something else?

Section 03 · Chapters 9–11

The Player

To create a great experience, you must know your audience better than they know themselves. Demographics, psychology, motivation, and the secret workings of the mind that hosts every play experience.

Chapter 9 — The Game Is Made for a Player

Einstein once accepted an invitation to lecture a small audience of older women on relativity. Looking out at the crowd, he made a different decision: he played them his violin instead. They cared less about physics and more about an intimate encounter with the famous Einstein. He knew his audience. So must you.

Project yourself

The best way to understand your audience is empathy. Walt Disney famously crouched while inspecting Disneyland — to see it the way a child would. You must project yourself into the mind of your player, watching with their eyes, feeling with their hands.

Age demographics, briefly

  • 0–3 — toys, not games. Touch interfaces are intuitive; abstract controllers are not.
  • 4–6 — first interest in simple games, often co-played with parents.
  • 7–9 — "the age of reason." Independent gameplaying begins.
  • 10–13 — "the age of obsession." Tweens dive deep into their interests.
  • 13–18 — teens experiment with identity through play.
  • 18–24 — young adults: time, money, and established tastes.
  • 25–35 — peak family formation; mostly casual play.
  • 35–50 — career and family; play often family-oriented.
  • 50+ — empty nesters; social games and old favorites.

Stereotypes as inclusion tools

Generalizations about gender, when handled with care, are tools of inclusion rather than exclusion. Knowing that certain play patterns are more common in different groups lets you design games that support multiple styles of play at once. The classic example: the family target-shooting game whose scoring unintentionally favored rapid-fire players until a second metric — accuracy — was added. Suddenly both styles felt rewarded.

LENS #19
The Lens of the Player
  • What do they like? What don't they like? Why?
  • What do they expect to see in a game?
  • If I were in their place, what would I want to see?
  • What would they like or dislike about my game in particular?

Psychographics & game pleasures

Marc LeBlanc's eight game pleasures: sensation, fantasy, narrative, challenge, fellowship, discovery, expression, submission. Richard Bartle's four player types — Achievers ♦, Explorers ♠, Socializers ♥, Killers ♣ — map onto a quadrant of acting/interacting vs. world/players. There are many more pleasures: anticipation, completion, schadenfreude, gift giving, humor, possibility, naches, surprise, thrill, fiero, wonder.

LENS #20
The Lens of Pleasure
  • What pleasures does your game give to players? Can these be improved?
  • What pleasures are missing from your experience? Why? Can they be added?

Chapter 10 — The Experience Is in the Player's Mind

The human mind is the most complex object in the known universe. Most of its workings are hidden from us. Game experiences happen entirely inside this black box.

Four powers that make play possible

  1. Modeling — the brain doesn't deal with reality; it deals with simplified models of reality. Games are predigested models.
  2. Focus — the brain's ability to pay attention to selected information. The pleasurable extreme is flow.
  3. Imagination — the everyday power to fill gaps. Tell someone "the mailman stole my car," and dozens of details appear unbidden.
  4. Empathy — projecting ourselves into another. We can do it with humans, with animated dogs, with stick figures.

Flow

Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi's well-known concept: when challenges meet skills just right, attention deepens, time falls away, and we feel fulfillment. Flow requires clear goals, no distractions, direct feedback, and a continuously calibrated challenge.

Skilled designers move the player up the channel in a "tense / release / tense / release" pattern — gradually raising the challenge, then granting a brief easier period, then raising it again.

LENS #21
The Lens of Flow
  • Does my game have clear goals? Are the player's goals the same goals I intended?
  • Does my game provide a steady stream of not-too-easy, not-too-hard challenges?
  • Are the player's skills improving at the rate I hoped?

Chapter 11 — The Player's Mind Is Driven by Motivation

Games are not important. The entire goal of entertainment is to take something unimportant and make it seem very important indeed.Jesse Schell

Maslow & needs

Maslow's hierarchy is a workable map for thinking about why games engage us. Most game activities live on the self-esteem tier (challenge, mastery), but multiplayer games reach down into love/belonging and safety — which is why they tend to hook deeper.

Self-determination theory

Edward Deci and Richard Ryan proposed three psychological needs:

  • Competence — I need to feel good at something.
  • Autonomy — I need freedom to do things my way.
  • Relatedness — I need to connect with other people.
LENS #22
The Lens of Needs
  • On which levels of Maslow's hierarchy is my game operating?
  • Does it fill the needs of competence, autonomy, and relatedness?
  • How can I make my game fill more basic needs than it already does?

Intrinsic vs. extrinsic motivation

Not a binary — a gradient. A famous study found that paying children to draw caused them to make more pictures of lower quality, and to stop drawing entirely when payment ended. Extrinsic rewards can drain intrinsic motivation.

Wanna vs. Hafta

Pleasure-seeking and pain-avoiding are different brain systems. Many "free-to-play" games drift from wanna to hafta over time — and as designer Sheri Graner Ray put it, "people don't just stop playing these games, they divorce them."

LENS #23
The Lens of Motivation
  • What motivations do players have to play my game?
  • Which motivations are internal? Which are external?
  • Which are pleasure-seeking? Which are pain-avoiding?
  • Which motivations support each other? Which are in conflict?

Novelty

Players want what is new. Novelty drives purchases and keeps people playing — especially novelty that makes them think in a new way. Portal's slogan said it best: "Now you're thinking with portals."

LENS #24
The Lens of Novelty
  • What is novel about my game?
  • Does my game have novelties throughout, or just at the beginning?
  • When the novelty wears off, will players still enjoy my game?
LENS #25
The Lens of Judgment

People don't hate being judged — they hate being judged unfairly. Games are excellent systems for fair, objective judgment.

  • What does your game judge about the players?
  • How does it communicate this judgment?
  • Do players feel the judgment is fair? Do they care?
  • Does the judgment make them want to improve?

Section 04 · Chapters 12–15

Mechanics

The skeleton of every game. Seven categories of element, plus the disciplines that hold them in balance: difficulty, choice, puzzles, and interface.

Chapter 12 — Some Elements Are Game Mechanics

Strip away aesthetics, story, and technology, and what remains are the mechanics: the interactions and relationships at the heart of the game. They fall into seven categories.

1. Space

Every game has a space — the "magic circle" where play happens. Spaces are discrete or continuous, have some number of dimensions, and contain bounded areas that may or may not be connected.

LENS #26
The Lens of Functional Space
  • Is the space of this game discrete or continuous?
  • How many dimensions does it have?
  • What are the boundaries? Are there subspaces? How are they connected?

2. Time

Time, like space, can be discrete (a turn) or continuous (real time). Games use clocks (absolute limits) and races (relative pressure). They can stop time, speed it up, rewind it.

LENS #27
The Lens of Time
  • What determines the length of my gameplay activities?
  • Are my players frustrated because the game ends too early? Bored because it goes too long?
  • Would clocks or races make my gameplay more exciting?

3. Objects, attributes, and states

Objects are the nouns of mechanics. Each object has attributes; each attribute has a state. State diagrams: every ghost in Pac-Man cycles through Chasing → Blue → Flashing → Eyes → In Cage.

LENS #28
The Lens of the State Machine
  • What are the objects in my game?
  • What are their attributes? What states can those attributes be in?
  • What triggers the state changes?

Secrets

Critical: who knows what? Public state, private state, state known only to the game, state known only to fate.

LENS #29
The Lens of Secrets
  • What is known by the game only?
  • What is known by all players? By only one?
  • Would changing who knows what improve the game?

4. Actions

Actions are the verbs. Two kinds: basic actions (the moves the rules permit) and strategic actions (the meaningful plays that emerge). An elegant game has a small number of basic actions and a large number of strategic ones.

Five ways to encourage emergent gameplay:

  1. Add more verbs.
  2. Make verbs that can act on many objects.
  3. Allow goals to be achieved more than one way.
  4. Provide many subjects to control.
  5. Make side effects change constraints.
LENS #30
The Lens of Emergence
  • How many verbs do my players have?
  • How many objects can each verb act on?
  • How many ways can players achieve their goals?
LENS #31
The Lens of Action
  • What are the basic actions in my game?
  • What are the strategic actions?

The most fundamental mechanic. Above all, every game has one top rule: its goal. Good goals are concrete, achievable, and rewarding.

6. Skill

7. Chance

Probability theory was literally invented to balance a gambling problem (the Chevalier de Méré's lost bets in 1654). Designers use expected value — the average outcome of a decision — to balance options. But beware: players don't choose only by expected value. Regret is a real cost.

A false balance is an abomination to the Lord.Proverbs 11:1

The twelve balances

  1. Fairness — symmetrical games are fair by construction; asymmetrical games require careful tuning.
  2. Challenge vs. success — keep the player in the flow channel.
  3. Meaningful choices — beware of dominant strategies and meaningless choices.
  4. Triangularity — a low-risk/low-reward option vs. a high-risk/high-reward one. Space Invaders' flying saucer.
  5. Skill vs. chance.
  6. Head vs. hands.
  7. Competition vs. cooperation.
  8. Short vs. long.
  9. Rewards — variable rewards beat fixed ones.
  10. Punishment — used delicately, creates endogenous value.
  11. Freedom vs. controlled experience.
  12. Simple vs. complex — aim for elegance.

Chapter 17 — Story

Two methods that actually work

  • The String of Pearls. A noninteractive story (the string) punctuated by stretches of free play (the pearls).
  • The Story Machine. Build rules that generate stories from play. Baseball, golf, The Sims, Minecraft.
LENS #73
The Lens of the Story Machine
  • How can I give players choices that create different stories?
  • How can I let players personalize the story?
  • Do my rules lead to stories with good interest curves?

Five problems that block branching narrative

  1. Good stories have unity. Cinderella's beginning only works with Cinderella's ending.
  2. Combinatorial explosion. Ten depth-three branches = 88,573 outcomes.
  3. Multiple endings disappoint.
  4. Not enough verbs. Game characters run and shoot; story characters talk and plead.
  5. Time travel makes tragedy obsolete.

The Story Stack

From least flexible to most flexible: Fantasy → Action → Economy → World → Story. Story is the servant; it must never become the master.

Eleven story tips

  1. Respect the story stack.
  2. Put your story to work — it can solve technical problems for free.
  3. Goals, obstacles, and conflicts.
  4. Make it real to you, or it won't be real to them.
  5. Provide simplicity and transcendence.
  6. Consider the Hero's Journey.
  7. Keep your story world consistent.
  8. Make your story world accessible.
  9. Use clichés judiciously.
  10. Sometimes a map brings a story to life. Treasure Island began as a drawing.
  11. Surprise and emotion.

Chapter 18 — Indirect Control

We don't need to give players real freedom — we need to give them the feeling of freedom.

Six techniques of indirect control:

  1. Constraints. Two doors instead of an open field.
  2. Goals. A player given the goal "find the bananas" will go where the bananas appear to be.
  3. Interface. A plastic guitar silently rules out a thousand actions.
  4. Visual design. The Disney castle visible from Main Street. The single red line painted on the floor of Aladdin's throne room that no one consciously remembers following.
  5. Characters. If you make players care about an NPC, you can make them do almost anything.
  6. Music. Restaurants speed up music at lunch and slow it at three.

Chapter 22 — Presence

VR and AR are not technologies for fooling the eye. They are technologies for fooling the body — or, more precisely, for fooling the mind's sense of where the body is. That sense is called proprioception.

Six presence breakers

  1. Motion sickness. Keep framerate ≥ 60fps. Avoid virtual camera motion. Don't accelerate. Hide edges. Keep the horizon level.
  2. Counter-intuitive interactions.
  3. Intensity overload.
  4. Unrealistic audio.
  5. Proprioceptive disconnect.
  6. Lack of identity.

Six presence builders

  1. Hand presence. The strongest variety.
  2. Social presence.
  3. Familiarity.
  4. Realistic audio.
  5. Proprioceptive alignment.
  6. Comedy. A comedic world tolerates more strangeness.
LENS #93½

  • What in my game is diminishing or breaking presence?

Chapter 23 — Aesthetics

Game designers who dismiss aesthetics as surface miss the point. Good artwork draws players in, makes the world feel real, satisfies a basic pleasure of sensation, builds atmosphere.

Learn to see

The brain's labeling instinct slaps a name on what it sees so it can move on. To make great visual experiences, you must override it — really see shapes, proportions, shadows, relationships.

Let aesthetics guide design

Some studios delay involving artists until the game is "designed." This is backward. Concept art, especially at the start, can completely change a project's direction.

How much detail?

Just enough to be wonderful, no more. J.R.R. Tolkien used "distant mountains" — names of places never visited — to make Middle-earth feel vast. Cinderella's Castle is gorgeous from far away and close up.

Use audio

One study showed that improving audio quality made players rate the graphics higher. Pick music as early as possible. The right piece will guide a thousand later decisions.

The Lens of Atmosphere

Man is a social animal. Humans generally avoid being alone whenever possible.Jesse Schell

The Team

Game design is rarely a solo activity. The skills of working together — loving the work, communicating clearly, documenting honestly, and playtesting fearlessly — matter as much as the design itself.

Chapter 26 — The Designer Usually Works with a Team

The secret

The secret of successful teamwork is love. Not love between teammates. Love of the game you are making together, and the audience you are making it for.

Three love problems break teams:

  3. Members in love with different visions of the same game. Talk it out, hear them out, find shared ground.

The "magic template" for a game design document does not exist. Documents are theories about what your game might be — written, they age the moment they're complete.

Two purposes only: memory and communication. The right documents for your project are determined by what needs to be remembered and what needs to be communicated.

Chapter 28 — Playtesting

WUBALEW

How often should you playtest? When useful, but at least every week.

The six questions of any playtest

Why? Every playtest answers specific questions. Who? Match the testers to your questions. When? Earlier than you think. Where? Studio, lab, public venue, player's home, online. What? The things you know you're looking for — and the surprises. How? Be there if you can be objective.

FFWWDD — six post-game questions

  1. F — Frustrating. "What was the most frustrating moment or aspect?"
  2. F — Favorite. "What was your favorite moment or aspect?"
  3. W — Wanted. "Was there anything you wanted to do that you couldn't?"
  4. W — Wand. "If you had a magic wand to change anything, what would it be?"
  5. D — Doing. "What were you doing in the experience?"
  6. D — Describe. "How would you describe this game to your friends and family?"

The Business

Technology, clients, pitches, profit. The forces that surround your design and shape it from the outside — and how to negotiate them while keeping your vision intact.

Chapter 29 — Technology

Foundational vs. decorational

The cake of a cupcake is foundational — without it, no cupcake. The icing is decorational. Steamboat Willie is built on synchronized sound. Sonic the Hedgehog was built on the Genesis's scrolling. Ico turned ragdoll physics — usually decorational — into the foundation of a love story.

The Hype Cycle

Every new technology rides Gartner's curve: technology trigger → peak of inflated expectations → trough of disillusionment → slope of enlightenment → plateau of productivity.

The Innovator's Dilemma

Companies fail by listening to their existing customers. New technologies look "not good enough" — until suddenly they aren't.

The Law of Divergence

Technologies don't converge. They split. The number of remotes in your living room only goes up. The exception is the pocket.

Chapter 30 — The Client

Form follows funding.Bran Ferren

Coping with bad suggestions

When the client says "these cars need more chrome," the wrong responses are agreeing reflexively or arguing reflexively. The right response is to understand the problem they're trying to solve.

Three layers of desire

Every client says one thing with their words, thinks another in their mind, and feels a third in their heart. Listen to all three.

When the mayor of Florence told Michelangelo the nose of his David was too big, Michelangelo invited the mayor up the scaffold to "fix it together," scooped some marble dust into his hand, faked a few chisel taps, and dropped the dust. "I like it better." The deeper layer of desire was creative ownership — and Michelangelo gave it to him without compromising the work.

Chapter 32 — Profit

Follow the money

To understand a business model, follow a dollar. In retail, a $50 game's average breakdown: retailer $16, markdown reserve $3, platform holder $7, advertising $6, disk and box $8, publisher $10, developer $3. Free-to-play moves the math entirely: revenue equals lifetime value × users, and you must spend on customer acquisition without exceeding LTV.

Learn the language

SKU, COGS, burn rate, sold-in vs. sold-through, breakeven. For free-to-play: churn, cost of acquisition, DAU, MAU, ARPU, ARPPU, LTV, K-factor, whale.

The importance of barriers

The iOS gold rush collapsed prices to zero. About 90% of iOS games lose money. The slot machine industry, by contrast, has fewer competitors — about 90% of slot games are profitable. Barriers make markets profitable.

Section 09 · Chapters 33–35

Impact

Games are not trivial amusements. They change the people who play them. The designer's responsibilities, the danger and the promise of the medium, and the question only you can answer.

Chapter 33 — Games Transform Their Players

What games can do for us

  • Emotional maintenance — venting, cheering up, gaining perspective, building confidence.
  • Connecting — a social bridge with shared topics and memories.
  • Exercise — physical and mental.
  • Education — though with constraints.

Why games are powerful learning tools

  • Visible progress.
  • Making the abstract concrete.
  • Full engagement.
  • Lots of small goals.
  • Conveying systems of relationships — the thing games do best.
  • Creating teachable moments.
  • Cultivating curiosity — possibly the most valuable mental asset in the twenty-first century.

Can games be bad for us?

Two real concerns: violence and addiction. On violence: most players are good at distinguishing fantasy from real action — but rating systems exist for a reason. On addiction: real, especially for MMOs. Shigeru Miyamoto signs autographs for children with: "On a sunny day, play outside."

Chapter 34 — Responsibility

There is only one person

Only one person can take responsibility for the games you make. That person is you.

Corporations have legal responsibility, but not ethical responsibility — they have no souls. Only individuals can take ethical responsibility.

In 1922, Rudyard Kipling designed a ritual for graduating engineers — an iron ring placed on the pinky of the dominant hand, a lifelong reminder of obligation. The pinky, the smallest finger, secretly guides the hand. Take the invisible ring. It doesn't come off.

LENS #14
The Problem Statement

What problem am I really trying to solve? Is a game the best solution? How will I know if it's solved?

LENS #15
The Eight Filters

Artistic, demographic, experience, innovation, business, engineering, social, playtesting. Pass all eight without change, and you're done.

LENS #16
Risk Mitigation

What could keep this game from being great? How can we stop that from happening?

LENS #17
The Toy

If my game had no goal, would it still be fun to play with? Do people want to touch and manipulate it before knowing what to do?

LENS #18
Passion

Am I filled with blinding passion about how great this game will be? If I've lost it, can I find it again?

LENS #19
The Player

Stop thinking about your game. Start thinking about your player. What do they like? What don't they like?

LENS #20
Pleasure

What pleasures does my game give? What pleasures are missing? Sensation, fantasy, narrative, challenge, fellowship, discovery, expression, submission.

LENS #21
Flow

Does my game have clear goals? Does it provide a steady stream of not-too-easy, not-too-hard challenges?

LENS #22
Needs

On which Maslow levels is my game operating? Does it fill competence, autonomy, and relatedness?

LENS #23
Motivation

What motivations drive play? Internal or external? Pleasure-seeking or pain-avoiding? Which support each other, which conflict?

LENS #24
Novelty

What is novel about my game? Is the novelty just at the beginning? When it wears off, will players still care?

LENS #25
Judgment

What does your game judge about the players? How does it communicate this? Do players feel the judgment is fair?

LENS #26
Functional Space

Is the space discrete or continuous? How many dimensions? What are the boundaries and subspaces?

LENS #27
Time

What determines the length of gameplay activities? Frustrated by ending too early, bored by going too long? Would clocks or races help?

LENS #28
The State Machine

What are the objects, attributes, and states in my game? What triggers state changes?

LENS #29
Secrets

What is known by the game only? By all players? By only some? Would changing who knows what improve the game?

LENS #30
Emergence

How many verbs? How many objects each verb acts on? How many ways to achieve goals? How do side effects change constraints?

LENS #31
Action

What are the basic actions in my game? What are the strategic actions? What do players wish they could do but can't?

LENS #32
Goals

What is the ultimate goal? Is it clear? Are goals concrete, achievable, and rewarding? Good balance of short and long term?

LENS #33
Rules

Foundational vs. operational rules? Modes? Who enforces? Easy to understand, or in need of clarification?

LENS #34
Skill

What skills does my game require? Which are dominant? Can players improve them with practice?

LENS #35
Expected Value

What is the actual chance of an event? The perceived chance? What values do outcomes have? Are the choices interesting?

LENS #36
Chance

What in my game is truly random? What just feels random? Does randomness give players positive or negative feelings?

LENS #37
Fairness

Symmetrical or asymmetrical? Should the game reliably measure skill, or challenge everyone interestingly?

LENS #38
Challenge

What are the challenges? Too easy, too hard, or just right? How does difficulty increase? Is there variety?

LENS #39
Meaningful Choices

Are the choices meaningful? Right number of choices? Are there dominant strategies?

LENS #40
Triangularity

Do I offer a meaningful choice between low risk / low reward and high risk / high reward? Is it balanced?

LENS #41
Skill vs. Chance

Are players here to be judged (skill) or to take risks (chance)? Should chance enliven tedious parts?

LENS #42
Head and Hands

Mindless action or intellectual challenge? Where can the player relax their brain? Where do they get to outwit?

LENS #43
Competition

Does my game give a fair measurement of skill? Do players want to win? Is winning something to be proud of?

LENS #44
Cooperation

Do players have enough opportunity to communicate? Are tasks possible only together? Do they force communication?

LENS #45
Competition vs. Cooperation

Can I give players a choice between cooperative and competitive play? Does my audience prefer one, the other, or a mix?

LENS #46
Reward

What rewards is my game giving? Are players excited or bored by them? Are they variable, building, and related to one another?

LENS #47
Punishment

What are the punishments? Why am I punishing players? Do they seem fair? Could punishments become rewards?

LENS #48
Simplicity / Complexity

Innate complexity vs. emergent complexity. Is my design as simple as it can be while still producing interesting behavior?

LENS #49
Elegance

Count purposes of each element. Combine single-purpose ones. Can multi-purpose elements serve even more purposes?

LENS #50
Character

Is there anything strange that players talk about excitedly? Does the game have lovable quirks? Are there flaws people like?

LENS #51
Imagination

What must the player understand? Can imagination fill the gaps? What details inspire imagination? What stifles it?

LENS #52
Economy

How can players earn? How can they spend? Is money too easy or too hard to get? Are the choices meaningful?

LENS #53
Balance

Does my game feel right? Why or why not?

LENS #54
Accessibility

Will players know how to begin? Does my puzzle or game act like something they've seen before? Does it draw people in?

LENS #55
Visible Progress

What does it mean to make progress? Is there enough? Can I reveal hidden progress?

LENS #56
Parallelism

Are there bottlenecks? Can I offer parallel challenges? Are they different enough to refresh the player?

LENS #57
The Pyramid

Can all pieces of my puzzle feed into a singular climactic challenge? Is the final challenge interesting enough?

LENS #58
The Puzzle

What are the puzzles in my game? Should I have more or less? How do they integrate with the rest of the experience?

LENS #59
Control

Does the interface do what's expected? Is it easy to master? Do players feel powerful — that is, in control?

LENS #60
Physical Interface

What do players pick up and touch? How does it map to actions in the world? What metaphor is the interface using?

LENS #61
Virtual Interface

What information needs to reach the player beyond what they see in the world? When? How can it amplify their power?

LENS #62
Transparency

No matter how beautiful an interface, less is better. Is it simple enough to vanish with practice?

LENS #63
Feedback

What do players need to know at this moment? What do they want to know? How can feedback create the feeling I want?

LENS #64
Juiciness

Is my interface giving continuous feedback for actions? Is there satisfying second-order motion?

LENS #65
Primality

What parts of my game are so primal an animal could do them? What parts could be more primal?

LENS #66
Channels and Dimensions

What data travel between game and player? Which are most important? What channels and dimensions are available?

LENS #67
Modes

What modes do I need and why? Can any be collapsed? When the game changes modes, how does the player know?

LENS #67½
Metaphor

Is my interface already a metaphor for something else? Am I making the most of it — or is it getting in the way?

LENS #68
Moments

What are the key moments? How can I make each moment as powerful as possible?

LENS #69
The Interest Curve

How is the curve shaped? Does it have a hook, rising interest, rests, and a finale? Is the structure fractal?

LENS #70
Inherent Interest

What captures interest immediately? Does the game let players see or do something they have never done?

LENS #71
Beauty

How can each element be more beautiful? How can elements compose into something poetic?

LENS #72
Projection

What can players relate to? What captures their imagination? Do they get to be the character they could imagine being?

LENS #73
The Story Machine

Do my rules generate stories worth telling? Can players personalize them? Are there good interest curves in the stories that arise?

LENS #74
The Obstacle

What is the relationship between protagonist and goal? Who or what is in the way? Do obstacles increase in difficulty?

LENS #75
Simplicity and Transcendence

How is my world simpler than the real world? What transcendent power do I give the player without removing challenge?

LENS #76
The Hero's Journey

Does my story have elements that qualify it as a hero's journey? Would the story be improved by including more archetypal elements?

LENS #77
The Weirdest Thing

What is the weirdest thing in my story? Will it confuse the player? Are there too many weird things? Or none at all?

LENS #78
Story

Does my game need a story? Why? How does it support the other parts of the tetrad?

LENS #79
Freedom

When do players have freedom of action? Do they feel free? Are there places I can let them feel freer?

LENS #80
Help

Within the context of the game, who is the player helping? Can I make them feel more connected to characters who need help?

LENS #81
Indirect Control

Ideally, what would I like players to do? Can constraints, goals, interface, visuals, characters, or music guide them?

LENS #82
Collusion

What do I want the player to experience? How can the characters help fulfill it without compromising their goals?

LENS #83
Fantasy

What fantasy does my world fulfill? Who does the player fantasize about being? What about doing?

LENS #84
The World

How is my world better than the real world? Can there be multiple gateways? Could many stories happen here?

LENS #85
The Avatar

Is the avatar an ideal form players want to project into? Or iconic enough to be a blank slate?

LENS #86
Character Function

What roles do I need filled? Which characters fit which roles? Can any character fill more than one role?

LENS #87
Character Traits

What traits define my character? How do these traits manifest in words, actions, and appearance?

LENS #88
The Interpersonal Circumplex

Plot characters by friendliness and dominance. Are there gaps? Extreme characters? Are friends together or apart?

LENS #89
The Character Web

How does each character feel about each of the others? Are there gaps? Are too many connections similar?

LENS #90
Status

What are the relative status levels of the characters? How can they show appropriate status behaviors?

LENS #91
Character Transformation

How does each character change through the game? Is there enough change? Are the changes surprising and believable?

LENS #92
Inner Contradiction

What is the purpose of my game? Are there parts that contradict that purpose? If so, how can I change them?

LENS #93
The Nameless Quality

Does my design feel alive? Which of Alexander's fifteen properties does it have? Where does it feel like myself?

LENS #93½
Presence

Is my player experiencing presence? Could it be stronger? What is breaking it? What is building it?

LENS #94
Atmosphere

Without using words, how can I describe the atmosphere of my game? How can artistic content deepen it?

LENS #95
Spectation

Is my game interesting to watch? Why or why not? How can it be more spectator-worthy?

LENS #95½
Cheatability

Can players cheat? Will anyone notice? Do players trust my game?

LENS #96
Friendship

What kind of friendships are players looking for? How do they break the ice? Become friends? Stay friends?

LENS #97
Expression

How am I letting players express themselves? Are they proud of their identity?

LENS #98
Community

What conflict is at the heart of the community? Does architecture shape it? Are there events? Why do players need each other?

LENS #99
Griefing

What systems in my game are easy to grief? How can I make my game boring to grief? Am I ignoring any loopholes?

LENS #100
Love

Do I love my project? Does everyone on the team? If not, how can that change?

LENS #101
The Team

Is this the right team for this project? Is communication objective, clear, comfortable, trusted, honest, private, unified?

LENS #102
Documentation

What do we need to remember while making this game? What needs to be communicated?

LENS #103
Playtesting

Why are we testing? Who should be there? When, where, what for, and how?

LENS #104
Technology

What technologies deliver the experience I want? Foundational or decorational? Is there a disruptive technology to use instead?

LENS #105
The Crystal Ball

What will this technology be like in 2, 4, 10 years? Why? Concretely answered, this trains intuition about the future.

LENS #106
Utopia

Am I creating something that feels magical? Does my game advance the state of the art? Does it make the world better?

LENS #107
The Client

What does the client say they want? What do they think they want? What do they really want, deep down?

LENS #108
The Pitch

Why are you pitching this game to this client? What's in it for them? What do they need to know?

LENS #109
Profit

Where does the money go in my game's business model? Why? How much will it cost? How much will it make?

LENS #110
Transformation

How can my game change players for the better? How might it change them for the worse?

LENS #111
Responsibility

Does my game help people? How?

LENS #112
The Raven

Is making this game worth my time?

LENS #∞
Your Secret Purpose

Why am I doing this?
