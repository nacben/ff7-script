A close reading from a working game writer

The Wound and the Witness

A craft document on Final Fantasy VII's screenplay — its structural architecture, dialogue technique, choice systems, localization, foreshadowing inventory, and the principles other game writers can steal from it.

01 — Overview & Thesis

What kind of script is this, actually?

Final Fantasy VII is often discussed as a graphical landmark, a soundtrack, or a fandom phenomenon. Read purely as a screenplay, it is something stranger and more ambitious: a 30,000-word interrogation of an unreliable narrator embedded inside a populist eco-thriller, served through the conventions of a JRPG. The script's central trick — that the protagonist is lying to himself, the player, and the party — is established in the very first scene and not paid off until two thirds of the way through the runtime. Few mainstream narratives, in any medium, sustain a misdirection that long.

Working thesis FF7's screenplay is a study in narrated identity: a story whose dramatic engine is the gap between who a character claims to be and who they are remembered as. The entire ensemble — Cloud, Tifa, Aeris, Barret, Cid, Vincent, Reeve — is structured around versions of the same question. Memory, performance, and witness are the three vocabularies the script keeps returning to.

The three plots, stacked

The script attempts three things at once. They are not always in harmony, and the seams are part of the texture:

The political plot

Shinra Inc. as extractive capital. A reactor blows up in chapter one; the corporation drops a chunk of city onto its own slum and frames the bombers. The first eight hours are class-conscious in a way the genre rarely permits, with named workers and a vertical city whose architecture is the politics.

The personal plot

Cloud, an ex-elite mercenary, gradually discovers that his memories of being elite are fabricated. The script weaponizes the JRPG's reliance on flashback by giving us a long, vivid one in Act II that turns out to be a forgery — and then making the player, alongside Tifa, rebuild the true version inside the protagonist's mind.

The cosmic plot

A two-thousand-year-old wound in a living planet. Sephiroth is not the villain so much as the wound's voice. Holy and Meteor are not weapons; they are immune responses. The metaphysical scaffolding is heavy, but the script has the sense to let Bugenhagen — an old man humming into a hologram — deliver it instead of a chosen-one prophecy.

What makes it specifically a game script

This is the thing most prose criticism misses. The script is built around two conventions you cannot replicate in linear media:

  • Choice as confession. The flower-buying choice in Sector 8, the dress-up at Wall Market, the date selection at Gold Saucer, dozens of dialogue branches — none of them fork the plot. They fork the relationship texture. Aeris remembers whether you bought a flower; Tifa remembers whether you said "I worked hard for it"; the Highwind night sequence runs a different version depending on counters tracked since chapter one. The player isn't choosing what happens; they are composing how they were the kind of person who let it happen.
  • Mechanical performance as character. Cloud's swagger ("Don't worry about it. It's no big deal. Just what I always do.") is Cloud impersonating Zack. The player has been performing that swagger themselves, picking it from menus. When the truth lands at Mideel, the player is implicated. The script makes the input device part of the lie.

That is the level of formal ambition I want to take seriously below. Everything else — the localization, the date mechanics, the dialect, the off-screen deaths — is in service of a script that is doing things only a game can do.

02 — Structural Anatomy

Four acts, three discs, and a beat sheet for Disc 1

FF7 is technically a four-act screenplay disguised as three discs. Each act has a distinct dramatic question, a distinct geography, and a distinct relationship to truth.

ACT I

Banner of Revolution

Setting: Midgar, vertical city

Dramatic question: Will Cloud finish the job and walk away?

The opening act is a heist movie. It establishes the worldview through architecture: a plate floats above the slums; the rich live on the lid, the poor live in the shadow it casts. The script smartly refuses to lecture about this. It lets Barret yell and Cloud shrug — "It's not my problem" — and lets the player decide who to side with. The act ends with the literal collapse of that architecture (the Sector 7 plate) and the symbolic collapse of Cloud's detachment.

"It can't run anywhere except where its rails take it." — Cloud, on the train. The opening's central image and its central thesis.

ACT II

Pursuit

Setting: The world map

Dramatic question: Who is Sephiroth, and what does he want?

The act opens up geographically, and the script smartly opens up tonally with it: Wutai's farce, Gold Saucer's date sequence, Cosmo Canyon's lore lecture, Cid's profanity, Junon's military pageant. This is the act of red herrings — the player and Cloud chase a Sephiroth who is, in truth, a hallucination. The Nibelheim flashback at Kalm is the act's anchor: a 20-minute monologue that the rest of the game will spend dismantling.

It ends with the death of Aeris.

ACT III

Turmoil

Setting: The interior

Dramatic question: Who is Cloud?

The boldest act. The protagonist is removed from the party for hours of runtime; Tifa becomes the playable POV; the player learns the world's politics from inside Shinra HQ while Heidegger and Scarlet plot a public execution. The act climaxes inside Cloud's own mind in the Lifestream — a literalized therapy session, complete with a mosaic of remembered Clouds the player must walk between. It is the rare AAA narrative where the climax is an act of self-correction, not violence.

"I never lived up to being 'Cloud'." — Cloud's confession aboard the Highwind.

ACT IV

Decisive Battle

Setting: The wound

Dramatic question: Is it worth fighting for a world that may already be lost?

An unusually contemplative final act. Shinra collapses off-stage. The Sister Ray sequence kills the corporate antagonist before the metaphysical one. The script then deliberately slows down: each party member is sent home to find their own reason. The final boss is preceded not by escalation but by a campfire conversation. "There ain't no gettin' offa this train we on" returns as a benediction.

A working beat sheet for Disc 1

What follows is how I would brief a junior writer on the dramatic function of each Disc 1 sequence. The "function" column is what the scene does structurally, separate from what happens in it. This is the level of analysis I find most useful when teaching FF7.

1.01 No. 1 Reactor bombing Establishes voices, establishes Cloud's detachment, establishes that the script will treat extras (Biggs, Wedge, Jessie) as people. The "Watch out! This isn't just a reactor!!" intrusion plants the unreliable-narrator engine in the first ten minutes.
1.02 Flower girl, soldier evasion, train Introduces the optional purchase system and Aeris in three lines. The train ride functions as a train ride functions in any heist movie — recovery, exposition, motif planting ("rails it can't get off").
1.03 7th Heaven / meeting Tifa & Marlene Establishes the "promise" that will pay off later as both romance and identity proof. Marlene's flower acceptance routes a flag that the script will silently track for the rest of Disc 1.
1.04 No. 5 Reactor / Air Buster / fall Cliffhanger one. Separates Cloud from the team. Functions as a forcing mechanism for the Aeris meet-cute and as a structural reset before the Wall Market interlude.
1.05 Sector 5 church / Reno chase The first time the script slows down. A whole sequence built on Aeris's pace. The barrel-jumping minigame is a smart choice: it makes the player participate in protecting her, which the back half of Act II will need them to remember.
1.06 Wall Market & Don Corneo Pure farce, but doing essential prep work. Cloud is forced to wear someone else's clothes. Foreshadows the larger costume he has been wearing all along. The Corneo answer-game ("Why do bad guys talk?") foreshadows the same structure two acts later when Cloud forces Corneo's confession.
1.07 Sector 7 pillar collapse First permanent loss. Three named characters die off-screen. The script teaches the player the rules: people die here, and grief is not narrated but performed by absence.
1.08 Aeris's house / Elmyra's monologue First exposition dump on the Cetra. The script wisely puts it in the mouth of an adoptive mother, not a sage, so the worldbuilding lands as a domestic story.
1.09 Shinra HQ infiltration Tonal pivot from oppressed-slum heist to corporate thriller. The 60-floor stair-climb is a comedic load-bearer that the script gets away with because it gives the players Cid-style banter from Barret two acts before Cid arrives.
1.10 Hojo's lab / first Sephiroth tease Reveals that the Jenova specimen exists; reveals that Hojo treats Aeris as livestock. Sets up Sephiroth's "return" by demonstrating what he is returning to.
1.11 Prison cell night / Sephiroth massacre Cliffhanger two. Sephiroth's first appearance is bodies in a hallway and a sword in the President's back. He does not speak. The script understands that an off-stage villain who leaves a sword in your boss is more frightening than any monologue.
1.12 Motorcycle escape Punctuation. A pure-genre setpiece to release the pressure of the prison sequence and exit Midgar with kinetic momentum, not contemplation.
1.13 Kalm flashback The script's thesis statement, told as a lie. Twenty minutes of monologue, the entire game's crisis architecture established here, every detail of which will be reframed in Disc 2.
1.14 Junon parade / Cargo ship Re-establishes Shinra after the Midgar departure threatens to drop them as antagonists. The parade rhythm minigame is unironically the script telling you that Shinra's power is a performance everyone has rehearsed.
1.15 Costa del Sol → Mt. Corel → Gold Saucer Tonal palette cleanser. Beach, mountain, theme park. Establishes that Sephiroth can be in front of you and you cannot reach him; sets up Barret's backstory; introduces Cait Sith with what will become a betrayal.
1.16 Corel Prison / Dyne Barret's act-arc payoff. The script forces the most ideologically loud character to look at his own face in his most damaged friend, and to choose to go on without absolution.
1.17 Cosmo Canyon / Cave of the Gi Red XIII's act-arc payoff. The script repeats the structural pattern from Corel: a misremembered father, a confrontation with the truth, an inheritance.
1.18 Nibelheim "discovery" First crack in the Kalm flashback. The town is still there. Sephiroth has been reading in the basement for years. Cloud's grasp on his own narrative starts visibly slipping.
1.19 Rocket Town / acquiring Tiny Bronco Cid joins. Mobility upgrade. Tonally the loudest party member arrives at the moment the script needs to tilt back toward profanity and momentum after Nibelheim's quiet.
1.20 Temple of the Ancients Tseng dies (or appears to). Cloud hands Sephiroth the Black Materia under hypnosis. The protagonist crosses a moral line he did not consent to. Cait Sith sacrifices a body. The act's machine starts roaring toward its end.
1.21 Forgotten Capital / Aeris's death Disc 1 ends. Permanent loss. The script's central permadeath is placed at the disc break — a structural choice that uses the technological seam as a punctuation mark.

The disc breaks as story beats

The disc swap is unintentional dramaturgy, but the script weaponizes it. Disc 1 ends with Aeris's death — a permanent loss for the player and the file. Disc 2 ends with Cloud falling into the Lifestream, identity dissolving. The technological constraint becomes a punctuation mark: each disc ends with a piece of the protagonist erased.

03 — The Lie Engine

How the unreliable narrator actually works

This is the technique most worth stealing. FF7's unreliable narrator is not a clever twist — it is a mechanism. It runs all 30,000 words. Here is how the parts fit together.

1. The intrusion

An off-key line, marked with no quotation marks or speaker tag, surfaces inside Cloud's POV: "Watch out! This isn't just a reactor!!" First reactor, ten minutes in. The reader files it as ambient text. It is, in fact, a memory leaking from the wrong character.

2. The performance

Cloud's lines fall into two registers. Performed: "It's no big deal. Just what I always do." Authentic: "...sigh," "...yeah, sorry," "I know." The performed lines borrow Zack's idiom; the authentic lines are short, declarative, and embarrassed. Once you see it, you can't unsee it.

3. The witness

Tifa says nothing. Her dialogue is full of small flinches: "Cloud... Are you feeling all right?" "Cloud... You just look a little tired I guess." "But, you know, Cloud......" with the trailing dots. The script teaches you to read trailing dots as suppression.

4. The interrogator

Sephiroth's job is structural, not antagonistic. He is the only character who knows the truth and uses it. The Northern Crater illusions and the Mideel reveal are therapeutic in form — a hostile therapist forcing a client to confront a false memory. Replace "puppet" with "patient" and the scene is a Lacanian intervention.

5. The corrector

The Lifestream sequence reframes every false memory by re-staging it from the witness's POV. The script does not invent new information; it gives you the same scenes again with a different camera. This is the technique's most disciplined choice — no retcons, only reframes.

6. The confession

Cloud's Highwind speech is the engine's exhaust. He does not become a different person. He becomes the same person who can describe himself accurately. The script's idea of growth is unusually specific: not strength gained but truth tolerated.

Why this is hard to write: each layer has to be planted before the player has any reason to look for it. The script cannot say "remember Cloud's odd line in Sector 1" — it has to plant it cleanly enough to be ignored on first read and clearly enough to be recoverable on second. The PS1-era localization helped here. Everything looks slightly off-key in 1997 JRPG translation, so the deliberate off-keyness camouflages.

Stealable principle If you are writing an unreliable narrator, do not rely on a single twist scene. Run a full mechanism: intrusion, performance, witness, interrogator, corrector, confession. The reveal is not a moment; it is a pattern that the reader has already half-detected.

04 — Character Studies

The ensemble, line by line

Cloud Strife

Protagonist · Unreliable narrator

Cloud is the script's central formal experiment. From the first reactor scene he is given two voices: a performed swagger ("Once this job's over… I'm outta here") and a glitch ("Watch out! This isn't just a reactor!!" — a thought intruding from someone else's memory). The script signals the trick early and trusts the player not to notice.

The genius is that Cloud's swagger is not arrogance — it is cosplay. He is a failed SOLDIER candidate impersonating his dead friend Zack while wearing the borrowed memory of a charisma he never had. Every "Hmph" and "Don't worry about it. It's no big deal. Just what I always do." is a line he is repeating from someone else.

What makes this work as game writing rather than novel writing: the player has been performing Cloud's swagger themselves, choosing it from menus. When the truth lands, the player has been complicit in the lie.

The performance also explains the otherwise odd choice to give Cloud almost no comedic voice. A character pretending to be cool cannot also be funny. Cid arrives in Act II partly to take over the comic register Cloud cannot occupy without breaking his disguise.

Tifa Lockhart

Witness · Co-author of memory

Tifa is the most underrated character in the script. She knows from Kalm onward that Cloud's story is wrong (she was there; he wasn't), and she chooses not to say so. Her great line — "I'm not sure about… me. My feelings." — is a moral statement disguised as a romantic one. She is, structurally, the audience's surrogate: she sees the contradiction and protects it because the alternative is worse.

The Lifestream sequence is therefore her arc as much as Cloud's. "I can help too. We'll work together to try and get the real you back!" turns her from secret-keeper into co-author of the corrected memory. The script earns its romantic payoff (the Highwind night) precisely because it has spent fifty hours making her complicity expensive.

One subtle craft point: Tifa never directly contradicts Cloud's lies, even when alone with him. Her dissent is registered through ellipses and flinches. Read the whole script for ellipses-after-Tifa-name and you have her arc.

Aeris Gainsborough

Refusenik · Walking exposition

Aeris is introduced as a manic-pixie flower girl and almost every line she speaks fights that introduction. "I'm used to it" (re: danger). "Hee, hee! Cloud! You want to go on a date?" is followed minutes later by a quiet meditation on a boyfriend who never came back. She is the script's only character who is allowed to be openly inconsistent — playful and grieving, naive and the most knowledgeable person in the room.

Her death scene works because the script refuses to comment on it. There is no eulogy in the moment. Cloud's response is not poetic; it is symptomatic — "My fingers are tingling. My mouth is dry. My eyes are burning!" The script understands that grief, in the first hour, is somatic. The eulogy comes later, indirectly, through Bugenhagen and through the Forgotten Capital crystal.

Aeris is also the script's main exposition vehicle, and the writing handles this elegantly: she rarely delivers exposition; she reacts to it. The Cetra plot is told through her confusion and partial recall, not her authority.

Barret Wallace

Ideology · Grief · Father

Barret is often dismissed as a caricature. The script earns him. His speeches read as agitprop — "It's the damn Shinra! They're evil and destroyin' our planet" — but the script keeps undermining him: he blew up a reactor and killed civilians, he abandoned a daughter to fight, he was complicit in the original Corel disaster, his best friend Dyne went mad with the same arm. The script asks: what does revolutionary politics look like when the revolutionary is also a grieving, guilty father?

The Dyne scene is where the script earns its political seriousness. It does not absolve Barret. "I want to destroy everything… Even me." Dyne is what Barret could have been, and Barret has to kill him. The act-arc structurally rhymes with the rest of the cast: every party member has a doppelgänger or shadow they confront in Act II.

Red XIII / Nanaki

Inherited shame

Red XIII's arc is small in word count and disproportionate in craft. He believes his father was a coward; he learns his father was the one who held the line. The reveal is staged in a single still frame — Seto petrified mid-leap. The script does not narrate his realization. He just looks. The image is the script.

Note the structural rhyme with Cloud: a son's misremembered father (Seto) versus a son's misremembered self (Cloud). The same pattern, scaled differently, run twice in one disc. This is the script's main compositional trick — repeat the same arc with different voltages.

Cid Highwind

Profanity · Dignity · Comic register

Cid arrives late and reorients the tone. Until him, the comic register has belonged to Barret's outbursts. Cid is more textured: a profane man whose anger is grief about a launch he aborted to save someone he then bullies for fifteen years. The Shera flashback is the script's clearest study in unspoken apology.

Cid's late takeover as Highwind party leader, when Cloud is removed in Act III, is the script trusting its supporting cast to carry plot weight. He plays the role exactly like Cloud was supposed to — except more honestly. His "What're we gonna do?" doesn't pretend to know.

Vincent Valentine

Sin · Slow time

Optional, which is a structural mistake (he should be mandatory), but characterologically the most interesting late-add. Vincent is a man who chose to sleep through his own punishment. Where Cloud invented a self he could live with, Vincent abandoned the self he had. He is the script's grim alternative to Cloud's solution.

The Lucrecia waterfall scene is one of the script's quietest. "I wanted to die... But the Jenova inside me wouldn't let me die..." The villain is also the reason the lover is alive. The script lets that contradiction sit without commenting.

Yuffie Kisaragi

Comic relief · Materialist · Patriot

Yuffie's sidequest is the script's most underrated act-arc. The setup is pure comedy (she steals materia, the party hunts her); the payoff is political (Wutai lost a war, was demilitarized into a tourist village, and her father chose dishonor to keep it alive). The script lets her resent her father and learn he was right, in the same conversation.

Note how her dialect — "GROSS-NESS!", "Mind your own beeswax!" — drops away during the climactic pagoda scene. Loss of comic register signals dramatic seriousness more reliably than any stage direction.

Cait Sith

Spy · Toy · Bureaucrat

The most formally interesting character, narratively. The reveal that he is a Shinra mole — voiced through a stuffed cat by an executive (Reeve) at corporate HQ — is also a sly meta-joke about the JRPG party member as company employee. He is the only character explicitly working for the antagonist while being playable. The script does not punish him; it lets the others continue to use him after extracting a promise.

His sacrifice in the Temple of the Ancients (a body, not a life) is the script's inversion of the standard heroic-sacrifice beat. He is a controller, not a self. The script's metaphysical plot is anchored in souls returning to a planet; Cait Sith is the cast member without one. The pun is intentional.

Sephiroth

Antagonist · Absence

Sephiroth has remarkably few lines for a villain on a magazine cover. The script understands that he is most effective when implied: through Cloud's flashback, through bodies in hallways, through the stolen Jenova arm. When he speaks, he speaks in a register no other character uses — biblical, cosmic, second-person. "Soon, you will live again as a part of me." This stylistic isolation is the writing's main tool for menace. He doesn't share dialogue idioms with the protagonists; he isn't in their world.

Compare his rhetoric to the prosaic register of Hojo, his actual creator. Hojo says things like "Ha, ha, ha! Ha, ha, ha!" and "Heh, heh, heh." Sephiroth says "I am becoming one with the Planet." The villain has stolen the prophet's voice from the world's mediocre god.

The Shinra brass: Rufus, Heidegger, Scarlet, Reeve, Hojo

Bureaucracy as villain

Each Shinra executive is a different failure mode of corporate evil. Rufus is the cynic ("I'll control the world with fear"). Heidegger is the bully who horse-laughs ("Gya, ha, ha!"). Scarlet is the engineer-sadist ("Kyaa haa hah"). Hojo is the scientist who has lost the ability to ask why. Reeve is the conscience the corporation tolerates because his guilt makes him efficient.

The script's smartest move is letting Reeve become Cait Sith, which means a Shinra exec is in the party as an antagonist's voicebox. The villainy is structural, not personal — and the corporation contains its own opposition, which it deploys as a pet. That is sharper political writing than the genre usually permits.

The Turks: Tseng, Reno, Rude, Elena

Antagonist · Coworker

The Turks are the script's most successful supporting block. Their through-line is that they are middle managers doing their jobs. Reno's "It's no big deal. Just part of the job." after dropping a city plate is the most chilling line in the script precisely because it is the most quotidian. The script grants them dignity (they break for vacation in Wutai, they negotiate a temporary truce against Corneo, they refuse to chase the heroes when off-duty) without absolving them.

This is hard to write. The genre default is to make middle-management antagonists incompetent. The Turks are competent, lethal, and clearly choosing their employer freely. The script lets them be morally complicated and resists redeeming them.

The supporting cast — who get to refuse

Ensemble · Final act

The Highwind "go home and find your reason" sequence in Act IV is a quiet masterstroke of party writing. Each character is given the option to not return. They all do, but each comes back with a private reason — Barret for Marlene, Red XIII for Bugenhagen, Cid for the dignity he never claimed, Vincent because his sin will not let him sleep, Yuffie because she is incapable of cool detachment. Compare this to the standard JRPG ending where the party "stays together because friendship": FF7 makes them choose individually, and that costs nothing mechanically but everything dramatically.

05 — Key Scenes

Eight scenes that carry the weight

1. The Sector 7 Pillar

A masterclass in moral compression. The script takes 90 minutes to get the player to know Biggs, Wedge, and Jessie by name and gesture (Jessie's makeup, Wedge's bravado, Biggs's deciphered codes). It then kills all three off-screen and makes the player run upward toward the falling plate without being able to save them. Reno's casual "It's no big deal. Just part of the job" is the only postmortem they receive. The withholding of grief is the grief.

"Cloud… so you don't care…what happens… to the…Planet?" — Biggs, dying. The script does not punctuate it. Neither does Cloud.

2. The Kalm Flashback

A 20-minute monologue staged inside an inn upstairs. Structurally extraordinary: a JRPG stops the world, gives the player full control of a flashback that is narrated by the protagonist as he is delivering it, and lets the player wander Nibelheim collecting details that will, fifty hours later, be revealed as forgeries. The flashback ends with Sephiroth burning the town — a "real" memory glued onto a false frame. The audacity is that the game shows you the lie and trusts you to forget you saw it.

Note the small details that re-pay later. The cameraman who wants a photo. The villager who calls Cloud "the assistant." Tifa's father warning Sephiroth. Each is information that, in Disc 1, reads as flavor; in Disc 2, reads as evidence.

3. The Don Corneo Bedroom

The script's most under-respected scene. A farce structured as a moral exam. Corneo asks the heroes — and later himself, when the Turks arrive — "why do you think I'd swallow my pride and plead for my life?" Choices include "because he's ready to die" and "because he doesn't know what's going on." It's a pop-quiz in genre logic. When Corneo asks himself the question and answers wrong, the script kills him with the same construction. A joke turned into a structural rhyme. The moral grammar of the whole game compressed into a one-room comedy beat.

4. Corel Prison / Dyne

The cleanest doppelgänger scene in the script. Same accident, same operation, same gun-arm — different choice. The script does not let Barret talk Dyne out of it. Dyne dies on his own terms and gives Barret a pendant for Marlene. The dialogue is not eloquent: "Don't… make Marlene… cry." It is small and exact and does not pretend otherwise. The scene's brilliance is structural: the script teaches you that this is what every character will get — not redemption, but a specific request from a specific dead person.

5. The Forgotten Capital

Two facts about the Aeris death scene that often go uncredited to the script:

  • The dialogue immediately before it is among the most ordinary in the game. "Cloud!" "Aeris?" "We have to find Aeris." The script refuses to foreshadow.
  • The dialogue immediately after it does not let Cloud articulate what happened. He is forced to describe — "Aeris will no longer talk, no longer laugh, cry…" — the writing equivalent of a child explaining death by listing what is no longer possible.

Sephiroth's response is the line that earns the moment its theme: "Stop acting as if you were sad. There's no need to act as though you're angry either. Because, Cloud. You are…" The interrupted sentence — completed only after the boss fight as "…a puppet" — is the structural pivot of the whole game. A death has been used to shift the question from "who killed her?" to "who are you?"

6. The Tifa Execution / Highwind Theft

Often overlooked. With Cloud comatose at Mideel, the script puts Tifa in a gas chamber, makes Scarlet slap her in a button-mashing minigame, has Barret rescue her from a literal Mako cannon barrel, and ends with the rebel-faction crew of the Highwind defecting because they liked Cid that one time. It's a deeply silly sequence that does serious structural work: it gives the women's mid-game arc its own action setpiece, gives Barret his single greatest moment of competence, and reframes the Shinra airship as an ensemble asset.

The slap minigame in particular is a small craft note. The script could have made Tifa passive. Instead, the player has to literally fight back. Mechanically tiny, dramatically large.

7. The Lifestream

Tifa and Cloud walk through fragmented Clouds in a literalized mind-palace. The script does something audacious here: it retells the Kalm flashback, but the camera now points at the witness who was always there. We see Tifa's father, dying. We see young Tifa fall from Mt. Nibel. We see Cloud, the boy who was never invited into the group, watching the well from a distance. The same events, reframed. This is what the script has been building since Disc 1.

The line that lands the scene is small: "I'm sorry, Cloud. If I had only remembered more clearly what happened, I could have done something sooner." Tifa apologizes for failing to be a better witness. The game's whole moral argument — that we owe each other accurate memory — is in that one apology.

8. The Highwind Conversation

The final dramatic beat before the descent into the crater. Cloud asks each character what they are fighting for. He explicitly refuses to make it about saving the planet. "I think we all are fighting for ourselves. For ourselves… and that someone… something… whatever it is, that's important to us."

This is the script's final repudiation of the JRPG "save the world" idiom. The world is being saved as a byproduct of people taking responsibility for one specific thing each. It's the smallest possible scale for the largest possible stakes, and it is the moment the script has been earning for fifty hours.

06 — Annotated Scene

The opening, line by line

Below is the first conversation in the game, with craft notes in the right column. This is the opening sixty seconds and it is doing seven jobs at once.

Barret"C'mon newcomer. Follow me."
Job: OnboardingEstablishes the player as a new arrival in two ways at once — narratively (Cloud is new to AVALANCHE) and pedagogically (the player is new to the controls). One line, two contracts signed.
Biggs"Wow! You used to be in SOLDIER all right! ...Not everyday ya find one in a group like AVALANCHE."
Job: Backstory by reactionBackstory is delivered as reaction, not assertion. Biggs's awe tells us what SOLDIER means in this world without a glossary. Note the elided "ya" — Biggs's class register is set in his second line ever.
Jessie"SOLDIER? Aren't they the enemy? What's he doing with us in AVALANCHE?"
Job: Conflict on arrivalThe script plants the suspicion the player should have. Why is this guy here? Jessie is functioning as the audience's voice. The next line will dispatch the suspicion narratively while leaving it dramatically active.
Biggs"Hold it, Jessie. He WAS in SOLDIER. He quit them and now is one of us."
Job: Lie installationThis is the lie. Cloud was never in SOLDIER. The script is, in its fourth line of dialogue, planting the central deception. It is presented as exoneration, which is exactly how cons work.
Biggs"Didn't catch your name..."
Job: Name entryThe naming prompt — a UI element — is given a diegetic justification. The player is asked to give Cloud his name at the moment Cloud is being asked to perform an identity. This is not accidental. It is the unreliable narrator's first piece of stagecraft: the player names the mask.
Cloud"...Cloud."
Job: Voice registerThe leading ellipsis is Cloud's authentic voice. Three dots before the name. He is hesitating. Compare this to his first declarative line, which is the next one and which is performed.
Cloud"I don't care what your names are. Once this job's over...I'm outta here."
Job: Performed swaggerThis is the Zack voice. Curt, transactional, self-mythologizing. The trailing dots before "I'm outta here" are the tell — a beat of staging the line. The script is making the performance audible. We hear it as cool; later we will hear it as audition.
Barret"The hell you all doin'!? I thought I told you never to move in a group!"
Job: Voice contrastBarret's first line establishes the dialect range the script is willing to write. Profanity, elision, command voice. The contrast with Cloud's clipped neutral register sets up the comic-and-political pairing the next eight hours will live inside.
Barret"Ex-SOLDIER, huh? Don't trust ya!"
Job: Re-plant the lieThe script returns to the deception within four exchanges. The lie has now been stated by Cloud (implicitly), Biggs (defensively), and Barret (suspiciously). Three speakers anchoring one false fact. By the time the player reaches Mideel, sixty hours later, the unreliable-narrator engine has been priming this exact assumption.

Seven craft jobs in nine lines: onboarding, backstory, conflict, lie installation, name-entry diegesis, voice contrast, dialect range, and trust establishment. Most opening scenes in AAA games do three of these. FF7's opener does all seven and reads as casual workplace banter.

Stealable principle The first thirty seconds of dialogue should plant the central deception, the dialect range, and the conflict the player will spend hours resolving. Aim for density of jobs, not density of plot.

07 — Dialogue Craft

Techniques you can name, and where they show up

Dialect as character ID

The localization commits hard: Barret's elided "the" → "th'", Cid's "&%$#" punctuation, Yuffie's surfer slang, Cait Sith's drawl, Vincent's full sentences, Aeris's italicized softness. In a script with limited animation, prose is performance.

Test: cover the speaker tag. Can you identify them in one line? FF7 passes more often than most.

Profanity as register

Profanity is not adolescent here; it's a class signifier. Barret and Cid curse openly. Sephiroth and Rufus never curse. Cloud only curses when he forgets to perform. The asterisks ("&%$#") are doing tonal work the player feels even without parsing them.

Cid: "Don't gimme that, jackass!" / Sephiroth: "Hmph." Two enforcement officers, two universes.

Repetition as motif

"There ain't no gettin' offa this train we on" appears three times — Midgar (literal), Mideel (metaphorical), Northern Crater (mythological). The line gets longer in meaning each time without changing one word. Cleanest piece of motif construction in the script.

A motif must change function across recurrences without changing content. Barret's line passes the test.

Ellipsis as flinch

Trailing dots are not stylistic; they are diagnostic. Cloud's intrusion thoughts begin with them. Tifa's suppressed dissent ends with them. Aeris's grief over Zack opens with them. The script teaches you to read three dots as a tell, then weaponizes that knowledge in Disc 2.

Tifa: "But, you know, Cloud......" — six dots is more honest than the sentence she finishes.

Choice as confession

Player choice does not branch the plot — it branches the relationship texture. Aeris remembers whether you bought a flower; Tifa remembers whether you said "I worked hard for it"; the date you get is determined by tracked counters going back to chapter one. Honest player accounting.

Choice does not change what happens. It changes who you were when it happened.

Off-screen storytelling

President Shinra is killed off-screen. Hojo's history with Lucrecia is told through a hidden videotape the player may never find. Zack's death is implied through a flashback Cloud cannot finish. The script has the confidence to put climactic information in optional places.

Confidence: the script doesn't need every player to comprehend everything. It rewards the players who look.

Comic relief as foreshadow

Wall Market crossdressing and the Don Corneo bedroom are often called tonal whiplash. Read structurally, they are doing essential work: they isolate Cloud from his SOLDIER persona by forcing him into a costume. The cross-dressing prefigures the larger truth — Cloud has been wearing someone else's costume the entire game.

A farce that doesn't earn its main plot's themes is just a farce. FF7's farces are setup.

Backstory by reaction

The script almost never delivers backstory through dumps. It delivers it through how characters react to each other. Biggs's awe at "SOLDIER" tells us what SOLDIER means. Tifa's silence about Cloud tells us about Cloud. Reno's casual cruelty tells us about Shinra.

Don't tell us what the world is. Show us what the world makes people do.

Death by description

When grief lands, the script avoids eloquence. "My fingers are tingling. My mouth is dry. My eyes are burning!" is Cloud's line at Aeris's death. The body's symptoms instead of metaphor. The script trusts that the reader does the metaphor.

First-hour grief is somatic. Second-week grief is poetic. The script knows the difference.

Doppelgänger structure

Every major party member meets a darker version of themselves: Barret/Dyne, Red XIII/Seto, Vincent/Hojo, Cid/Shera (his abandoned future), Cloud/Sephiroth, Aeris/Jenova. The script is unusually rigorous about pairing each character with a mirror.

A character without a mirror is just a description. A character with a mirror has a question.

08 — Localization

The Baskett translation as a writing object

The English script of FF7 was localized by Michael Baskett, working alone, against deadline, in 1997, without retranslation passes. The result is famously rough in places — Aeris's name was originally "Aerith"; Cait Sith says "kya ha ha"; many lines read as compressed-Japanese-sentence-padding. It is also, in places, brilliant.

Three reasons it matters as a writing object:

1. The localization invented voices the original didn't have

Barret's African-American Vernacular English coding, Cid's profanity, Cait Sith's drawl, Yuffie's surfer slang are largely localizer choices. The Japanese script has class and regional markers but does not have the strong dialect ladder the English script imposes. This was a craft decision — to use the limited translation budget to differentiate speakers by idiom rather than by exposition. It is the single biggest reason the script reads as well as it does.

2. The "errors" become tone

"This guy are sick" is the famous line. Read in context (NPC: an exhausted villager describing a dying man), the agreement error reads like dialect. "Off course!" "Don't ack so big" — these are misspellings the player processes as accent. The script's surface roughness, accidentally or otherwise, reinforces its main thematic concern: nobody here is speaking from authority, everyone is improvising their identity.

If the script had been polished into AAA-localization neutral by a 2010s team, it would lose this. Worse — the unreliable narrator engine partly relies on Cloud's own English sounding slightly off. When everyone's English sounds slightly off, his off-ness camouflages.

3. The censoring is louder than profanity

The script censors profanity with random punctuation: "&%$#", "*&^%$", "$#&%". This is a Nintendo-era artifact, but it functions like a prosody mark. The player hears Cid swearing. Self-censoring with visible obscenity is, accidentally, a more literary device than just printing "fuck." It marks the speech act without performing it.

Working note If you are localizing a script today, the FF7 lesson is not "be sloppy." It is: use idiom to differentiate before you use exposition. A surfer-talking ninja and a profane Britsh pilot communicate ten times more efficiently than two paragraphs of bio text would.

4. The set pieces of localization

Two scenes deserve specific localizer credit:

  • The Don Corneo torture scene. The "I'll chop them off / rip them off / smash them" rotation depending on which character is asking is a localization-only joke. The Japanese delivers the same tonal beat differently. The English version is a structural rhyme that pays off across three speakers.
  • Cid's launch failure flashback. The English "GODDAMMIT, Shera... you wanna make me a murderer?" is sharper than the Japanese. The localizer let Cid be cruel at the moment of his greatest decency, which is the dramatic irony the scene needs.

09 — System Voice

The narrator who is the game itself

FF7 has a sneaky third narrator beyond Cloud and the omniscient: the system. Menu text, save points, NPC barks, item names, world-map hints. This is the layer most analyses ignore. It is doing real work.

NPC chatter as world description

The script is generous with named-but-unnamed NPCs. Kalm's red-haired man with his "Listen to me!" hook becomes a recurring informant who reports the state of the world from town to town. The Kalm Traveler in his fourth-house upstairs is a quiet world-meter:

  • Disc 1: "The world's at peace... all's well."
  • Disc 1 mid: "Tomorrow's another day... all's well."
  • Disc 1 late: "I got my health, and enough to eat... all's well."

Three lines, three rounds of optimism, escalating in specificity, in a man whose later dialogue is "If Shinra was our last hope, and they've been beaten by terrorists... Are we all going to die?" The script treats him as a barometer, and his reading is a bit of worldbuilding the player only catches if they revisit. Optional, repeated dialogue as historical record.

Item names as moral commentary

Don Corneo's torture room rewards: "Cotton Dress," "Satin Dress," "Silk Dress." Cloud's optional outfit upgrades named for class. Tifa's ultimate weapon: "Premium Heart." Aeris's: "Princess Guard." Cloud's: "Ultima Weapon." The naming convention shifts as the script's mythology deepens. Mundane / ironic / elegiac.

Save points as ritual

Save points are silent in FF7, but their placement is voiced. The script puts a save point immediately before Aeris's death. Immediately before the Kalm flashback. Immediately before the Highwind Conversation. The save point is the script's way of saying "this is going to matter." It is the closest thing the game has to a chapter break, and the script uses it dramaturgically.

Battle barks as character

"Attack while it's tail's up!" — Cloud's first battle bark, instructing the player. It is also slightly wrong (the Guard Scorpion counterattacks if you attack while its tail is up). The script plants Cloud's intrusive memory in a battle UI line. Re-read: Cloud is repeating advice he heard from someone else, applied incorrectly. The unreliable narrator is in the tutorial.

This is the level of detail that makes FF7 worth reading as a screenplay. The system text is not stage directions. It is voice.

10 — Choice Architecture

What the menus are tracking, and what they reveal

FF7 runs invisible counters for every dialogue choice you make involving Aeris, Tifa, Yuffie, and Barret. Those counters compose into the Gold Saucer date, the Highwind night, and a dozen smaller payoffs. The script's choice architecture is the most disciplined in PS1-era RPGs and the design pattern is worth naming explicitly:

Choices that tier, not branch

No choice in FF7 cuts off content. The Gold Saucer date is variable, but it always happens; the Highwind speech adapts but never absents. The script trusts that texture is more affecting than divergence.

Choices revealed, never explained

The system never tells the player what is being tracked. Aeris simply remembers the flower; Tifa simply phrases the morning differently; Yuffie simply gives or doesn't give materia back. The lack of UI feedback is the design.

Choices with delayed cost

The Aeris flower decision in chapter one (1 gil) determines whether Aeris recognizes you in the church (chapter five) and influences the Gold Saucer date (chapter twenty). A six-second decision propagates twenty hours forward. This is the script's most patient design.

Choices that change the player, not the plot

The Wall Market dress-up offers no plot variance. It offers self-presentation variance. How earnestly did you go about it? The script silently scores you on commitment to the bit and rewards you with Don Corneo picking Cloud. Mechanic and theme aligned.

A working list of tracked variables

Counters the script keeps, as best as can be reconstructed from script branching:

Aeris affection
Incremented by buying flower, "Don't see many flowers around here", giving Aeris the flower (vs Marlene), bodyguard fee discount, Gold Saucer dialogue, and many side-replies. Drives: Gold Saucer date, Aeris's bodyguard scene tone, several minor lines.
Tifa affection
Incremented by Sector 7 dialogue choices, "Worried about me!" line, drink at 7th Heaven, Mt. Nibel reactor reactions, Highwind night responses. Drives: Gold Saucer date, Highwind night dialogue, "I just got lucky" vs "I worked hard for it" distinction.
Yuffie affection
Incremented by recruitment dialogue choices, sailor-suit comment, Junon "Yeah... you look great" reply. Drives: Gold Saucer date eligibility (rare), Yuffie banter variance.
Barret affection
Incremented by ignoring him, agreeing with his ideology, sailor-suit gag. Drives: Gold Saucer date as a joke route. The script knows.
Don Corneo dress score
0–20 depending on dress, wig, makeup, perfume, tiara, and underwear collected. Determines who Corneo picks for the night.
Live TV ratings
Tracked during the Junon parade. Determines the gift Heidegger sends after the parade. A throwaway gag system that the script writes specific reactive dialogue for. Generosity beyond the call.
Stealable principle Treat dialogue choices as character accounting, not plot branching. Every reply is an entry in a ledger. The ledger should pay out twenty hours later in a small, specific way that the player feels but cannot prove.

11 — Foreshadowing Inventory

Setups and payoffs, paired

A partial catalog of long-distance setups in the script. Anyone trying to write a long-form game story should study these. The ratio of setup-to-payoff distance is what makes FF7 reread well.

Setup · Disc 1, ch. 1Watch out! This isn't just a reactor!! An unattributed thought intrudes on Cloud's POV.
Payoff · Disc 2, MideelRevealed as Zack's voice. Cloud has been borrowing Zack's memory of a previous reactor mission since the first ten minutes of the game.
Setup · Disc 1, Sector 8Aeris sells flowers. The player can buy one (1 gil). Aeris asks if anyone here has flowers — there are no flowers in Midgar.
Payoff · Disc 1, Sector 5Aeris's church grows flowers when nothing else in Midgar can. The Cetra connection is planted as a horticultural anomaly before it becomes mythology.
Setup · Disc 1, 7th HeavenTifa's "promise" flashback. Cloud agreed to come if Tifa was ever in trouble.
Payoff · Disc 2, LifestreamThe promise is the key memory Cloud uses to anchor his real self after the dissolution. The romantic plant becomes the ontological one.
Setup · Disc 1, Wall MarketCloud is forced to wear someone else's clothes. The script presents it as comedy.
Payoff · Disc 2, MideelCloud has been wearing someone else's identity all along. The crossdressing was a literal-comic instance of the abstract-tragic structure.
Setup · Disc 1, Sector 5 churchThe flower bed "broke your fall." The roof is broken because something fell.
Payoff · Disc 1, endAeris dies in the same kind of garden. Things fall on flowers; flowers cushion or absorb. The stagecraft of her death has been blocked since chapter five.
Setup · Disc 1, KalmCloud's flashback puts him in a SOLDIER uniform alongside Sephiroth. We see him from outside the helmet only at one point — the truck-sickness scene.
Payoff · Disc 2, LifestreamThe truck-sickness scene is the only shot in which we see Cloud's face during the flashback. This is because Cloud was in Shinra infantry, masked, and only Zack saw his face in the truck. The Kalm flashback secretly shows you the truth.
Setup · Disc 1, Cosmo CanyonBugenhagen explains spirit energy and the Lifestream while floating through a hologram of stars.
Payoff · Disc 3, Northern CraterHoly as a literal plea to that spiritual current. The lecture you sat through becomes the cosmology you are using.
Setup · Disc 1, Costa del SolAeris recognizes Zack's parents' house. She mutters "Zack..."
Payoff · Disc 2, MideelZack is the SOLDIER First Class Cloud has been impersonating. Aeris's first love and Cloud's stolen self are the same person. The Gongaga scene is the script's most efficient piece of co-foreshadowing.
Setup · Disc 1, Wall Market diner"Don Corneo, why do you think a bad guy like me would swallow his pride and plead for his life?" The Don asks the question as a joke.
Payoff · Disc 1, end of Wall MarketThe Turks ask Corneo the same question. He answers wrong. They kill him with the construction. The joke is the structure.
Setup · Disc 1, prison cellCloud says: "It's true that sometimes I can't figure out who I am." Tifa says nothing.
Payoff · Disc 2, LifestreamTifa's silence at the prison cell is later revealed as the moment she chose not to correct him. The whole Lifestream sequence is her atoning for that single non-line.
Setup · Disc 1, Cargo ShipSephiroth: "...the time...is now..." A piece of Jenova in a Shinra uniform delivers four words of dialogue.
Payoff · Disc 2, Whirlwind MazeThe "Sephiroth" you have been chasing was Jenova in costume, controlled by the real Sephiroth from the Northern Crater. Every appearance of him in Disc 1 retroactively revealed.
Setup · Disc 2, Mideel clinicCloud, comatose, mutters: "Zac......k. Zack..." Tifa hears it and says "You remembered!!"
Payoff · same sceneThis is the Lifestream's first crack. The script's most elegant piece of self-payoff: setup and payoff in the same scene, separated by Tifa's relief.
Stealable principle The best foreshadowing in long-form games doesn't read as foreshadowing on first pass — it reads as flavor. The bar is: a returning player should say "wait, that was already there?" If they say "ah, the foreshadowing," you planted it too loudly.

12 — Pacing & Tonal Modulation

How the script breathes

FF7 is a 40-hour text. It would not survive if it stayed at one register. The script's tonal modulation is more disciplined than its reputation suggests.

The pressure-release pattern

Almost every major dramatic beat is followed by an explicit comic or kinetic palate-cleanser. This is not accidental.

  • Sector 7 Pillar (devastation) → Sector 6 Wall Market return / Aeris's house (domestic).
  • Shinra HQ massacre / Sephiroth reveal (horror) → Motorcycle escape (kinetic).
  • Kalm flashback (existential) → Mythril Mine / Junon parade (silly).
  • Aeris's death (loss) → Snowboarding minigame on the next continent (kinetic).
  • Mideel reveal (truth) → Huge Materia chase (heist montage).
  • Sister Ray firing / Hojo (climax) → Highwind reunion (warmth).

The script does not let the player sit in any single emotional register for more than an hour. This is a form of authorial respect — it assumes the player needs metabolic time after a major beat.

The slow-down

Conversely, the script's most important moments are explicitly slower than what surrounds them. The Kalm flashback is twenty minutes of inn upstairs. The Lifestream sequence is dozens of small interactive memory tiles. The Highwind Conversation is a campfire. The script knows that revelation requires room.

Optional content as breath

Chocobo breeding, the Battle Square, Wutai's pagoda, Vincent's Lucrecia waterfall, the underwater Gelnika — all these are placed in Disc 2 specifically. The script needs the player to spend time in the world after Aeris's death and before the final descent. The "side content" is a structural deceleration. A player who skips it will feel the back half rushed for a reason that is not the writer's fault.

Sequence rhythm vs scene rhythm

Within scenes, the script favors short paragraphs and frequent speaker switches. Long monologues are rare and earned: Bugenhagen's spirit-energy lecture, Elmyra's adoption story, Cid's launch flashback, Vincent's confession. Each of these is the deepest piece of context for a character or theme, and each is given to a single voice for several screens of text. Otherwise, the dialogue ricochets — three speakers per beat, ellipses doing the connective work.

13 — Themes

What the script is actually about

Memory as moral act

FF7 is fundamentally a story about who has the right to narrate. Sephiroth tries to overwrite Cloud's memory with Hojo's experimental record. Tifa restores it by walking him through her own. Bugenhagen frames the planet itself as a memory-organism — the Lifestream is "spirit energy that returns to the planet," that is, every life remembered by the world. The villain is, in this reading, an editor; the heroes are co-witnesses.

The planet as body

The script is unusually consistent in its central metaphor. Mako is "blood." Reactors are needles. Meteor is a "wound." The Northern Crater is a scar from the original Cetra-Jenova catastrophe, two thousand years old, still healing. Weapon is the planet's immune system. This is not just flavor text — it is the moral grammar of every political conflict in the game. To extract is to bleed.

Complicity

No character in the script is innocent. Barret bombed reactors. Tifa was AVALANCHE. Cid pushed the Emergency Engine Shutdown that arguably grounded human spaceflight. Vincent watched Lucrecia die and called it punishment. Reeve worked for Shinra. Cait Sith spied on the heroes. Even Cloud handed Sephiroth the Black Materia. The script's most adult choice is that guilt is a precondition for action, not a disqualification.

The cost of "saving the world"

Holy "will not save the world" automatically. The Lifestream emerges in the closing FMV not as deus ex machina but as a collective answer — souls that have been witnessed are returning to do work. The closing shot ("Five hundred years later") is not triumphal. The script does not promise that humans survived. It promises only that the planet did. That is unusually severe writing for the genre.

Performed vs inherited identity

Every major character has an identity they were given (Cloud's invented SOLDIER career, Red XIII's father's reputation, Vincent's Turks role, Cid's pilot's pride, Yuffie's princess birth, Barret's miner's heritage) and an identity they perform on top of it. The script treats both as real and asks which the character is willing to take responsibility for. The "fight for your reason" speech in Act IV is the thematic reduction of this whole layer.

Witness as duty

The line that the script keeps coming back to, in different mouths: "I saw it." Tifa says it about Sephiroth at Nibelheim. Vincent says it about Lucrecia. Bugenhagen says it about the planet. Cloud's whole arc is becoming able to say "I saw it" about himself. The script considers seeing accurately a moral act with the same weight as acting bravely.

14 — Working Takeaways

Principles a game writer can steal

P1 · The lie should be a system, not a twist

If your game has an unreliable narrator, build the mechanism. Cloud's deception runs across intrusions, performed register, witness silence, antagonist interrogation, restorative co-witness, and final confession. Six layers, all live by chapter one.

If you cannot point to all six layers in your script outline, the twist will land as a cheat.

P2 · Dialect before bio

A reader should be able to identify your speaker from one line, with no tag, by chapter three. If they can't, your characters are bios, not voices. Use idiomatic markers, sentence length, ellipsis habit, profanity license, vocabulary class. Backstory is paper. Voice is performance.

P3 · Choices are confessions, not branches

Branching plot is expensive and underread. Tracked relational choices are cheap and over-felt. Spend your variability on how characters remember the player, not on what happens. Aeris noting a 1-gil flower three chapters later is worth more than a forked ending.

P4 · Plant the truth where it reads as flavor

The Kalm flashback's Cloud is wearing infantry armor; the helmet only comes off for two lines. Players don't notice. Returning players notice everything. Plant your twist in places where casual playthrough reads it as ambient detail.

P5 · Mirror every protagonist with a darker self

FF7 pairs Cloud with Sephiroth, Barret with Dyne, Red XIII with Seto, Vincent with Hojo, Cid with the man he could have stayed. A character without a doppelgänger is a portrait; a character with one is an argument.

P6 · Grief should be physical before it is poetic

"My fingers are tingling. My mouth is dry." Cloud at Aeris's death. The script knows that the first hour after a loss is somatic. The eulogy comes later, said by someone else, often misattributed. Writing grief as immediate metaphor is the most common amateur tell.

P7 · Optional content is structural, not extra

Vincent and Yuffie are optional. Wutai, Gold Saucer dating, the Gelnika, Lucrecia's cave, the Chocobo Sage — optional. The script uses these as pacing for the players who find them and respects players who don't. Never put the climax in optional content. Always put the texture there.

P8 · Use the system as a third narrator

Save points, item names, NPC barks, and menu text are all available to you. They are usually wasted. FF7 plants the unreliable narrator engine in a battle bark. Treat every line of system text as potentially diegetic.

P9 · Trust the disc break (or whatever break you have)

Disc 1 ends on Aeris's death. Disc 2 ends on Cloud falling into the Lifestream. The technological constraint becomes punctuation. If your game has chapters, save points, or load screens, use them as act breaks. They are free dramaturgy.

P10 · The final question should be small

"What are you fighting for?" Cloud asks each party member individually. Not "will you save the world." The script's final move is to refuse the genre's largest scale and replace it with the smallest possible one. The world is saved as a byproduct of someone keeping a specific promise to a specific dead person.

15 — Critique

What doesn't work

An honest reading has to admit the seams.

  • The Cetra exposition dumps. Cosmo Canyon, Icicle Inn, and the Ancient Temple all halt the story to deliver several screens of lore. The Cetra-Jenova-Weapon backstory is over-explained in places where atmosphere would have served better. Compare to the elegance of "I hear the cries of the Planet" — the script knows how to imply, but doesn't always trust itself.
  • The Aeris-Tifa friendship is undercooked. The script gives Aeris and Tifa one direct conversation about Cloud (in the Wall Market basement), and after Aeris's death Tifa is rarely allowed to mourn her on-page. The Lifestream sequence repairs this somewhat by reframing Tifa as Aeris's structural successor, but the writing leaves the women's friendship as subtext when it could have been a third leg of the story.
  • Yuffie and Vincent are optional, which costs them. Both have richer backstories than several main-line characters. Locking them behind side-quests means many players experience the final act with a thinner ensemble than the script seems to assume. The Highwind speech in particular reads more powerfully when seven characters are present.
  • The Shinra brass collapse too quickly. Rufus is built up as a competent, ideological villain ("I'll control the world with fear"), then sidelined and killed off-screen by Diamond Weapon's beam. The political antagonist gets less narrative attention in the back half than he deserves; the story's gravity has shifted entirely to the metaphysical plot, and the corporate critique loses its teeth.
  • Cait Sith's reveal is rushed. The "I was a spy" beat at Gold Saucer is one of the script's strongest setups (the fortune that breaks down, the date sequence that ends in betrayal). His later integration into the party — after threatening Marlene's life — happens with surprisingly little friction. The script forgives him faster than the characters realistically would.
  • Hojo as final antagonist is a tonal mistake. The Sister Ray sequence puts Hojo between the player and Sephiroth; he is a cackling mad scientist where the script needed a structural antagonist. The corporate conspiracy ends with a man ranting about his son. It satisfies plot bookkeeping at the cost of thematic shape.
  • The ending is allergic to closure. "Five hundred years later" is a defensible thematic choice but a frustrating dramatic one. The cast's individual fates — did Cid see Shera again? did Barret return to Marlene? — are abandoned. The script trades emotional payoff for cosmic scale, and the trade does not always feel like the right one.
  • The flashback-inside-flashback architecture overreaches. The Lifestream sequence is brilliant when it works and confusing when it doesn't. The Nibelheim-inside-Nibelheim layering, the multiple Clouds, and the unmarked transitions assume more attention than 1997 conventions trained the player to give. Some players exit the sequence not understanding what changed.
  • Romantic ambiguity becomes evasion. The Cloud-Tifa-Aeris triangle is left structurally unresolved in a way the script frames as respect for the player but reads, in 2020s terms, as unwillingness to write either woman's interiority directly. Aeris dies before the question can be asked. Tifa's love is implied by ellipsis.

16 — Legacy

What the script did first

Several techniques that are now genre-standard were either invented by FF7 or stabilized into the form we recognize:

Playable POV switch

The disc-2 sequence where Tifa becomes the protagonist while Cloud is missing was a structural shock in 1997. It is now standard in BioWare and FromSoftware games, and the visual-novel convention of "this chapter is from X's perspective" descends partly from this beat.

Permadeath of a main character

Aeris's death wasn't the first in a JRPG, but it was the first to be permanent for a marquee character — no Phoenix Down, no resurrection arc, no "she's been in a coma." The script enforces it. Every later "and they really die" beat in mainstream RPGs sits in this scene's shadow.

Memory-as-mechanic

The Lifestream sequence and the player's ability to walk through the protagonist's mosaicked self anticipates a generation of "psyche levels" — Catherine's nightmares, Persona 5's Palaces, Hellblade's voices. FF7 staged the inside of a mind as explorable space.

Choice tracking as relationship texture

Mass Effect's Paragon/Renegade, Stardew Valley's heart events, Disco Elysium's thought cabinet — all descend from the FF7 affection-counter pattern. FF7 was the first to make the system invisible to the player by design.

Optional villain content

Hojo's secret video, Lucrecia's cave, the Gelnika Turks fight, Vincent's whole backstory — the practice of putting the antagonist's most revealing material in optional spaces is now standard (FromSoft is the obvious heir) but FF7 was an early disciplined practitioner.

The "world map after Disc 1" structure

Open the world after a controlled vertical opening; let the player triage their party arcs in any order. Every modern open-world RPG with a long urban prologue (Witcher 3, Cyberpunk, Mass Effect) is following this shape.
