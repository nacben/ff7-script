What is philosophy?

Philosophy is not the preserve of brilliant but eccentric thinkers. It is what every one of us does in those quiet moments when we stop and wonder what life and the universe are really about. We are inquisitive creatures, gifted with reason — and whenever we reason, we philosophize.

The earliest philosophers, in ancient Greece, China, and India, were not satisfied with the explanations supplied by religion and custom. They sought rational answers, and — just as importantly — they argued with each other. Ideas were sharpened in debate, refined in dialogue, and passed down through schools of thought that still shape how we think today.

Philosophy is less a body of answers than a way of asking. To follow its long argument is to learn how to think.

700 BCE — 250 CE

The Ancient World

✦

Where it begins. In a handful of Greek city-states, in the courts of warring Chinese kingdoms, and beneath a bodhi tree in northern India, a new kind of question is being asked — not what do the gods say? but what can reason show?

Pre-Socratic · Miletus

Thales of Miletus

c. 624 – 546 BCE

Everything is made of water.

He was wrong about water, and that is the point — what matters is the move he made. Instead of accepting "the gods did it," he demanded a natural cause: something observable, arguable, and capable of being proven wrong. Every scientific explanation since sits in the chair Thales pulled up. The practical habit to copy: when faced with a mystery, do not stop at "mysterious." Ask what would have to be true for this to happen, then look for evidence. That single discipline separates inquiry from superstition.

Branch · Metaphysics · Monism

Daoism · China

Laozi

c. 6th century BCE

The Dao that can be told is not the eternal Dao.

The deepest truths escape language; the moment you name them, you replace the living reality with a label. The remedy is wu wei — not laziness, but action without forcing, like water that wears down stone by yielding to its shape. The practical discipline: stop pushing against the grain of a situation. Observe what it wants to do and align with that. In conflict, yield where you can; in work, choose effort that flows rather than effort that strains. The strongest move is often the one that refuses to insist.

Tradition · Chinese · Daoism

Mathematics & Mysticism

Pythagoras

c. 570 – 495 BCE

Number is the ruler of forms and ideas.

Pluck a string; halve its length and the note jumps an octave; at two-thirds you get a perfect fifth. The world is not chaos beneath the music — it runs on ratios. From this, Pythagoras drew a startling conclusion: matter may be less fundamental than the mathematical structure that organizes it. Modern physics has quietly proved him right, describing reality not in terms of stuff but equations. The takeaway for daily thinking: when something seems random or overwhelming, look for the proportions. Many problems that resist intuition surrender to a sketch, a ratio, or a careful count.

Branch · Metaphysics · Pythagoreanism

Buddhism · India

Siddhartha Gautama — The Buddha

c. 563 – 483 BCE

Happy is he who has overcome his ego.

Suffering, the Buddha observed, is not caused by what happens to you but by your craving for things to be different. Lose a job, lose a loved one, lose your reputation — the pain is real, but the suffering is the part you add through clinging. The remedy is not numbness or withdrawal but seeing clearly how the self works: you are not a fixed thing demanding to be served, but a stream of changing experiences. The Eightfold Path is the practical curriculum — right speech (do not lie or wound), right livelihood (do not earn from harm), right mindfulness (watch your mind without becoming it). Try it for a week and notice how much daily misery is reaction rather than event.

Tradition · Eastern · Buddhism

Confucianism · China

Confucius

551 – 479 BCE

Hold faithfulness and sincerity as first principles.

Confucius noticed something we still need: a society does not run on laws so much as on small daily acts of trust. A friend who shows up, a worker who does not cheat, a leader who keeps her word — these accumulate into civilization. No rule can substitute for character, so the only honest way to change others is to change yourself first. Practical test: in every relationship, ask whether you are reliable in the small things. And take his Golden Rule in its sharper, negative form — do not impose on others what you would not choose for yourself. Easier to apply than the positive version: you usually know what you would resent.

Tradition · Chinese · Confucianism

Flux & Change

Heraclitus

c. 535 – 475 BCE

Everything is flux.

You cannot step into the same river twice — not only because the river has changed, but because you have. Reality is process, not stuff. Marriages, businesses, bodies, friendships: nothing stays the thing it was without continuous remaking. Two practical consequences. First, expecting permanence is the source of much misery; build for change instead and your projects last longer. Second, opposites need each other — rest only means something against work, joy against sorrow — so a life that flees one half of any pair flattens the other too.

Branch · Metaphysics · Monism

Eleatic School

Parmenides

c. 515 – 445 BCE

All is one.

Parmenides reasoned his way to a startling conclusion: change is logically impossible, so the changing world our senses report must be illusion. Few accept the conclusion, but the method made history. He showed that pure reasoning can override even the most vivid evidence, and that what feels obviously true may be incoherent on inspection. Practical takeaway: when your gut and a tight argument disagree, sometimes the gut is right and the argument hides a flaw; sometimes the argument is right and the gut is parochial. Learning to tell which is which is most of philosophy — and most of good judgment.

Branch · Metaphysics · Monism

The Sophists

Protagoras

c. 490 – 420 BCE

Man is the measure of all things.

Truth, beauty, and justice are not engraved on the cosmos; they are judgments people make, shaped by where and when they live. A dish one diner praises another sends back. A practice one culture calls just another calls cruel. Protagoras was the first to face this squarely. The danger is moral paralysis: if every standard is relative, why fight injustice? The honest response is not retreat but humility — your certainties were also shaped, and must be defended in open argument rather than imposed from on high. Useful in any disagreement: stop asking who is cosmically right and ask whose framing, formed by what experience, each of you is using. The argument often becomes tractable the moment you can name the frames.

Branch · Ethics · Relativism

Atomism

Democritus & Leucippus

c. 460 – 371 BCE

Nothing exists except atoms and empty space.

Two and a half millennia before microscopes confirmed it, the atomists guessed: cut a thing finely enough and you reach particles that cannot be cut further, swimming in emptiness. Their boldness was to drain all mystery from matter — no spirits, no purposes, just configuration and motion. The implications for living are surprisingly direct. At death your atoms disperse and recombine elsewhere; you are not annihilated, only redistributed. And if matter is just arrangement, then minds, moods, and meanings are arrangements too — which is humbling and oddly freeing. Many rigid pictures of the self soften when held against this.

Branch · Metaphysics · Atomism

Athens · Founder of Western Philosophy

Socrates

469 – 399 BCE

The unexamined life is not worth living.

Socrates' method is simple and devastating. He asks: what do you mean by justice? You give a definition. He finds a case where it fails. You refine. He finds another. By the fourth round you realize you do not know what you have been confidently using the word to mean. The lesson is not that knowledge is impossible — it is that most certainty is unexamined assumption. Try this on your most-held beliefs about success, love, what counts as a good life. If you cannot answer "what exactly do you mean, and why?" without contradicting yourself, the belief is borrowed rather than yours. Most adult unhappiness is built on borrowed beliefs.

Branch · Epistemology · Dialectical Method

The Academy · Athens

Plato

c. 427 – 347 BCE

Earthly knowledge is but shadow.

Plato's cave: prisoners chained facing a wall, watching shadows cast by a fire behind them, certain that the shadows are the world. He thought ordinary experience works the same way — we see imperfect copies and never the perfect Forms that ground them. You do not have to swallow the whole metaphysics to use the picture. Notice how often you mistake one bad relationship for love itself, or one corrupt institution for politics itself. Thinking clearly means distinguishing the case from the kind. And Plato's uncomfortable lesson about teaching: most people, when shown a truth that contradicts their shadows, will turn back to the wall. Be ready for that — in others, and in yourself.

Branch · Epistemology · Rationalism

The Lyceum · Athens

Aristotle

384 – 322 BCE

Truth resides in the world around us.

Where Plato looked up to perfect Forms, Aristotle looked carefully at what was in front of him: animals, governments, friendships. Each thing has a telos — what it is for, what flourishing looks like for that kind of thing. A good knife cuts; a good eye sees; a good human reasons, befriends, and acts well. From this comes his ethics of virtue, which is the most usable ethical system ever devised. Do not ask "what rule applies here?" Ask "what would a person of good character do?" and then build that character through repeated practice. Courage is not what you feel when the crisis arrives; it is the habit you built in the months before. Generosity, patience, honesty — all are skills, trained like any other.

Branch · Epistemology · Empiricism

The Garden · Athens

Epicurus

341 – 270 BCE

Death is nothing to us.

Most of what you fear, Epicurus argues, dissolves under analysis. Death cannot harm you because while you exist it is not here, and when it comes you are not. The gods, if they exist, have better things to do than punish you. The recipe for a good life is modest and durable: a few close friends, simple food, distance from politics and ambition, freedom from manufactured wants. Modern life would horrify him — endless craving produced by advertising and status competition. A useful exercise: list everything you currently want, then mark which would actually relieve a real pain, and which are wants for wants' sake. Most chronic dissatisfaction lives in the second column.

Branch · Ethics · Epicureanism

The Cynics

Diogenes of Sinope

c. 404 – 323 BCE

He has the most who is most content with the least.

Diogenes lived in a barrel, owned a cup until he saw a child drink from cupped hands and threw the cup away, and mocked Alexander the Great to his face. The performance had a point: almost every misery in a comfortable society comes from desires we have been taught to have. Cut the desire and the misery goes with it. You do not need to move into a barrel. For one week, notice each purchase and ask: did I want this thing, or did I want to want it? The reduction is usually surprising — and so is the freedom that follows.

Branch · Ethics · Cynicism

Stoicism

Zeno of Citium

c. 332 – 265 BCE

The goal of life is living in agreement with nature.

The Stoic insight is operational. Divide everything in your life into what is in your control (your judgments, choices, attention, effort) and what is not (other people, the weather, the past, most outcomes). Spend your energy only where it can land. Practice this seriously and most anxiety quiets, because most anxiety lives in the second column. The Stoics were not unfeeling — they were strategic about feeling. Marcus Aurelius ran an empire by these lines and Epictetus survived slavery by them; the philosophy bends to fit either palace or chain. Start with the morning question: what part of today is actually mine to shape?

Branch · Ethics · Stoicism

Wonder is very much the affection of a philosopher; for there is no other beginning of philosophy than this. — Plato
250 — 1500

The Medieval World

✦

Athens fades; the cathedrals rise. For a thousand years, philosophy in Christendom, the Islamic world, and Jewish thought wrestles with the same problem: how to reconcile the inheritance of Greek reason with the demands of revealed faith.

Christian Platonism

St. Augustine of Hippo

354 – 430

God is not the parent of evils.

If God is good, why is there evil? Augustine's answer is two moves. First, evil is not a thing on its own — it is an absence, the way blindness is an absence of sight, or rust the absence of intact metal. Second, the world contains evil because rational beings must be free, and freedom that cannot choose wrongly is not freedom at all. You do not need his theology to use the insight. Many things you call "problems" are actually gaps — missing trust, missing rest, missing competence — and respond not to being attacked but to being filled. Diagnose the absence before swinging at the symptom.

Branch · Ethics · Christian Platonism

Late Roman

Boethius

c. 480 – 525

God foresees our free thoughts and actions.

Boethius wrote his most famous book in prison, awaiting execution. The puzzle: if anyone — God, a perfect predictor, your future self — already knows what you will do, are you still free? His answer is elegant. Knowing what someone will choose is not the same as causing them to choose it. A friend who predicts your order at a familiar restaurant has not chosen for you. Useful when others argue you out of responsibility because circumstances "made" you act: prediction is not coercion, and being predictable is not the same as being unfree.

Branch · Epistemology · Christian Platonism

Islamic Aristotelianism

Avicenna (Ibn Sînâ)

980 – 1037

The soul is distinct from the body.

Imagine you wake suspended in midair, blindfolded, every sense numb. You feel no body at all. Do you still know that you exist? Avicenna says yes — and reasons that the self therefore cannot be identical to the body. Six centuries later Descartes would repeat the move almost exactly. Modern brain science complicates the conclusion (damaged brains produce damaged selves), but the experiment is still worth running on yourself. It pulls apart what feels welded together and shows how much of your identity is body-based and how much is something else. Most existential questions live in that gap.

Branch · Metaphysics · Arabic Aristotelianism

The Ontological Argument

St. Anselm

1033 – 1109

Just by thinking about God we can know he exists.

Anselm's argument has fascinated philosophers for a thousand years because it is so clearly clever, and so unclear whether it works. Define God as the greatest conceivable being. If God existed only in your mind, you could conceive of something greater — namely the same being, but real. So God must exist. Critics from Kant onward have argued that existence is not the kind of property you can build into a definition. The deeper lesson is portable: arguments can be valid (the steps follow correctly) without being sound (the conclusion is actually true). Train yourself to feel the difference. Many persuasive pitches collapse the moment you notice that the conclusion was smuggled into the premises.

Branch · Philosophy of Religion

Islamic Philosophy

Averroes (Ibn Rushd)

1126 – 1198

Philosophy and religion are not incompatible.

Averroes proposed a domestic peace between faith and reason. Scripture speaks in images because it must reach everyone; the educated have a duty to read it philosophically, taking literal claims as poetry whenever reason demands. The move generalizes well beyond religion. Any inherited story — national, family, professional — can be honored at the level of meaning while being challenged at the level of fact. You do not have to choose between reverence and honesty. Most "either/or" cultural fights are arguments that should have been "both, at different levels."

Branch · Arabic Aristotelianism

Jewish Philosophy

Moses Maimonides

1135 – 1204

God has no attributes.

Any positive description shrinks its subject to the size of a thing you can describe. Better, Maimonides argued, to say only what God is not. The technique of negative theology is borrowable for anything genuinely large — love, grief, a person you actually know well. Confident positive descriptions of these tend to flatten and falsify. Try negation instead. "Not what I expected," "not reducible to that other thing," "not what people usually call it" — these often get closer than the confident definitions ever do. The most important things resist direct naming and reveal themselves by what they refuse to be.

Branch · Philosophy of Religion

Sufism · Persia

Jalal ad-Din Rumi

1207 – 1273

Don't grieve. Anything you lose comes round in another form.

Rumi watched a continuous flow of life: minerals into plants, plants into animals, animals into people, and outward. Loss in one form is appearance in another. You do not have to share his metaphysics to use the consolation. The molecules that made a friend you lost are still in the world, in soil and air and other bodies. The lessons they taught you live on in your choices. Grief is real, but the picture of total annihilation that fuels its worst nights is not the only picture available — and walking back from it lets you grieve without being crushed.

Tradition · Islamic · Sufism

Scholasticism · The Synthesis

Thomas Aquinas

c. 1225 – 1274

The universe has not always existed.

Aquinas refused the easy path of dismissing positions he disagreed with. Aristotle's logic for an eternal universe was sound given his premises; the Bible's claim of a beginning was true on faith; and Aquinas showed that both could stand. God could just as well have created an eternal universe as a starting one. The model is invaluable for any disagreement: do not destroy the strongest version of the position you oppose. Find what survives an honest reading, make peace with that part, and reserve your disagreement for what really matters. You will be right more often, and you will keep more allies.

Branch · Metaphysics · Christian Aristotelianism

German Mysticism

Nikolaus von Kues

1401 – 1464

God is the not-other.

Cusa's gambit is to describe the ultimate reality only by what it is not. Even calling God "a being" makes God one item among many. Better to call God the Not-Other — that through which everything else has its existence. The technique transfers. Try defining what it means to be a person, or to love someone, only by negation, and you often get further than by listing positive traits. The most fundamental things resist direct naming, and gain in clarity from being approached sideways.

Branch · Philosophy of Religion

Renaissance Humanism

Desiderius Erasmus

1466 – 1536

To know nothing is the happiest life.

Half satire, half theology. Erasmus accused the learned of strangling living faith in fine distinctions, and praised the willingness to be foolish — to trust, to love, to act — as the actual human good. The point is sharper than it looks. Some people are paralyzed by needing to understand everything before they commit to anything; the studying becomes a substitute for the living. Erasmus' question to such a person: at what point will you have learned enough to start? If the honest answer is "never," the studying was always a kind of hiding.

Branch · Philosophy of Religion · Humanism

I am myself and my circumstances. — José Ortega y Gasset
1500 — 1750

Renaissance & the Age of Reason

✦

The Church loses its monopoly on truth. Science replaces dogma, humanity replaces God at the center of the picture, and philosophy splits into two rival schools — those who would build knowledge from reason alone, and those who would build it from experience.

Renaissance · Florence

Niccolò Machiavelli

1469 – 1527

The end justifies the means.

The most-quoted and most-misquoted line in political theory. Machiavelli was not endorsing cruelty for its own sake; he was reporting what actually keeps states intact. Mercy at the wrong moment kills more people than firmness. Goodness against bad actors is suicide. The hard lesson is that political ethics cannot import private virtues uncritically: a leader is responsible for outcomes, not just intentions, and what is right for an individual may be reckless for someone holding power for others. You do not have to accept the trade-off, but you should see clearly what is being traded. Most "principled" politics that fails has refused to look.

Branch · Political Philosophy · Realism

Renaissance Humanism

Michel de Montaigne

1533 – 1592

Fame and tranquillity can never be bedfellows.

Fame requires the constant attention of strangers, and the price is your own peace. Montaigne is not opposed to ambition; he is reporting the actual mathematics. You cannot care intensely what others think of you and also be at ease — pick one. The corollary is liberating. Most of what you call "how others see me" is in fact a story you tell yourself about an audience that is mostly not watching. Reclaim that attention and spend it on what you actually value. The hours saved on imagined performance are startling.

Branch · Ethics · Humanism

Scientific Revolution · England

Francis Bacon

1561 – 1626

Knowledge is power.

Bacon broke with the scholarly habit of building philosophy out of older philosophy. Real knowledge comes from observing the world carefully, designing experiments, and discarding treasured theories when evidence demands. He named four "idols" — habits of mind that distort thinking: the biases of being human, the quirks of being yourself, the slipperiness of language, and the inherited authority of older theories. Audit your own thinking against the four occasionally. Most stuck arguments stem from one of them, and naming the idol usually unsticks the argument.

Branch · Philosophy of Science · Empiricism

Physicalism · England

Thomas Hobbes

1588 – 1679

Man is a machine.

Hobbes drew the conclusion others avoided. If everything in the universe is matter in motion, then so are we — thought, feeling, and will included. From this materialism came his politics: without a strong sovereign, rational self-interest leads each person to fear the others, and life becomes a war of all against all. So we trade some freedom for security. Modern liberal democracies still wrestle with the trade. Where exactly is the line between protection and oppression? Hobbes' uncomfortable answer: wherever it must be to keep the war from breaking out. Every concrete debate about state power is haggling over that line.

Branch · Metaphysics · Physicalism

The Father of Modern Philosophy · France

René Descartes

1596 – 1650

I think, therefore I am.

Descartes set himself a brutal exercise: doubt everything you can possibly doubt. The senses can deceive. Mathematics could be the work of an evil deceiver. The whole external world might be a dream. What survives? Only this — the doubting itself is happening, so the doubter must exist. The technique is worth borrowing even if you reject the conclusions. When confused or panicked, ask: what here am I absolutely certain of? You almost always have some solid ground, and the project is to extend it carefully outward. Most paralysis comes from trying to defend an entire belief system at once instead of one piece at a time.

Branch · Epistemology · Rationalism

Jansenism · France

Blaise Pascal

1623 – 1662

Imagination decides everything.

Reason is not master in its own house. A judge in robes seems wise; the same person in pajamas does not. Pascal observed how much of what we call rational decision was actually anchored by a feeling first, with reasons recruited afterward. His famous wager about God is one application; the deeper insight is more general. When you find yourself with strong conviction, ask which came first — the argument or the inclination. If the inclination came first, your argument is doing publicity work, not analysis. Knowing this does not free you from imagination's pull, but it lets you spot it in operation and discount accordingly.

Branch · Philosophy of Mind · Voluntarism

Rationalism · Netherlands

Benedictus Spinoza

1632 – 1677

God is the cause of all things, which are in him.

There is only one substance, Spinoza argued, with infinitely many attributes; we call it God or Nature, indifferently. You, your mind, your body, every passing thought — all are modes of this single substance. The practical consequence is consoling. Nothing is genuinely separate. Your mind and your body are two aspects of the same thing, not warring tenants. Your freedom comes not from rebelling against necessity but from understanding it deeply enough to act in alignment with it. A grief or a joy you can name in detail loses much of its power to throw you. Spinoza's universe is austere but you are not a stranger here.

Branch · Metaphysics · Monism

Empiricism · England

John Locke

1632 – 1704

No man's knowledge here can go beyond his experience.

Locke argued that the mind at birth is a blank slate. No innate ideas, no inborn morality — everything we know we built from sensory experience and reflection on it. The political payoff was enormous. If minds are made, not born superior or inferior, then aristocracy, slavery, and rigid hierarchy lose their cosmic justification. Education becomes the central political act. Most of modern liberalism — natural rights, government by consent, religious toleration — traces back to this single picture of human beings as equal, learning creatures. When you find yourself arguing about whether some group is "naturally" suited to a role, Locke's question lands: are you sure that is nature speaking rather than the absence of equal upbringing?

Branch · Epistemology · Empiricism

Rationalism · Germany

Gottfried Leibniz

1646 – 1716

There are two kinds of truths: truths of reasoning and truths of fact.

Some statements (a triangle has three sides) are necessarily true; denying them is self-contradiction. Others (it rained yesterday) are contingently true; they could have been otherwise. The distinction is one of the most useful tools in clear thinking. Many disputes confuse the two — treating a contingent matter as if it were necessary, or vice versa. "Successful people are X" sounds necessary but is contingent and revisable. "Contradictions cannot both be true" sounds contingent but is necessary. Sort any claim into the right column before arguing about it, and the argument usually gets shorter.

Branch · Epistemology · Rationalism

Idealism · Ireland

George Berkeley

1685 – 1753

To be is to be perceived.

Everything you ever experience is an idea in your mind — colors, sounds, the feel of this page. So what grounds do you have for believing in "matter" as something behind the ideas? Berkeley drew the radical conclusion: there is no matter, only minds and ideas. You can reject the theology that keeps his world stable and still keep the puzzle. We talk easily about a world independent of all observers, but every account of it is itself an observation. The gap between "the world as it is" and "the world as anyone could possibly know it" may be smaller than it looks — which is humbling whenever you are sure you have the situation pegged.

Branch · Metaphysics · Idealism

Doubt is not a pleasant condition, but certainty is absurd. — Voltaire
1750 — 1900

The Age of Revolution

✦

Old orders crumble. The American and French revolutions overturn kings; the Industrial Revolution overturns society itself. Philosophers ask new questions about liberty, equality, history, and the human mind — and German Idealism reshapes the map of thought.

The Enlightenment · France

Voltaire

1694 – 1778

Doubt is not a pleasant condition, but certainty is absurd.

Almost every fact you were taught with confidence as a child has since been revised. Almost every dogma you cling to now will be. Voltaire is not recommending paralysis — he wants you to hold beliefs as best guesses, ready to be improved, and to grant the same provisional status to your opponents. The corollary is his other great commitment: defend the right of someone you disagree with to speak. Not because they are right but because you might be wrong, and you will never discover the error by silencing them. A working principle for any disagreement that matters.

Branch · Epistemology · Scepticism

Empiricism's High Point · Scotland

David Hume

1711 – 1776

Custom is the great guide of human life.

Why do you believe the sun will rise tomorrow? Not because reason proves that it must — Hume showed it does not. You believe because it always has, and habit hardens into expectation. The deeper implication: causation itself is something you project onto repeated sequences, not something you ever directly observe. Almost all everyday knowledge runs on this habit-engine, including most science. The lesson is not to distrust it — it works — but to notice how thin its rational basis really is, and to be generous when other people rely on their own habits rather than on your logic. You are doing the same thing they are.

Branch · Epistemology · Empiricism

The Social Contract · Geneva

Jean-Jacques Rousseau

1712 – 1778

Man was born free, yet everywhere he is in chains.

Civilization is the deal: in exchange for safety and goods you accept inequality, dependency, and a thousand subtle coercions. Rousseau did not think we could undo it, but he proposed a remedy. A legitimate state is one where the laws come from the general will of all citizens and apply equally to all. You are free not when you obey no rules, but when you have a real voice in making the rules you obey. Apply the test to any institution you live inside — workplace, government, family. Do those subject to it actually shape it? If not, the chains are visible, and so is the work of unbinding them.

Branch · Political Philosophy

Economics · Scotland

Adam Smith

1723 – 1790

Man is an animal that makes bargains.

No animal trades. Humans do, constantly, and from this single fact Smith built modern economics. When each party pursues self-interest in an open market, an unplanned coordination emerges that no central planner could match. But Smith was no apologist for cruelty. He warned that repetitive factory labor would degrade workers, that monopolies would corrupt the market, and that businessmen left alone would conspire against the public. The working balance he points to is still the right one: markets are remarkable engines for coordination, and they need ethical and regulatory limits to keep doing the job. Either half of the lesson, alone, gets the economy wrong.

Branch · Political Philosophy · Economics

Transcendental Idealism · Königsberg

Immanuel Kant

1724 – 1804

There are two worlds: our bodies and the external world.

Kant's Copernican turn: we do not simply see the world as it is, we see it as our minds are built to construct it. Space, time, and basic categories like cause and substance are the lenses you cannot remove; reality in itself lies forever beyond them. From this came his ethics, which gives you a workable test for any action. Ask: could I will that everyone act on this principle? Could everyone lie when it is convenient? No — the practice of lying depends on most people not doing it, so universalizing collapses it. The test sounds abstract but is brutal in practice: most rationalizations for personal exceptions fall apart the moment you try to make the exception the rule.

Branch · Metaphysics · Transcendental Idealism

Conservatism · Britain

Edmund Burke

1729 – 1797

Society is indeed a contract.

But not the kind you sign and tear up at will. Society, said Burke, is a partnership between the living, the dead, and those yet to be born. Institutions carry slow knowledge — about what kinds of arrangement actually let people live together — that no one generation could rederive from scratch. Reform yes; sweeping revolution almost never. A working test for any large proposed change: do you understand why the existing arrangement is set up the way it is? If not, the chance you are about to rediscover a known failure is high. Many revolutions repeat the mistakes they were certain they had outgrown.

Branch · Political Philosophy

Utilitarianism · England

Jeremy Bentham

1748 – 1832

The greatest happiness for the greatest number.

Judge every action and policy by one yardstick: does it produce more pleasure than pain, across everyone affected, counted equally? Bentham's calculus sounds crude until you try it. All the best Marxist analyses are always analyses of a failure.

Žižek's needle: the political left has gotten so good at explaining why every revolution failed that the explanation itself becomes the consolation. As long as the better society remains hypothetical, it cannot be tested, and the analysis can preserve its moral purity. The same trap operates outside Marxism — in any movement that keeps its ideal so far in the future that it never has to deliver. The harder question to ask of any politics, including your own: what would you actually do if you won? If the answer is vague, the program may be designed to lose.
