— The Core —

The Seven Elements of Improvisation

The soft skills — the willingness to listen, forge trusting relationships, take responsible risks, adapt to change, and stay positive in the face of adversity — are no longer merely nice to have. They are paramount. And they can be learned.

01

Yes, And

Two words that form the bedrock of all improvisation. Creative breakthroughs occur where ideas are not just explored, but heightened and stretched. You don't have to act on every idea — but you do have to give every idea a chance to be acted on.

Affirm & Build
02

Ensemble

Stars are born out of ensembles — never sacrificing the greater needs of the whole group. Stars emerge when all members address the main enemies: the need to be right, the need to steal focus, and the need to appear in control.

Together is Better
03

Co-Creation

Dialogues push stories further than monologues. The sum of co-creation is greater than its parts. Use improv methodologies to co-create new content, new products, new campaigns, new processes — and new relationships between departments.

Make It Mutual
04

Authenticity

"Dare to offend." Strong leaders and organizations acknowledge what's not working. By allowing team members to air grievances and call out the truth, managers can learn and grow. Inject a healthy amount of irreverence into your culture.

Speak True
05

Failure

Failure is not an option — it is a requirement. By deflating the negative power of failure, you erode fear and allow creativity to flourish. Bake failure into any creative process so individuals aren't paralyzed when it happens, but see it as necessary.

Fall Into the Crack
06

Follow the Follower

Leadership is more about understanding status than maintaining status. The flexibility to allow any member to assume leadership for as long as their expertise is needed, and then to shuffle the hierarchy again once needs change.

Lead & Be Led
07

Listening

There is a huge difference between listening to understand and listening while waiting for the chance to respond. One enriches and broadens your perspective. The other feeds your need to be right. The care and feeding of the listening muscle is an absolute priority.

Hear the Whole
∞

The Takeaway

At The Second City, we don't teach you how to be funny. You learn to tap into the part of your brain that censors the truth for fear of being judged. When people feel free to express themselves without inhibition — that's when the funny happens.

ChapterOne

The Business of Funny

The Lansdowne Conference Center in Leesburg, Virginia, is a fine place to hold a business meeting. Yet every January for the past thirteen years, Lansdowne rocks with laughter brought on by actors from The Second City who come to help about a hundred Major League Baseball rookies adapt to the unusual challenges of life in The Bigs.

Major League Baseball brings in Second City talent who perform custom-written comedy vignettes, facilitate conversations, and teach improv-based communications skills. Make 'em laugh. Make 'em think. A winning formula not only for baseball rookies, but for education reformers, cruise line directors, and the millions of professionals Second City has reached.

Despite all the planning, processes, controls, and governance, business is one big act of improvisation.

When The Second City first opened its doors on a snowy December night in 1959 — housed in a converted Chinese laundry — few suspected they were present at the birth of an institution that would serve as the leading source of cutting-edge comic artistry for the next half century.

ChapterTwo

Yes, And — How to Make Something Out of Nothing

A young HR manager (we'll call her Katie) was chosen for her company's "High Potentials" program — required to rotate into new jobs every six months. Bright, skilled, highly motivated, but somewhat introverted and terrified by the prospect of joining new teams. So we had her play a game called Exposure.

What improvisation does, in its simplest form, is take the focus off ourselves and allow us to dial down our personal judgment. When we're concentrating hard and fully present in the moment, there's no room for self-consciousness.

Yes, And: It is the secret sauce, the source code, the key that unlocks every door worth opening.

One actor offers an idea onstage and other actors affirm and build onto that idea with something of their own. "Wow, I've never seen so many stars in the sky." → "I know. Things look so different up here on the moon."

That simple affirmation gives the first actor information to build on and opens up a great many possibilities. Yes, And is the lens through which we view our entire business and our place in the world.

ChapterThree

How to Build an Ensemble

A team implies competition — sides in a game. An ensemble carries no such baggage; it is a thing unto itself, an entity that is only its true self when its members are performing as one.

At The Second City, we believe in building ensembles, not teams. The high-functioning ensemble is a gift to both management and the workforce — a finely tuned unit, capable of generating and executing ideas autonomously, yet also in sync with the business as a whole.

Stars are born out of ensembles — without ever sacrificing the greater needs of the whole group.

The three keys: Be in the Moment (focus on what's right in front of you), Give and Take (share focus in equal measure), and Surrender the Need to Be Right (the biggest barrier to ensemble creativity).

ChapterFour

The Co-Creation Story, or Audiences Want In on the Act

For our 1998 revue The Psychopath Not Taken, the ensemble developed a scene that began with Superman entering in a wheelchair — just a couple of years after Christopher Reeve had been paralyzed. The audience groaned. Director Mick Napier didn't cut the scene; he transformed it, writing a song that broke the fourth wall and created a poignant commentary on celebrity and irony.

We listen to their reactions — their laughter, for sure, but also their groans and even their silences.

Co-creation requires three things: Find the idea, not your idea. Cede control. Eradicate fear. Whether you're developing comedy, marketing slogans, or efficiency on the factory floor, co-creation provides remarkable insight — and ensures you're always giving customers what they want, even when they can't tell you what that is.

ChapterFive

Change Is Hard — Comedy & Improv Make It Easier

Anne Libera identifies three essential elements of comedy: recognition, pain, and distance. When we made fun of everything aboard Norwegian Cruise Line — the tiny cabins, the long buffet lines, the bad weather — audiences ate it up. There is power in allowing yourself to look foolish. There is power in addressing the elephant in the room.

You can't change behaviors until you change attitudes — and you can't change attitudes until people are paying attention.

Replace revere with respect. Respect demands you consider the other. Reverence makes idolatry. The line between them isn't easy to see — but individuals and companies who inject a healthy amount of irreverence into their culture not only improve morale, they set the foundation for being more competitive.

ChapterSix

Using Failure

Failing isn't that bad; it's the fear of failure that can be paralyzing. We've failed spectacularly — a Royster Oyster (an oysterburger that caused food poisoning), a theatre opening with no advertising, a sales-training video series gone over budget, and three failed announcements of a Montreal theatre that never materialized.

Sometimes you have to accumulate a series of failures in order to finally succeed.

The six ways to embrace failure: Fail in public. Fail together. Fail fast. Fail free of judgment. Fail with confidence. Fail incrementally. The biggest fear is that of failure. Companies that model fearlessness — by creating low-risk opportunities for employees to try things — see tremendous increases in productivity, morale, and innovation.

ChapterSeven

Follow the Follower

Peter Drucker, the Austrian-born management consultant, championed flat structures and knowledge workers. Viola Spolin, the pioneer of American improvisational theatre, proposed "Follow the Follower" as a dynamic way to provide leadership inside an ensemble. They were saying the same thing.

Build the right sandbox, hire the right people, and get the hell out of the way. — Andrew Alexander

When managers can access and apply the best ideas of the individuals they oversee, they gain a strategic advantage. Studies show today's leadership traits — collaboration, listening, building relationships — are more commonly found in women. Tina Fey: "There is not one management course in the world where they recommend self-righteousness as a tool."

ChapterEight

Listening Is a Muscle

According to Glenn Lopis, 85% of what we know we learn through listening, and 45% of our workdays are spent listening — yet humans only listen at a 25% comprehension rate. More alarming: only 2% of professionals have ever taken any formal training in listening skills.

None of us listen enough, do we, dear? We only listen to a fraction of what people say. It's a wonderfully useful thing to do. You almost always hear something you didn't expect. — Father Joe

Great listening is at the core of great improvisation. Improvisers go beyond mere active listening — every word your scene partners utter is a gift, a lifeline, because it offers the ensemble something to build on. Listening keeps you in the moment, not looking backward or jumping three steps ahead.

If more people listened with empathy, there would be fewer misunderstandings, leading to less conflict. Just imagine the possibilities inherent in large groups of people actively, purposefully, professionally listening to one another. That's a world we want to live in.

— The Appendix —

Improv Exercises to Practice

Seventeen exercises distilled from The Second City's training room — tools for building focus, ensemble, co-creation, leadership, and listening.

01

Exposure

Focus

Two lines face each other. Stand and look. Once discomfort sets in, count the bricks on a wall. Focus rids you of fear and being in your head.

02

Word at a Time Story

Yes, And

Six to ten people in a circle tell an original story — one word at a time. Affirm and build to a far more interesting story than any one mind alone.

03

Talk Without I

Ensemble

Pairs converse about anything — without ever using the word "I." Learn to frame ideas in ways more accessible and other-directed.

04

Mirror

Ensemble

Pairs face each other. One leads with face and body; the other mirrors. Switch. Then attempt mirroring with no leader assigned.

05

Give and Take

Ensemble

Spread out. Give focus to others with a physical cue. Then take focus. Then do both — give, take, give back. Order or chaos? Both teach.

06

Parts of a Whole

Ensemble

On a suggestion (animal, machine, restaurant), the group silently molds itself into the shape — each contributing a part to make the whole.

07

Take That Back

Here & Now

Improvise a scene. On a bell, the speaker must replace the last line with a new one and continue. No preplanning — only the moment.

08

Thank You Statues

Co-Creation

One person steps into a circle and strikes a pose. Others tap in, build on the pose. Risk and support — and a final, collaborative statue.

09

Emotional Option

Co-Creation

Pairs converse. A leader calls out emotions — angry, giddy, somber. The same words mean different things filtered through different emotions.

10

Follow the Follower

Leadership

A circle of people. Everyone makes sounds and movements while also mimicking everyone else. A school of fish. Leadership in constant flux.

11

Who's the Leader?

Leadership

One person stands center, eyes closed. The group silently chooses a leader to mimic. The center person opens her eyes and tries to identify them.

12

Silent Organization

Communication

Line up by age — without words. Then by favorite band. Then by start date. Lose language; find new ways to communicate.

13

String of Pearls

Listening

A first line and a last line are given. Players in between must connect the two with logical, improvised lines of narrative.

14

Repetition

Listening

Pairs converse one sentence at a time. Before responding, each repeats the partner's full sentence. Practice listening all the way through.

15

Last Word Response

Listening

Pairs converse. Each new sentence must begin with the last word the partner spoke. Set partners up for easy responses — be generous.

16

Touch & Eye Contact

Listening

Pairs talk — but each speaker can only speak after making physical contact or strong eye contact with their partner. A foundation for real connection.

17

Gibberish Games

Listening

Two improvise in made-up sounds. A third "translates" the conversation. Listen for intent — body language, tone — beyond words.
