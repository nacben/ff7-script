Research-backed pedagogies

Scaffolding frameworks that actually work.

Decades of research and classroom practice have produced powerful, well-tested approaches for teaching programming. These pedagogies appear throughout the book.

UMC

Use → Modify → Create

A learning progression where students first explore an existing program, then modify it bit by bit, before creating their own original work. Ownership gradually shifts from teacher to learner.

Chapter 23
PRIMM

Predict · Run · Investigate · Modify · Make

A carefully sequenced approach where students first predict what code does (without running it), then explore, modify, and finally create their own. Builds code reading habits and conceptual understanding.

Chapter 23
TIPPSEE

Title · Instructions · Purpose · Play · Sprites · Explore · Extend

A reading-comprehension–inspired strategy for learning through example projects in Scratch. Adapted from Use-Modify-Create to give students a clear roadmap for exploration.

Chapter 23
UDL

Universal Design for Learning

Plan instruction proactively for the widest range of learners. Provide multiple means of engagement, representation, and expression — maximizing student strengths while reducing instructional barriers.

Chapter 21
CRP

Culturally Relevant Pedagogy

Connect computing to students' cultural assets and lived experiences. Empower learners to become change agents and build authentic relationships with families and communities.

Chapter 12
PP

Pair Programming

Two students share one computer, trading roles as "driver" and "navigator." Builds collaboration, communication, and code quality — and helps every student feel they belong in CS.

Chapter 16
WE

Worked Examples & Subgoal Labels

Present a problem, a complete solution, and a clear explanation. Group steps into named subgoals to reduce cognitive load and help students see the structure of a solution before writing their own.

Chapter 23
SW

Semantic Waves

Move learners between everyday language and abstract technical concepts. Unpack a concept using metaphors and unplugged activities, then repack it back into precise programming vocabulary.

Chapter 7
Powerful ideas inside

What students actually struggle with — and how to help.

Beneath every working program lies a forest of misconceptions and partial understandings. The book is candid about what trips students up — and what to do about it.

V

Variables aren't boxes

The familiar “box” metaphor for variables produces stubborn misconceptions: that boxes hold multiple values, never lose old ones, or contain expressions. A better mental model: a small whiteboard that holds exactly one value, erased when a new one is written.

From Chapter 14 · Naive Conceptions
B

Plan before you program

Four levels of planning: Problem (what's the idea?), Algorithm (in everyday language), Program (in actual code), Execution (debugging behavior). Moving between these levels gives students a roadmap and prevents the “just-start-coding” trap.

From Chapter 2 · Before You Program, Plan!
T

Bugs are the curriculum

Debugging isn't an obstacle to the lesson — it is the lesson. Build a classroom culture that celebrates errors: hold up a “buggy wall,” share favorite mistakes of the day, and teach systematic strategies for locating and fixing problems.

From Chapter 20 · Testing and Debugging
Y

Working code isn't enough

Good habits of programming: meaningful variable names, proper initialization, well-placed comments, no duplicated code, no unreachable blocks, testing with a range of inputs. The goal isn't a program that runs — it's a program that anyone can read.

From Chapter 25 · Good Habits of Programming
O

Expressions are trees

Every expression has a hidden tree structure. Visualizing nested operations as “circles of evaluation” or node-link diagrams reveals the order of evaluation — and exposes common misconceptions like comparing booleans to true or assuming nested calls evaluate outside-in.

From Chapter 15 · Operators and Expressions
G

Unplugged comes first

Before code, before screens — start with the everyday. Role-play variable assignment with boxes and shredders. Simulate searching with a divide-and-conquer game. Build the concept in the body, then bridge it back to the technical language of programming.

From Chapter 7 · Guided Exploration
The deeper learning framework

Programming teaches more than programming.

Successful programming classrooms attend to three interconnected domains of student growth — not just content knowledge, but also collaboration skills and the mindsets that fuel resilient learning.

C

Cognitive

Deep content knowledge, critical thinking, problem-solving, computational thinking — algorithms, variables, debugging.

I

Interpersonal

Collaboration and communication. Expressing ideas, working in pairs and teams, sharing work, giving feedback.

I

Intrapersonal

Beliefs, mindsets, persistence, self-efficacy. The growth mindset that lets students persevere through bugs and frustration.

Common student misconceptions

What teachers need to watch out for.

Students don't arrive as blank slates. They bring intuitions from everyday language and mathematics that can lead them astray. Knowing the most common misconceptions lets you address them head-on.

01

The “human interpreter” problem

Students expect computers to fill in obvious steps, interpret intent, or take “reasonable” defaults — because that's how humans communicate. Computers, of course, do not.

02

Assignment isn't equality

From mathematics, students bring = as equality. In code, a = b means: evaluate b, then store the value in a. Right to left. Replacing, not equating.

03

The continuous-checking myth

“While the cat is touching red, do X.” Students often assume the condition is monitored continuously and the loop exits the instant it becomes false. The condition is only checked at the top of each iteration.

04

Boundary and off-by-one errors

Loops that iterate one too many or too few times. Conditions that miss edge cases. Mishandled empty lists or division by zero. The classic Rainfall problem — average a list of numbers, stop at 99999 — has tripped up novices for decades.

05

Mixing up print and return

Many beginners can't articulate why a function should return a value rather than print it. Building functions that return values — then composing them — is one of the harder pedagogical leaps in introductory programming.

06

Fine-grained, bottom-up coding

Block-based environments make it easy to write many small disconnected scripts, each attached to its own trigger. The result: spaghetti code that loses logical coherence. Teach students to plan from the top down and use structured loops.

How to use the book

For every teacher, at every stage.

New to teaching programming?

Read the book in its entirety. It will give you an enduring foundation in what you're teaching and how to teach it. Begin with four overarching “umbrella” chapters: K (Learning Goals), J (Programming Languages), N (Naive Conceptions), and F (Formative Feedback).

Tip: Pair each concept chapter with a pedagogy chapter. Read about variables, then read about scaffolding strategies. Read about loops, then read about debugging.

Already teaching CS?

Use the book as a reference. Read a chapter over a coffee break. Pull it off the shelf when planning a unit. You'll discover new ideas and have plenty of “aha” moments from the examples and pedagogical tips shared by 40+ experienced teachers and researchers.

Tip: The chapters reference each other extensively. Each chapter cross-references related concepts and pedagogies, so you can follow your curiosity through the book.

A curriculum designer?

Use the book to inform what topics to teach, in what order, and with what supports. Each chapter discusses what students typically struggle with — invaluable when designing a coherent learning progression for elementary, middle, or high school.

Tip: The book is programming-language agnostic. Examples appear in Scratch, Snap!, Python, Java, JavaScript, App Inventor, and more — but the foundational ideas transcend any single language.

A teacher trainer?

The book is ideal as a text in K–12 CS teacher-training programs. Each chapter is designed to be accessible and friendly to all teachers, drawing on research but written for practical, actionable use in real classrooms.

Tip: The book transcends geographical boundaries. With authors from the US, UK, Canada, Finland, Switzerland, Germany, and Australia, the ideas are deliberately portable across school systems.
