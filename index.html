Part One · The Basics

Google is a librarian
with a long memory.

Pay-per-click advertising is exactly what it sounds like: advertisers only pay when someone clicks. But Google Ads — formerly Google AdWords — is the most powerful PPC system on the planet because Google itself is the closest thing to publicly available AI. It's a machine that thinks.

And it knows you. Probably better than you know yourself.

Google
knows
everything

The library analogy.

Google is the librarian. The internet is the library. Every webpage, video, product listing, and news article is a book on a shelf — and the librarian's job is to collect, sort, and personally guide every reader to exactly what they need.

This librarian is observant. They notice what you're wearing. They know your checkout history. They remember every book you returned with a sigh. They watch what other readers like you have picked up.

Google's number one priority is relevancy. Everything else flows from that one obsession.

Google is much more than a search engine.

Every branch of the Google ecosystem is collecting a different kind of intimate signal. Together, they paint a picture sharp enough to predict intent — and that means putting your ad in front of someone who's ready to buy, before they even know they want to.

The Ads Auction

You're not bidding
against each other.

This is the part most advertisers misunderstand. There's a fixed number of ad slots per search, and yes — advertisers place bids. But the highest bidder doesn't automatically win. And when they do win, they don't even pay what they bid.

You're not bidding against your competitors. You're bidding for the value of the click.

Your slot. Your price.
Step 01

The Googler enters a query.

"Red shoes." A user types it, taps it, or whispers it to their phone. Google instantly takes inventory.

Step 02

The advertisers are weighed.

Every bid is compared with quality scores, extensions, landing-page relevance, and the searcher's own profile and context.

Step 03

The slots are filled.

In under a second, ads are sorted by Ad Rank. The winner pays only what's needed to beat #2. Sometimes #2 is the best place to be.

★

Quality Score: the only score you can see.

Graded 1–10, your quality score reflects expected CTR, ad relevance, and landing page experience. Not your Ad Rank — but your tool for making it better. Higher quality means lower CPC and higher placement. Win twice.

Core Concepts

The flywheel
never ends.

Every successful Google Ads campaign rotates through the same four stages — forever. Skip a stage and you'll either run out of budget or run out of patience. The most counterintuitive part? You start by guessing. You'll be wrong as often as you're right. That's the whole point.

You aren't paying for customers up front. You're paying for data.

The Flywheel
01Assume
02Test
03Observe
04Optimize

From guess to gospel — in four turns.

01 Assume

Guess your audience, keywords, offer, and ad copy. Yes, guess. Even our worst assumptions teach us something.

02 Test

Launch the campaign. Resist the urge to tweak. Learning what doesn't work is just as valuable as learning what does.

03 Observe

Let it sit. Use the statistician's base-100 model: 100 impressions reveal CTR, 100 clicks reveal time-on-site. Don't pull the plug at 40.

04 Optimize

Now act on data, not feelings. Double down on what works. Cut what doesn't. Spawn new assumptions. Repeat forever.

Before You Spend a Dollar

Is Google Ads right
for your business?

For most businesses, the answer is yes — but it's not always, and it's never immediately. It takes time to build, launch, and optimize. It takes money to gather meaningful data. And it takes a business that's actually ready to handle what comes through the door.

Answer the seven honestly. If two or more are no, hold off.

1

Are you ready to commit?

It can take months to determine whether there's a predictable, scalable growth path.

2

Can you afford the cost?

$1,000–$1,500/mo bare minimum. We recommend starting at $2,000+ for meaningful data velocity.

3

Do you know your goals?

Close rates, leads-to-sale ratio, monthly leads needed for profit — these aren't optional.

4

Is your website fast and clear?

Under 2–3 second load time. Mobile responsive. Informative. Trust signals everywhere.

5

Can you handle the traffic?

Will you answer the phone? Is inventory stocked? Is the sales team trained?

6

Is your audience big enough?

Hyper-local, rural, niche-of-a-niche audiences may be too small for Google Ads to scale meaningfully.

7

Can you compete on spend?

If your bids can't compete in your industry, Google Ads won't tip the scales. Choose your battles.

Deep Dive

Pick your battlefield.

Lead generation, eCommerce, YouTube — each requires its own foundations, prerequisites, and campaign architecture. Choose your context. The strategy is built around it.

Lead generation: data first, leads second.

Your initial budget isn't buying customers. It's buying the signals Google needs to build the audience that will become customers. The first three months are the most painful. After Performance Max entered the mix, the old 90-day rule became more like 45 days just to see what you've built. Day 46 is now Day 0.

Before launch, you'll need these seven non-negotiables:

  • Reasonable monthly spend (we don't take clients under $2,000/mo)
  • Appropriate time expectations (45+ days before judging anything)
  • Quality website with depth, not a one-pager
  • Margins that can actually support paid traffic
  • Unique selling proposition (no, "cheapest" doesn't count)
  • Customer avatars — sub-types of your demographic, not demographics
  • 15-question minimum form fills to deter click farms

Then comes the build: the six key campaigns, conversion tracking via Google Tag Manager, ad extensions, RLSA observation lists, exclusion lists for past converters — and yes, manually scoring inbound phone calls so Google learns from real conversions, not random hang-ups.

eCommerce: the feed makes or breaks you.

For online stores, your product feed is the campaign. Out-of-stock items, broken pages, mismatched prices — any of these will sink performance fast. We use DataFeedWatch to optimize feeds before they reach Google Merchant Center, because static spreadsheets can't catch a 404 in real time.

Your nine prerequisites:

  • Reasonable monthly spend ($2k+ minimum)
  • Realistic time expectations (Day 46 is Day 0)
  • A quality website that does more than transact
  • Margins that survive rising CPCs
  • A unique selling proposition that isn't "cheaper"
  • No one-off products — single GTINs vanish forever
  • Customer avatars + bespoke creative for each
  • A robust, live, audited product data feed
  • Average order values that justify paid traffic (~$50+)

The campaign mix shifts: Standard Shopping (20%), Performance Max (15%), Dynamic Remarketing (10%), Search Brand (10%), Search Competitor (10%), DSA (20%), and expansion campaigns — YouTube, Discovery, Display — at 5% each. Performance Max wins on volume, Standard Shopping wins on transparency, and you need both.

YouTube: avatar-based, not intent-based.

Google Search is intent. A user with a problem is actively looking for a solution. YouTube is avatar — a user with an interest, but not necessarily ready right now. YouTube is the long-form sales letter of the internet: position yourself in front of the right audience and drip content over time. With the right business, it's extraordinarily effective.

The five ad types:

  • Skippable in-stream — pay only after 30s or a click
  • Non-skippable in-stream — up to 30s, charged per 1,000 impressions
  • Bumper ads — 6 seconds, unskippable, impression-billed
  • Video discovery — appears in search & related videos, click-billed
  • Overlay / non-video — banner ads on the bottom 20% of a video

The script is everything. Hook in the first six seconds. Address identity, agitate the problem, present the solution, dismantle objections, show proof, end with a clear CTA. Then upload five videos minimum — Google split-tests them against each other and pushes ~80% of spend to the winners.

Part Five · The New Paradigm

Performance Max
changes everything.

Between 2020 and 2022, Google ripped feature after feature out of the ads platform. Many of us thought they were trying to muscle out agencies entirely. We were wrong. They were phasing in something new — and on its surface, it looks deceptively simple.

Performance Max isn't lazy. It's leveraging Google's full ecosystem on your behalf. Lazy advertisers will fail with it. Strategic ones get separated from the pack.

i.

Goals

This is the first Google campaign that's truly goal-oriented. Define your primary conversion actions carefully — Google chases what you tell it to chase. Secondary goals report, but don't optimize.

ii.

Assets

You no longer create the ads. You provide asset groups — images, logos, videos, headlines, descriptions. Google mixes, matches, and deploys them across Search, Display, YouTube, Gmail, Discovery. URL Expansion picks the right landing page per user.

iii.

Audiences

Audience Signals are starting points — custom segments, customer data, interests, demographics. Google uses your signal as a hypothesis, then finds better audiences than you would have. The Insights tab tells you who's actually converting.

Old Google Ads

Keyword-centric. Network-targeted.

  • You picked the campaign type per network
  • You built ad groups around keywords
  • You manually targeted demographics
  • You decided which landing page received traffic
  • You chased Quality Score and Ad Rank
Performance Max

Goal-oriented. Audience-centric. Algorithmic.

  • One campaign reaches every Google surface
  • Asset groups replace ad groups, organized by audience
  • Audience signals seed discovery, not constraint
  • URL Expansion sends users to the highest-converting page
  • You give Google a goal — it finds the rest
⚡

The 500-touchpoint paradigm.

Google understands that on average, a user converts after roughly 500 brand touchpoints. So instead of forcing every click straight to a product page, URL Expansion routes users through whichever pages — "About," case studies, blog posts — actually move them toward conversion. Trust the machine. (But audit it.)

Part Six · Hiring Help

How to pick
the right agency.

Running Google Ads is a full-time job. Many businesses are better served by an outside expert than an in-house generalist trying to juggle SEO, content, email, and PPC simultaneously. But agencies vary wildly — some are strategists, most are account managers, and a few are flat-out criminal in how they bill.

Here's what to actually look for.

01

Certifications & Experience

Google Ads Certified is the floor. Google Partner — better. Premier Partner (top 3% in the U.S.) — best. Continued education matters more than the badge alone.

02

Real Tracking Stack

Google Analytics and Google Tag Manager — not one or the other. Plus call tracking, form tracking, predictive indicators, and manual conversion scoring for phone calls.

03

Transparent Reporting

Reports built in Google Data Studio (or similar) that reconcile cleanly with your Google account. If they use "proprietary dashboards," walk away — that's how vanity metrics get hidden.

04

Account Ownership

The Google account belongs to you. The agency is a guest. If they claim ownership, you'll lose every campaign asset the day you leave.

05

No Long-Term Contracts

Markets change. Algorithms change. Your business might change. A reputable agency earns its keep month-to-month and welcomes the pressure.

06

Diagnose Before Prescribe

No strategy should be proposed before they understand your business in depth. A doctor never prescribes without an exam. Neither should your agency.

07

Active In Your Account

Expect 100+ changes per month, especially in the first 90 days. Search terms reviewed monthly. Negative keywords updated. Real, visible work.

08

Specialists, Not Generalists

"Do-it-all" agencies do nothing well. A great Google Ads agency makes Google Ads their entire craft. The rest of your stack deserves its own experts.

!

Walk away if you hear...

Any one of these is a warning. More than one is a verdict.

  • Guaranteed results or promised revenue lifts
  • Long-term contracts with cancellation penalties
  • Proprietary dashboards you can't audit
  • Pricing that seems "too affordable to be real"
  • One person doing PPC + SEO + email + social
  • They want to own your Google Ads account
