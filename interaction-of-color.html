Introduction

Practice Before Theory

"Interaction of Color" is a record of an experimental way of studying and teaching color. It reverses the conventional academic order — placing practice before theory, which, after all, is the conclusion of practice.

The book does not begin with optics or physiology, nor with physics of light and wavelength. Just as knowledge of acoustics does not make one musical, no color system by itself can develop one's sensitivity for color.

"What counts here — first and last — is not so-called knowledge of so-called facts, but vision — seeing."

The aim of study is to develop — through experience, by trial and error — an eye for color. This means seeing color action as well as feeling color relatedness.

Practical exercises demonstrate through color deception (illusion) the relativity and instability of color. Experience teaches that in visual perception there is a discrepancy between physical fact and psychic effect.

The central insight: Color deceives continually. To use color effectively, we must recognize this — and learn to see it, feel it, exploit it.

Chapter I

Color Recollection & Visual Memory

If one says "Red" and there are 50 people listening, one can expect there will be 50 reds in their minds. And one can be sure all these reds will be very different — even when a specific red they've all seen countless times is named.

This reveals two important facts. First: it is hard, if not impossible, to remember distinct colors. Visual memory is very poor compared to auditory memory — which can often repeat a melody heard only once or twice.

Second: the nomenclature of color is most inadequate. Though there are innumerable colors — shades and tones — daily vocabulary contains only about 30 color names.

The Memory Test — Pick "Your" Red Interactive

Before looking at these swatches, picture "Coca-Cola red" in your mind. Now — which is it?

Albers: "Even if all listeners have hundreds of reds in front of them to choose from, they will select quite different colors."
Chapter IV

A Color Has Many Faces — The Relativity of Color

Albers opens this chapter with a haptic thought experiment: dip both hands into warm and cold water, then both into lukewarm. The lukewarm feels simultaneously cold and warm — a discrepancy between physical fact and psychic effect, a "haptic illusion."

The same principle governs color. The identical color, placed on different grounds, will be perceived as two entirely different colors. The influencing color is the ground; the influenced color is the figure.

The Same Color — Two Completely Different Appearances
The two center squares are identical — the same color of paper. Yet one appears markedly different from the other due solely to its ground.

The first exercise in Albers's class: make one and the same color look different. Students place two small rectangles of the same color on two very different grounds. The class then observes that:

  • Certain colors are hard to change
  • Others are highly susceptible to influence
  • Change is a result of influence — from both hue and light, simultaneously
"Color is the most relative medium in art."
"In order to use color effectively it is necessary to recognize that color deceives continually." Josef Albers, Interaction of Color
Chapter V

Lighter and/or Darker — Light Intensity

"If one is not able to distinguish the difference between a higher tone and a lower tone, one probably should not make music." Applied to color: almost everyone would prove incompetent. Very few can reliably distinguish higher and lower light intensity between different hues.

Albers tested his painting students repeatedly. The constant result: 60% of answers were wrong, only 40% correct — a disillusioning and revealing fact that should inspire humility in any colorist.

Which is Lighter? — Test Yourself

For each pair, decide which color is lighter (contains more white / less black). Then reveal.

The answers — check your intuitions. Counterintuitive? That's Albers's point. A saturated hue can make a darker color appear lighter than a lighter one of low saturation.

Gradation studies — collecting grey scales from magazine photographs — train a more discriminating sensitivity to light steps.
Chapter VI

1 Color Appears as 2 — Reversed Grounds

The first exercise was making one color look different. The next step goes further: make one color look like two — specifically, make it echo the two reversed grounds it sits upon simultaneously.

The task: find a single color that, placed on two contrasting grounds, appears to take on the character of each of those grounds in turn. The "chameleon color" must be topologically the middle between the two grounds.

3 Colors — Appearing as 4

The inner rectangles are identical. The ground shifts perception so dramatically that the middle color appears to "belong" to each opposite ground.

"1 color is to show 2 faces which refer to the 2 colors of the reversed grounds."
Chapter VII

2 Different Colors Look Alike — Subtraction of Color

The reverse task: where Chapter VI made one color look like two, Chapter VII makes two different colors look alike. The underlying mechanism is "subtraction" — any ground subtracts its own hue from colors it carries.

Place a red on a red ground: the red is absorbed — subtracted. Its distinguishing character disappears. The same subtractive logic applies to light: light grounds subtract light from colors placed upon them.

"Any ground subtracts its own hue from colors which it carries and therefore influences."
2 Very Different Colors — Made to Look Alike

Cover the side margin swatches. Focus on the centered small squares. Then uncover and see how different they actually are.

"2 different colors look alike" — 4 colors appear as 3. The factual difference is obliterated by the ground's subtractive action.
Chapter VIII

Why Color Deception? — After-Image & Simultaneous Contrast

Here is the cause of most color illusions. The nerve ends on the human retina (rods and cones) are tuned to receive any of three primary colors. Staring at red fatigues the red-sensitive parts, so when shifting to white (which contains red + yellow + blue), only yellow and blue remain active — producing green, the complement of red.

This psycho-physiological phenomenon proves that no normal eye, not even the most trained one, is foolproof against color deception.

Chapter IX

Color Mixture in Paper — Illusion of Transparence

Color papers cannot be mixed mechanically. Instead, Albers taught students to imagine mixtures — to find, from a collection of papers, a "descendant" that convincingly sits between two "parents." When arranged in overlapping shapes, a remarkable double deception appears:

  1. The mixture appears in the overlap zone
  2. The "mixture" paper loses its opacity — appears transparent or translucent
Illusory Transparence — Overlapping Color Papers
The center strip is a single, flat, opaque color — yet it appears to be a transparent overlay showing both parent colors through it.
Chapter X

Factual Mixtures — Additive and Subtractive

Additive — Light

When light is projected and mixed (as in theater, screens, physics), every overlap is lighter than either parent. The sum of all colors in light is white.

Additive mixture: RGB light. Overlaps become brighter. Sum → white.

Subtractive — Pigment

When pigments are mixed on a palette, every mixture absorbs more light. The more colors mixed, the darker the result. The sum approaches black.

Subtractive mixture: CMY pigments. Overlaps become darker. Sum → black.

"Mixtures gain light only in direct color, whereas mixtures of reflected colors lose light."
Chapter XIII

The Bezold Effect

Wilhelm von Bezold (1837–1907), a meteorologist, discovered this effect while searching for a way to change his rug designs entirely by adding or changing just one color. When a strong color is distributed evenly as an element (like mortar in brickwork), it changes the visual effect of the entire design.

The same red bricks laid with white mortar look light, airy, and warm. The same bricks with black mortar look heavy, cool, and completely different in character — as if a different color of brick.

Bezold Effect — Change One Color, Change Everything
The brick color is constant. Only the mortar changes. The entire perceived character — warmth, weight, light — shifts dramatically.

This same principle underlies the Impressionists' technique and modern 4-color printing, where tiny dots of a few base colors create the illusion of unlimited color through optical mixture.

Chapter XIV

Color Intervals and Transformation

"Good morning to you" can be sung in any key, by any voice, on any instrument — and remains instantly recognizable because the intervals between the four notes remain constant. The same principle applies to color.

Colors have a "distance" from each other in both light intensity and hue. A tetrachord of four colors can be "transposed" to a higher or lower key — just as a musical phrase is transposed — while preserving the relational intervals.

Chapter XVI

Color Juxtaposition — Harmony — Quantity

Color systems promise harmony through "correct" combinations — complementaries, triads, tetrads. Albers challenges this orthodoxy directly. Such rules are "worn out." No mechanical system is flexible enough to precalculate the manifold changing factors of real application.

"Good painting, good coloring, is comparable to good cooking. Even a good cooking recipe demands tasting and repeated tasting while it is being followed. And the best tasting still depends on a cook with taste."

What Albers proposes instead: study quantity. The same four colors, distributed in different amounts and frequencies, create entirely different moods, climates, temperatures — so different that the same cast appears as four different performances.

Schopenhauer's insight

Schopenhauer improved Goethe's color circle by assigning different quantities to different hues — proportional to their light intensity. Yellow (lightest) receives the smallest area; violet (darkest) the largest. Balance through weight, not through equality.

Quantity proportional to darkness — visual weight balance

Quantity as quality

Albers believed any color "goes" with any other color — if their quantities are appropriate. There are no inherently bad color combinations, only inappropriate proportions and insensitive handling.

The theatrical parallel: A set of 4 colors is a "cast." Varying their areas and recurrence creates 4 different "performances" — different scenes, different moods — with the identical cast.

Quantity Study — Same 4 Colors, 4 Different Climates
Same colors throughout. Only area and placement shift. "Changes in climate or temperature, in tempo or rhythm."
Chapter XVII

Film Color and Volume Color — 2 Natural Effects

Film Color

Distant mountains appear uniformly blue — regardless of whether covered with green trees or bare rock. The sky at sunset turns the sun full red. A white ceiling near a green lawn appears bright green from reflected light.

Film color appears as a thin, transparent, translucent layer between eye and object — independent of the object's actual surface color. It is a physical phenomenon, not psychological.

Volume Color

Coffee in a thin glass stem looks lighter than coffee in a cup, which looks lighter than coffee in a silex glass — though all hold the same liquid. Tea appears lighter in a spoon than in a cup.

Volume color exists in 3-dimensional transparent fluids. The more fluid, the darker the color. Milk — being opaque — does not behave this way. Many watercolors behave as volume colors; oil paint, gouache, pastel produce surface color.

Film Color — Swimming Pool Effect
↓ Deeper = More Blue
Equal step depth → the equal amounts of blue added appear in decreasing perceptual increments, following the Weber-Fechner Law.
Chapter XX

The Weber-Fechner Law — The Measure in Mixture

M. E. Chevreul instructed: add equal amounts of black ink layer by layer to produce a graduated grey scale. The result seems obvious — but it fails to produce visually equal steps. The surprise is that physical arithmetic does not translate to perceptual arithmetic.

Weber (1804–1891) and Fechner (1801–1887) formulated the law:

"The visual perception of an arithmetical progression depends upon a physical geometric progression."

To see steps of equal apparent height, each successive step must be twice the previous physical quantity. The mind "straightens" the geometric curve into the appearance of even steps.

Arithmetical vs. Geometric Progression Which looks more even?

Steps appear decreasing — the scale compresses
Geometric (×2 each step)
Layers: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64
Steps appear equal — perceptually even
Color Gradation — Arithmetic (top) vs. Geometric (bottom)
This law operates across all perception: sound, weight, temperature, light. It is why a candle in daylight is barely noticed, but one candle in darkness is brilliant.

The Weber-Fechner Law is almost unknown among colorists — yet it is crucial in astronomy, acoustics, and psychology. It explains why equal physical additions produce diminishing perceptual returns, and why the swimming pool steps appear to become softer as they deepen.

Chapter XXI

From Color Temperature to Humidity in Color

In Western tradition, blue is cool and the yellow-orange-red group is warm. But these are relative — not absolute. There are warm blues and cool reds within their own hue families. And when temperature indicators are combined with neutrals and intermediate hues, personal interpretations diverge wildly.

Color temperature chains through a remarkable set of perceptual associations: light-dark → light-heavy → soft-hard → quick-slow → young-old → warm-cool → wet-dry → here-there.

Chapter XXII

Vibrating Boundaries — Enforced Contours

When two colors are contrasting in hue but similar in light intensity, a disturbing phenomenon occurs: the boundary between them appears to vibrate, duplicate, or glow. Shadow appears on one side, a reflected light on the other.

This effect is felt as aggressive and uncomfortable. It is rarely used except in advertising seeking a screaming effect — hence its association with discomfort and dislike.

Vibrating Boundaries — Contrasting Hue, Similar Lightness

If you wear glasses, try with and without. Try close focus and far focus. The vibration may appear differently. Some people perceive it strongly; others less so.

The physio-psychological function of this effect has not been fully clarified — it is perceived differently by different people, with or without glasses, near and far.
Chapter XXIII

Equal Light Intensity — Vanishing Boundaries

The opposite of vibrating boundaries: when two adjacent colors share exactly equal light intensity, the boundary between them nearly vanishes. This is among the most surprising, delicate, and difficult effects in color — and the last exercise in Albers's course.

Albers describes finding this effect in nature: cumulus clouds seen against blue sky. The shaded grey undersides of the clouds — exactly matching the blue's light level — merge seamlessly: "we do not see where clouds end and where sky begins."

"Very few people — including many colorists and painters — have ever seen 2 adjacent colors of true equal light value."

Chapter XXV

On Teaching Color

Albers's pedagogical philosophy was radical: not "theory, then practice" — but practice leading to theory. The color studies are "admission tickets" to class: homework tried, brought in, compared, evaluated by all. The best examples are praised for "psychological engineering" — convincing presentation that eliminates misleading reading.

→

Trial and Error

Students are given a task without being told how to do it. Failure is productive — it reveals what doesn't work and why.

→

Comparison

Seeing means comparing. Studies exhibited together allow the whole class to see differences that individual work cannot reveal.

→

Disliked Colors

"A special effort in using disliked colors ends with our falling in love with them." Prejudice dissolves through experience.

→

Self-Education

"All thorough study is basic, and all education is self-education." The teacher's enthusiasm counts more than their knowledge.

"Besides, good teaching is more a giving of right questions than a giving of right answers."

Gestaltungstrieb

Albers distinguished the primitive drive to be occupied (Beschäftigungstrieb) from the more evolved drive to be productive and creative (Gestaltungstrieb). Education means moving students from the first to the second.

Variants vs. Variety

Albers distrusted "variety" as a lazy excuse for random change. He preferred "variant" — a thorough re-doing within a given scheme. "There is no final solution in form; form demands unending performance and invites constant reconsideration."

"In an age in which increased human sensibility has become such an obvious need in all areas of human involvement, color sensitivity and awareness can constitute a major weapon against forces of insensitivity and brutalization." University of Nevada reviewer, 1963
About

Josef Albers

JA

Josef Albers (1888–1976) was one of the most influential artist-educators of the twentieth century. Born in Bottrop, Westphalia, he studied in Berlin and Munich before joining the Bauhaus in the 1920s — first as a student, then as a master.

When the Bauhaus closed in 1933 under Nazi pressure, Albers emigrated to the United States, joining Black Mountain College in North Carolina — where he taught for sixteen years. In 1950 he became chairman of Design at Yale University, where "Interaction of Color" was developed and refined.

His paintings, the "Homages to the Square" — begun in 1950 — appear deceptively simple: nested squares of flat color. They are, in fact, sophisticated demonstrations of color interaction in action. Initially mocked, they were given a solo retrospective at The Metropolitan Museum of Art in 1971, making Albers the first living artist so honored.

"Interaction of Color," first published by Yale University Press in 1963, immediately became a landmark. The present 50th Anniversary Edition (2013) adds new plates and refines the presentation — while preserving every word of Albers's radical, practical, humane vision.
