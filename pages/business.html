Section 08 · Chapters 29–32

The Business

Technology, clients, pitches, profit. The forces that surround your design and shape it from the outside — and how to negotiate them while keeping your vision intact.

Chapter 29 — Technology

For a game designer, "technology" means the medium itself — paper and dice as much as polygons and GPUs. Each new technology offers new possibilities, but also tempts designers to forget that the goal is an experience, not a tech demo.

Foundational vs. decorational

The cake of a cupcake is foundational — without it, no cupcake. The icing is decorational. Steamboat Willie is built on synchronized sound (foundational). The same sound added to Plane Crazy was just frosting. Sonic the Hedgehog was built on the Genesis's scrolling ability. Ico turned ragdoll physics — usually decorational — into the foundation of a love story told through holding hands.

Test

When considering any new technology, ask: how can I make this foundational to my game?

The Hype Cycle

Every new technology rides Gartner's curve: technology trigger → peak of inflated expectations → trough of disillusionment → slope of enlightenment → plateau of productivity. Knowing the curve makes you immune to bandwagons, lets you inoculate teammates, and (if you must) raise funding at the peak and ship at the plateau.

The Innovator's Dilemma

Clayton Christensen's warning: companies fail by listening to their existing customers. New technologies look "not good enough" — until suddenly they aren't. Consoles disrupted PC; touch disrupted gamepads; motion controls disrupted both. AI, brainwave sensing, and other "not yet good enough" technologies are waiting their turn.

The Law of Divergence

Technologies don't converge. They split. The number of remotes in your living room only goes up. The exception is the pocket — convenience pushes things together for portability. Otherwise, expect diversity.

LENS #106
The Lens of Utopia

Players play because they're chasing a better world. The technologies most likely to deliver: magical interfaces, fair payment, less artificial AI, family-friendly and friend-connecting features, and tools of transformation.

Chapter 30 — The Client

Form follows funding.Bran Ferren

Almost every game has a client — a publisher, a license-holder, a studio executive, an organization that wants a game made. They are paying. They will have opinions. Your ability to navigate those opinions, gracefully, is the largest single factor in your long-term happiness as a designer.

Coping with bad suggestions

When the client says "these cars need more chrome," the wrong responses are agreeing reflexively or arguing reflexively. The right response is to understand the problem they're trying to solve. In the actual story: the cars felt too slow. More chrome was the client's idea of a solution. The right solution was to make them faster and lower the camera.

Not That Rock

A common pattern: "Bring me a rock." "How about this one?" "No, not that rock." A client who can't say what they want isn't being difficult — they're asking you to help them figure it out. Ask what they'll do with the rock. Then propose options that fit.

Three layers of desire

Every client says one thing with their words, thinks another in their mind, and feels a third in their heart. The words might say "make a game that teaches algebra." The mind might say "actually I want a geometry game." The heart might say "I want people to see I have a creative side." Listen to all three.

Michelangelo's nose

When the mayor of Florence told Michelangelo the nose of his David statue was too big, Michelangelo invited the mayor up to the scaffold to "fix it together," scooped some marble dust into his hand, faked a few chisel taps, and dropped the dust. "I like it better." The deeper layer of desire was creative ownership — and Michelangelo gave it to him without compromising the work.

The Lens of the Client
  • What does the client say they want?
  • What does the client think they want?
  • What does the client really want, deep down in their heart?

Chapter 31 — The Pitch

Pitching is a negotiation of power — and power isn't just "the ability to get." It's "the ability to get what you want." If you don't know what you want, you'll grasp forever.

The hierarchy of ideas

TypeDescriptionValue
IdeaA plain idea~$0.08 (dime a dozen)
Cool ideaCaptures the imagination$5
Really cool ideaA cool idea, shouted$5
Good ideaSomeone could actually use it$100
Good idea, right place, right time, well soldWhat you're aiming for$1,000,000+

Twelve pitch tips

  1. Get in the door. The back door (introductions) beats the front door.
  2. Show you're serious. Prototypes prove commitment.
  3. Be organized. Organization signals reliability.
  4. Be passionate. Not phony — actually feel it.
  5. Assume their point of view. State platform, audience, and genre first. Don't begin with story. Give the idea "handles" — short phrases that summarize it. Show, don't tell. Make it easy for the people you pitch to to pitch your game to their colleagues.
  6. Design the pitch. It's an experience. Surprises, beats, dazzle.
  7. Know all the details. Design, schedule, financial, risk.
  8. Exude confidence. Anchor it with the magic word "absolutely."
  9. Be flexible. The publisher who rejected Richard Garfield's RoboRally bought Magic: The Gathering when Garfield asked if he could pitch a different idea.
  10. Rehearse. Practice on your mom, your hairdresser, your dog.
  11. Get them to own it. Integrate their phrasing into your slides on the fly.
  12. Follow up. Patiently, without nagging.

Kickstarter and crowdfunding

Crowdfunding is really preorder. It works best for things with a fixed price and a physical element. Set the lowest funding goal you can. Shorter campaigns beat longer ones. Show, don't tell. Hustle. Plan stretch goals for virality. And — being already-famous helps a lot.

LENS #108
The Lens of the Pitch
  • Why are you pitching this game to this client?
  • What will you consider "a successful pitch"?
  • What's in it for the people you are pitching to?
  • What do they need to know about your game?

Chapter 32 — Profit

You are designing games out of love. The industry around you is not. Profit is the fuel that keeps the industry alive — and you'd better understand it, because financial decisions become design decisions whether you participate or not.

Follow the money

To understand a business model, follow a dollar. In retail, a $50 game's average breakdown:

  • Retailer: $16
  • Markdown reserve: $3
  • Platform holder: $7
  • Advertising / agencies: $6
  • Disk and box: $8
  • Publisher: $10
  • Developer: $3

Direct download (e.g., Steam) cuts out the retailer, platform holder, and box manufacturer — but the developer now owes the marketing budget themselves. Free-to-play moves the math entirely: revenue equals lifetime value × users, and you must spend on customer acquisition without exceeding LTV.

Know your audience — and what they'll pay

The casual market is a hundred times larger than the hardcore market — but hardcore players will pay much more for a thing they love. Dungeons & Dragons Online figured out that adventure-game players hate "pay for a battle axe" but accept "pay for a quest." Same dollar, completely different psychology.

Learn the language

SKU, COGS, burn rate, sold-in vs. sold-through, breakeven. For free-to-play: churn, cost of acquisition, DAU, MAU, ARPU, ARPPU, LTV, K-factor, whale. Speak the language, and the money people will listen to you. Speak it better than they do, and they'll defer.

The importance of barriers

The iOS gold rush of 2008 collapsed prices to zero. The slot machine industry, by contrast, has fewer competitors — about 90% of slot games are profitable; about 90% of iOS games lose money. Barriers — technical, hardware, expertise, distribution, imagination, relationship, uncertainty — make markets profitable. Find a market where there's a real barrier you can cross.

LENS #109
The Lens of Profit
  • Where does the money go in my game's business model? Why?
  • How much will it cost to produce, market, distribute, and maintain?
  • How much money will this game make? Why do I think that?
  • What are the barriers to entry in the market for this game?
