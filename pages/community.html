Section 06 · Chapters 24–25

Community

Humans are social animals. Most games are played with other people — and the longest-lived games hold communities together for decades.

Chapter 24 — Playing with Other Players

Man is a social animal. Humans generally avoid being alone whenever possible. Jesse Schell

The single-player videogame is a historical aberration — born of a brief moment when technology was the limiting factor. As game platforms went online, multiplayer reasserted itself as the natural mode of play.

Five reasons we play together

  1. Competition. Fairness, challenge, worthy opponents, status, strategy. The cleanest measure of skill.
  2. Collaboration. Actions and strategies impossible alone; the deep pleasure of group problem-solving.
  3. Meeting up. Games give friends an excuse to be together, with something to focus on.
  4. Exploring our friends. "You can learn more about a man in an hour of play than a year of conversation." — Plato (purportedly)
  5. Exploring ourselves. How we behave under pressure, who we choose to ally with, how we cope with loss.

And: spectation. A game played in a living room is half-watched by everyone else — a game streamed online may be watched by millions. Designing for the spectator is no longer optional.

Chapter 25 — Communities

A community is a group of people with a shared interest who get to know each other over time. Around games, communities form for three concrete reasons:

  • Social need fulfillment. Lens #22 in action.
  • Longer period of contagion. Players in communities stay enthusiastic — and contagious — for years instead of weeks.
  • More hours of play. A weak game with strong community outlives a great game with none.

Ten tips for strong communities

  1. Foster friendships. Players need someone to talk to, something to talk about, and the means to talk. Friendships have three phases: breaking the ice, becoming friends, staying friends — design for all three.
  2. Put conflict at the heart. Communities are built on shared opposition or shared problem-solving.
  3. Use architecture to shape your community. Walkable neighborhoods produce neighborly relationships. So do online "town squares."
  4. Create community property. Guild halls, shared ships, jointly owned anything.
  5. Let players express themselves. Cosmetics, custom text colors, expressive avatars.
  6. Support three levels. Newbies (where learning is the game), Players (the standard middle game), and Elders (a different game entirely — harder challenges, governance, creation tools, mentorship). Most failed online games forget the elders.
  7. Force players to depend on each other. Toontown's no-self-healing rule. Spaceteam's distributed information.
  8. Manage your community. Treat the manager role like a gardener — observe, nurture, encourage.
  9. Obligation to others is powerful. Promises, gifts, scheduled meetups all create return.
  10. Create community events. They punctuate time and give people something to anticipate.

The challenge of griefing

Some players don't play your game for its content; they play it to spoil the games of others. Banning griefers is reactive — better to design systems that aren't fun to grief. Common attack surfaces:

  • Player-vs-player combat. Confine to specific areas if not core to the game.
  • Stealing. Almost universally a bad mechanic except in games that are literally about thievery.
  • Trading. Make sure both sides have total information about what they're agreeing to.
  • Obscenities. Combine filters with a community reporting system. The filter alone is a puzzle griefers will solve.
  • Blocking the way. Make collisions push-through.
  • Loopholes. Stay vigilant. Crashing the server with a hopping animation is a great prize for the right kind of jerk.
