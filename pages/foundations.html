Foundations

Everything begins with the designer. Before mechanics or technology, before pitches or playtests, there is a person who decides to make a thing — and an audience whose experience that thing exists to serve.

Chapter 1 — In the Beginning, There Is the Designer

If you want to become a game designer, the answer is almost embarrassingly simple: design games. Start now. Don't even finish this sentence. Go.

For many would-be designers, the trouble isn't knowing what to do — it's a crisis of confidence. They feel stuck in a catch-22: only game designers can design games, but you only become a game designer by designing them. The way out is just as simple. Say these words:

I am a game designer.

Say them out loud. Say them like a chant. You may not feel like one yet — but as we'll see, people become what they pretend to be. Doubt will plague you forever. When you're a novice, you'll think you don't know what you're doing. When you're seasoned, you'll think you've lost your touch. The work continues regardless.

If you aren't dropping, you aren't learning. And if you aren't learning, you aren't a juggler. A saying among jugglers

What skills does a game designer need?

In short — all of them. Almost anything you can be good at can become useful: animation, anthropology, architecture, brainstorming, business, cinematography, communication, creative writing, economics, engineering, games of every kind, history, management, mathematics, music, psychology, public speaking, sound design, technical writing, visual arts.

No one masters all of these. But the more of them you are comfortable working with, however imperfectly, the better off you will be — for growth only happens when we exceed our limits.

The most important skill

Of all those skills, one stands above the rest. It sounds so simple that many people miss it. It isn't creativity. It isn't critical thinking. It isn't even communication.

It is listening.

Not the surface kind. Deep listening. Listening that puts you off-balance, that accepts the possibility of upset. The kind described in Hesse's Siddhartha: "To listen with a silent heart, with a waiting, open soul. Without passion, without desire, without judgment, without rebuke."

Designers must listen to five things:

  • The team — they collectively hold the skills you don't
  • The audience — they decide whether your game succeeded
  • The game — you must hear what is wrong with it as a mechanic listens to an engine
  • The client — they are paying, and they need to be understood
  • The self — the hardest of all, and the source of your true creativity
The secret of the gifted

There are two gifts. The minor gift is innate aptitude — design comes easily. The major gift is love of the work itself. People with only the minor gift rarely produce great things. People with the major gift will keep at it, and their skills will grow to match or exceed those of the naturally gifted. The only way to find out if you have it is to start down the path and see if it makes your heart sing.

Chapter 2 — The Designer Creates an Experience

A game designer's goal is not to design games. It is to create experiences. Games themselves are just artifacts — clumps of cardboard or bags of bits — worthless until played. What we care about is what happens in the mind of the player.

This is hard territory because experiences are too familiar. We can't see them, hold them, or share them. No two people have the same experience of the same thing. And yet, creating experiences is all we really do.

The game is not the experience. The game enables the experience, but it is not the experience. Jesse Schell

Three approaches to chasing rainbows

Several fields have mapped fragments of the territory of human experience:

  • Psychology — the science of the mechanisms of the mind. Useful, though most modern psychology avoids the subjective experience we care about most.
  • Anthropology — especially cultural anthropology, which immerses itself in the lives of its subjects. We can do the same with our players.
  • Design — every other design discipline, from architecture to music, has lessons for us.

Introspection: powers, perils, and practice

The best window into human experience is your own. But introspection has two dangers: it can lead to false conclusions about reality (which is why scientists distrust it), and what is true of your experience may not be true of others. Game design escapes the first because we care about how things feel, not what is objectively true. The second we manage by carefully observing others and projecting ourselves into their place.

Essential experience

You can never replicate a real experience exactly. What you can do is capture its essence. A snowball-fight game doesn't need real snow — it needs the feel of cold, the chaos, the laughter, the friendly threat. Identify what makes the experience matter, then find as many ways as possible to deliver that essence through your game.

LENS #1
The Lens of Emotion

People may forget what you said, but they'll never forget how you made them feel. — Maya Angelou

  • What emotions would I like my player to experience? Why?
  • What emotions are players having when they play now? Why?
  • How can I bridge the gap between the two?
LENS #2
The Lens of Essential Experience

Stop thinking about your game. Start thinking about the experience of the player.

  • What experience do I want the player to have?
  • What is essential to that experience?
  • How can my game capture that essence?

Chapter 3 — The Experience Takes Place in a Venue

Every play experience happens somewhere. Technologies come and go — arcade, PC, console, handheld, mobile, tablet — but the venues in our lives are far more stable. Looking past the platform of the moment to the place where play actually happens reveals patterns that survive technological churn.

Private venues

  • The Hearth — once a fireplace, now a TV. A place where families gather. Good for games people can play together or enjoy watching.
  • The Workbench — a quiet, private place for focused, intense, often solitary work. PC gaming, MMOs, and VR live here.
  • The Reading Nook — a comfortable place to "lean back." Tablets thrive here, with games that are simpler and more relaxing.

Public venues

  • The Theater — many people focused on one performance. Games struggle here because interactivity dilutes with audience size.
  • The Arena — purpose-built fields of play, often paired with theaters. Physical sports' natural home; e-sports increasingly imitate it.
  • The Museum — a place we go to encounter the exotic. Arcades, zoos, and gallery installations all share this DNA.

Half-private, half-public

  • The Gaming Table — the intimate face-to-face venue of board, card, and dice games. So engrossing that the sandwich and the sushi roll were both invented by table-gamers who couldn't bear to stop.
  • The Playground — improvisational play in any open space. Largely the domain of children, mostly untouched by the videogame industry.
  • Anywhere — crosswords, Sudoku, and the entire smartphone market. Tiny bites, simple interfaces, played in spare moments.
LENS #3
The Lens of the Venue

The places we play exert tremendous influence on the design of our games.

  • What type of venue best suits the game I'm trying to create?
  • Does my venue have special properties that will influence my game?
  • What elements of my game are in harmony with my venue? What elements are not?

Chapter 4 — The Experience Rises Out of a Game

What is a game, exactly? Defining it is tricky — many people have tried and most have failed. But the journey of defining is far more valuable than the destination, because forcing yourself to think clearly about it sharpens every design instinct you have.

Schell's working definitions, distilled over many pages of patient analysis:

The Definitions

Fun is pleasure with surprises.

Play is manipulation that indulges curiosity.

A toy is an object you play with.

A good toy is an object that is fun to play with.

A game is a problem-solving activity, approached with a playful attitude.

Why does this last one work? Because almost every quality of a game — goals, rules, conflict, challenge, interaction, internal value, winning and losing, engagement, formal systems — naturally falls out of "problem solving." And the addition of "a playful attitude" handles the freedom and willingness that distinguishes play from work.

LENS #4
The Lens of Surprise

Surprise is so basic that we forget about it. It is at the root of humor, strategy, and problem solving.

  • What will surprise players when they play my game?
  • Does the story have surprises? Do the rules? The artwork? The technology?
  • Do your rules let players surprise each other? Themselves?
LENS #5
The Lens of Fun
  • What parts of my game are fun? Why?
  • What parts need to be more fun?
LENS #6
The Lens of Curiosity

Think about the player's true motivations — not just the goals your game sets, but the reasons the player wants to achieve them.

  • What questions does my game put into the player's mind?
  • What am I doing to make them care about these questions?
  • What can I do to make them invent even more questions?
LENS #7
The Lens of Endogenous Value

A game's success hinges on the players' willingness to pretend it is important.

  • What is valuable to the players in my game?
  • How can I make it more valuable to them?
  • What is the relationship between value in the game and the players' motivations?
LENS #8
The Lens of Problem Solving
  • What problems does my game ask the player to solve?
  • Are there hidden problems that arise as part of gameplay?
  • How can my game generate new problems so players keep coming back?

Chapter 5 — The Game Consists of Elements

To do design, you must learn to see past the skin of a game (what players experience) and into its skeleton (the elements that produce that experience). Schell organizes those elements into four categories — the Elemental Tetrad:

Aesthetics

How the game looks, sounds, feels. The most visible quadrant; the most directly connected to player experience.

Story

The sequence of events that unfolds in your game — linear or branching, scripted or emergent.

Mechanics

The procedures and rules. What makes a game a game. Goals, constraints, and consequences.

Technology

The medium itself — paper, plastic chits, polygons. What enables and constrains everything else.

↑ More visible to players · less visible to players ↓

None of these elements is more important than the others. Each one powerfully influences each of the others. A change in one ripples into the rest. You must develop the rare double vision that sees both skin and skeleton at once — the experience and the elements that produce it.

LENS #9
The Lens of the Elemental Tetrad
  • Is my game design using elements of all four types?
  • Could my design be improved by enhancing elements in one or more of the categories?
  • Are the four elements in harmony, reinforcing each other and working together toward a common theme?
LENS #10
The Lens of Holographic Design

See everything in your game at once — the four elements and the player experience and how they interrelate.

  • What elements of the game make the experience enjoyable?
  • What elements detract from the experience?
  • How can I change game elements to improve the experience?

Chapter 6 — The Elements Support a Theme

To write a mighty book, you must choose a mighty theme.Herman Melville

The strongest games have a clear theme that every element reinforces. Two steps:

  1. Figure out what your theme is.
  2. Use every means possible to reinforce it.

Themes come in two flavors. Experience-based themes deliver an essential feeling ("the fantasy of being a pirate"). Truth-based themes express something we deeply believe ("love is more important than life"). The most powerful games combine both.

When the Disney VR team built Pirates of the Caribbean: Battle for the Buccaneer Gold, the breakthrough was realizing the ride wasn't about pirates — it was about being a pirate. Once they had that theme, every decision flowed from it: the shape of the CAVE, the metal cannons, the swaying motion platform, the ten-speaker audio, even the bags of fishing nets to hold purses. The strongest games are themed all the way down.

LENS #11
The Lens of Unification
  • What is my theme?
  • Am I using every means possible to reinforce that theme?
LENS #12
The Lens of Resonance

Look for hidden power. Resonant themes are buried inside us — and shake us when something causes them to ring.

  • What is it about my game that feels powerful and special?
  • When I describe my game to people, what ideas get them really excited?
  • If I had no constraints of any kind, what would this game be like?
  • I have certain instincts about how this game should be. What is driving those instincts?
