Section 09 · Chapters 33–35

Impact

Games are not trivial amusements. They change the people who play them. The designer's responsibilities, the danger and the promise of the medium, and the question only you can answer.

Chapter 33 — Games Transform Their Players

The question is not whether games change us — they do, beyond doubt — but how, and for whose benefit. Some changes are positive, some negative, some both.

What games can do for us

  • Emotional maintenance — venting, cheering up, gaining perspective, building confidence, relaxing.
  • Connecting — a social bridge with shared topics and memories.
  • Exercise — physical and mental, increasingly important as bodies and minds age.
  • Education — though with constraints.

What makes games such powerful learning tools

  • Visible progress — a fundamental human motivator.
  • Making the abstract concrete — manipulable models the brain can grasp.
  • Full engagement — eyes, hands, mind, social, musical all at once.
  • Lots of small goals.
  • Drilling facts.
  • Practicing problem-solving.
  • Conveying systems of relationships — the thing games do best. Linear media (lectures, videos) cannot show how a complex system actually behaves. A simulation can.
  • Creating teachable moments — putting minds into a state where new knowledge is suddenly useful.
  • Cultivating curiosity — possibly the most valuable mental asset in the twenty-first century.

Transformational games — seven design tips

  1. Define your transformation. What specific change happens in the player, and how?
  2. Find great subject matter experts. Two kinds: those who know the facts, and those who know how to teach them.
  3. What does the instructor need? Don't try to replace the teacher; arm them.
  4. Don't do too much. One transformation, done well.
  5. Assess transformation appropriately — from "feels like it works" to scientific testing.
  6. Choose the right venue.
  7. Accept the realities of the market. A transformational game that no one can buy or play helps no one.

Can games be bad for us?

Two real concerns: violence and addiction.

On violence: most players seem to be very good at distinguishing fantasy from real-world action, and despite millions of hours of violent gameplay, the desensitization concern remains largely theoretical. But: children are still forming their world models, which is exactly why rating systems exist. Gerard Jones's Killing Monsters argues persuasively that some level of violent play is necessary for psychological development. We can't take the question for granted, and we shouldn't.

On addiction: it's real, especially for MMOs. Nicholas Yee's work shows that "problematic usage" is rarely about the game alone — it's about the game pulling in concert with something pushing from real life. The designer's responsibility isn't to make games less compelling. It's to make sure they fit into a balanced life. Shigeru Miyamoto signs autographs for children with: "On a sunny day, play outside."

Chapter 34 — Responsibility

The danger of obscurity

Game designers don't tend to become famous. Designers work in relative obscurity — which means no one will ask you to take responsibility for what you create. Corporations have legal responsibility, but not ethical responsibility — they have no souls. Only individuals can take ethical responsibility. So:

There is only one person

Only one person can take responsibility for the games you make. That person is you.

Being accountable

The most concrete form of design responsibility in online games is player safety. Foul language is a question of taste. Strangers contacting children is a question of life. If your design touches that surface, accept the responsibility before you ship.

Your hidden agenda

Beyond avoiding harm: can you find a way to make your games help? Management almost certainly won't ask you to do this, and may not understand if you propose it. Their job is to serve the corporation. Your job, if you choose, is to bake hidden good into the games that the corporation pays for. Find ways to make your most successful games quietly transform the players who love them.

The ring

In 1922, Rudyard Kipling designed a ritual for graduating engineers at the University of Toronto — an iron ring placed on the pinky of the dominant hand, a lifelong reminder of their obligation to society. The pinky, the smallest finger, secretly guides the hand. Game designers may one day have their own ritual. Until then: take the invisible ring. It doesn't come off.

Chapter 35 — Purpose

The most important kind of listening, we said at the start, is listening to your self. Now is when we do it.

Why is game design important to you? You may think the question can wait. It can't — life is short. Time, as Poe's raven, mocks every plan you've put off. You must do your important work now.

LENS #112
The Lens of the Raven
  • Is making this game worth my time?

Somewhere in you there is a purpose that brought you to this work. Maybe an image you can see in your mind's eye that you need to share. Maybe an experience you want to give back to the world. Maybe a wrong you want to set right. No one but you can know what it is.

Find it. Once you do, your conscious and subconscious motivations will fuse, and your work will gain a focus, a passion, and an intensity it cannot otherwise have.
