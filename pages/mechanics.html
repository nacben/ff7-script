Mechanics

The skeleton of every game. Seven categories of element, plus the disciplines that hold them in balance: difficulty, choice, puzzles, and interface.

Chapter 12 — Some Elements Are Game Mechanics

Strip away aesthetics, story, and technology, and what remains are the mechanics: the interactions and relationships at the heart of the game. They fall into seven categories.

1. Space

Every game has a space — the "magic circle" where play happens. Spaces are discrete or continuous, have some number of dimensions, and contain bounded areas that may or may not be connected. Tic-tac-toe is discrete and 2D; pool is continuous and 2D (or 3D if you count jump shots); Monopoly is really a 1D loop dressed up as 2D. Many games nest spaces — an outdoor map with town interiors as sub-spaces.

LENS #26
The Lens of Functional Space
  • Is the space of this game discrete or continuous?
  • How many dimensions does it have?
  • What are the boundaries? Are there subspaces? How are they connected?

2. Time

Time, like space, can be discrete (a turn) or continuous (real time). Games use clocks (absolute limits) and races (relative pressure). They can stop time, speed it up, rewind it, or — as in Braid — make manipulation of time the central mechanic.

LENS #27
The Lens of Time
  • What determines the length of my gameplay activities?
  • Are my players frustrated because the game ends too early? Bored because it goes too long?
  • Would clocks or races make my gameplay more exciting?
  • Would a hierarchy of time structures help?

3. Objects, attributes, and states

Objects are the nouns of mechanics: cards, pieces, characters, props. Each object has attributes (current speed, hit points, mode), each attribute has a state. State diagrams are useful: every ghost in Pac-Man cycles through Chasing → Blue → Flashing → Eyes → In Cage, with specific events triggering each transition.

LENS #28
The Lens of the State Machine
  • What are the objects in my game?
  • What are their attributes? What states can those attributes be in?
  • What triggers the state changes?

Secrets

Critical to design: who knows what? Public state, private state, state known only to the game, state known only to fate (a hidden die roll). Changing who knows what radically changes the game.

LENS #29
The Lens of Secrets
  • What is known by the game only?
  • What is known by all players? By only one?
  • Would changing who knows what improve the game?

4. Actions

Actions are the verbs. Two kinds: basic actions (the moves the rules permit) and strategic actions (the meaningful plays that emerge from combinations). Checkers has 3 basic actions but many strategic ones — "force an unwanted jump," "build a bridge," "sacrifice to trick the opponent." An elegant game has a small number of basic actions and a large number of strategic ones.

Five ways to encourage emergent gameplay:

  1. Add more verbs.
  2. Make verbs that can act on many objects.
  3. Allow goals to be achieved more than one way.
  4. Provide many subjects to control.
  5. Make side effects change constraints.
LENS #30
The Lens of Emergence
  • How many verbs do my players have?
  • How many objects can each verb act on?
  • How many ways can players achieve their goals?
LENS #31
The Lens of Action
  • What are the basic actions in my game?
  • What are the strategic actions?
  • What actions do players wish they could do but cannot?

5. Rules

The most fundamental mechanic. Parlett distinguishes operational rules (how to play), foundational rules (the underlying math), behavioral rules (implicit etiquette), written rules, laws (tournament adjustments), official rules, advisory rules ("strategy tips"), and house rules. Above all, every game has one top rule: its goal.

Good goals are concrete, achievable, and rewarding. The classic example: chess. "Capture your opponent's king" is more inviting than the standard mealy-mouthed explanation of check, checkmate, and exceptions.

LENS #32
The Lens of Goals
  • What is the ultimate goal of my game? Is it clear to players?
  • If there is a series of goals, do players understand that?
  • Are goals concrete, achievable, and rewarding?
  • Do I have a good balance of short- and long-term goals?
LENS #33
The Lens of Rules
  • What are the foundational rules vs. operational rules of my game?
  • Are there laws or house rules forming as the game develops?
  • Are there modes? Who enforces the rules?
  • Are the rules easy to understand?

6. Skill

Games demand real skills (which the player has) and feature virtual skills (which the character has). Skills fall into three groups: physical, mental, and social. The blend determines the character of your game.

LENS #34
The Lens of Skill
  • What skills does my game require?
  • Which skills are dominant? Are these creating the experience I want?
  • Can players improve their skills with practice?

7. Chance

The most subtle of the seven. Probability theory was literally invented to balance a gambling problem (the Chevalier de Méré's lost bets in 1654). Ten rules every designer should know:

  1. Fractions = decimals = percents.
  2. Probabilities range only from 0 to 1.
  3. "Looked for" ÷ "possible outcomes" = probability.
  4. Enumerate when in doubt.
  5. OR (when mutually exclusive) means add.
  6. AND (when independent) means multiply.
  7. 1 − P(it happens) = P(it doesn't).
  8. The sum of multiple linear random selections is NOT linear (two dice → a 7 is way more likely than a 12).
  9. When math is hard, roll the dice — Monte Carlo simulation gives real numbers.
  10. Geeks love showing off — find an expert and let them help.

Expected value

The average outcome of a decision. Designers use it to balance options. But beware: players don't choose only by expected value. Kahneman and Tversky showed that people will take a guaranteed $2400 over an 89% chance of $2500 — because regret is a real cost.

LENS #35
The Lens of Expected Value
  • What is the actual chance of an event? The perceived chance?
  • What value does each outcome have — quantifiable and intangible?
  • Do the expected values of actions give the player interesting choices?
LENS #36
The Lens of Chance
  • What in my game is truly random? What just feels random?
  • Does randomness give positive feelings or feelings of hopelessness?
  • What is the relationship between chance and skill?

Chapter 13 — Game Mechanics Must Be in Balance

A false balance is an abomination to the Lord.Proverbs 11:1

Balancing a game is adjusting its elements until they deliver the experience you want. It's far from a science — but there are twelve common kinds of balance you'll find yourself working on.

The twelve balances

  1. Fairness — symmetrical games are fair by construction; asymmetrical games require careful tuning. Rock-paper-scissors gives every option both strengths and weaknesses.
  2. Challenge vs. success — keep the player in the flow channel. Let easy parts go fast; create layers of challenge; let players choose difficulty; give the losers a break.
  3. Meaningful choices — beware of dominant strategies (one option clearly best) and meaningless choices (50 cars that drive identically). The number of choices should equal the number of player desires.
  4. Triangularity — a player point at one corner, a low-risk/low-reward option at another, a high-risk/high-reward option at the third. Space Invaders' flying saucer is the canonical example.
  5. Skill vs. chance — affects whether the game feels like a contest of merit or a casual amusement.
  6. Head vs. hands — how much thinking vs. dexterity?
  7. Competition vs. cooperation — the two basic social urges. Team competition combines both.
  8. Short vs. long — game-length is a design decision. Spy Hunter gives infinite lives for 90 seconds, then enforces death. Minotaur's "Armageddon" guarantees the game ends in under 25 minutes.
  9. Rewards — praise, points, prolonged play, gateways, spectacle, expression, powers, resources, status, completion. Variable rewards beat fixed ones.
  10. Punishment — used delicately, punishment creates endogenous value, excitement, and challenge. Used clumsily, it drives players away.
  11. Freedom vs. controlled experience — players want freedom, but not in every moment.
  12. Simple vs. complex — emergent complexity good; innate complexity dangerous. Aim for elegance.
LENS #37
The Lens of Fairness
  • Should my game be symmetrical or asymmetrical? Why?
  • Should it reliably measure skill, or provide interesting challenge to everyone?
LENS #38

  • How does difficulty increase as the player succeeds?
  • Is there enough variety in the challenges?
LENS #39

  • Am I giving the right number of choices?
  • Are there any dominant strategies?
LENS #40

Elegance

Count how many purposes each game element serves. Pac-Man's dots: short-term goal, long-term goal, slow you down (triangularity!), award points, and earn extra lives. Five purposes from one element. That's elegance.

LENS #49
The Lens of Elegance
  • What are the elements of my game?
  • What are the purposes of each? Count them up.
  • For elements with only one or two purposes, can they be combined or removed?
  • For elements with several, can they take on even more?
LENS #50
The Lens of Character

Chapter 14 — Puzzles

A useful working definition: a puzzle is a game with a dominant strategy. That is, once you find the answer, the puzzle ceases to be fun. Puzzles aren't gone from modern games — they're better integrated. The 7th Guest had explicit, incongruous puzzles ("rearrange these cans"). Wind Waker has puzzles seamlessly built into its world.

Ten principles of puzzle design

  1. Make the goal easily understood.
  2. Make it easy to get started. First, build a good toy.
  3. Give a sense of progress. Without progress, a puzzle becomes a riddle.
  4. Give a sense of solvability. Rubik's Cube ships in its solved state.
  5. Increase difficulty gradually. Jigsaw puzzles are masterful at this.
  6. Parallelism lets the player rest. Crossword puzzles let you skip the hard clue.
  7. Pyramid structure extends interest. Small puzzles feeding a final big puzzle.
  8. Hints extend interest. Better to solve with a hint than not at all.
  9. Give the answer. The "aha" is triggered by seeing the answer — not by working for it.
  10. Perceptual shifts are a double-edged sword. Either you see it or you don't.

Chapter 15 — Interface

The interface is the infinitely thin membrane between player and game. When it fails, the experience is snuffed out.

Its goal is not to look nice. Its goal is to make players feel in control.

LENS #59
The Lens of Control
  • When players use the interface, does it do what they expect?
  • Is your interface easy to master?
  • Do your players feel strong influence over the outcome?
  • Feeling powerful = feeling in control. Do they feel powerful?

The six mappings

Feedback & juiciness

Information flows in a loop — player → game → player → game — and the quality of that loop determines the experience. The basketball net does nothing for the game; it just shows the ball going in. The Swiffer's dirty cloth reassures you the floor got cleaner. Tenth-of-a-second rule: any interface that doesn't respond within 100ms feels broken.

A "juicy" system rewards every small input with continuous, satisfying feedback — second-order motion, sounds, particles. A juicy interface is fun before you even understand what the game is about.

The Lens of Primality

Touch is primal. Animals were doing it 400 million years ago. The more your game taps into pre-human cognition, the more intuitive it feels.

Channels, dimensions, and modes

An interface is a careful allocation of information across channels (the dashboard, the avatar, sound, music, color). Each channel has dimensions (size, color, font, position) and can carry multiple pieces of information at once — used elegantly, a single number can communicate three things via value, color, and size.

Modes (a change in mapping) let one interface do many jobs. Three rules: use as few as possible, avoid overlapping ones, make different modes look as different as possible.

The Lens of Metaphor

Game interfaces often mimic interfaces players already know. Use it to teach; don't let it trap you.

Ten interface tips

  1. Steal. Top-down — start from a known interface and modify.
  2. Customize. Bottom-up — design from scratch for a unique game.
  3. Design around your physical interface. One-size-fits-all interfaces produce one-size-fits-none games.
  4. Theme your interface. Reinforce the world all the way to the menus.
  5. Sound maps to touch. Audio simulates the haptic sense we can't yet supply.
  6. Balance options and simplicity with layers.
  7. Use metaphors.
  8. If it looks different, it should act different — and vice versa.
  9. Test, test, test. Nobody gets interface right the first time.
  10. Break the rules to help your player. Children can't always click the "right" mouse button — accept both.
