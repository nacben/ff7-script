Section 03 · Chapters 9–11

The Player

To create a great experience, you must know your audience better than they know themselves. Demographics, psychology, motivation, and the secret workings of the mind that hosts every play experience.

Chapter 9 — The Game Is Made for a Player

Einstein once accepted an invitation to lecture a small audience of older women on relativity. Looking out at the crowd, he made a different decision: he played them his violin instead. They cared less about physics and more about an intimate encounter with the famous Einstein. He knew his audience. So must you.

Project yourself

The best way to understand your audience is empathy. Walt Disney famously crouched while inspecting Disneyland — to see it the way a child would. You must project yourself into the mind of your player, watching with their eyes, feeling with their hands. If your audience is a group you used to belong to (a man designing for adolescent boys he once was), reach back to your memories. If they are a group you've never been (a young woman designing for retirees), become a cultural anthropologist — interview, observe, imagine.

Age demographics, briefly

  • 0–3 — toys, not games. Touch interfaces are intuitive; abstract controllers are not.
  • 4–6 — first interest in simple games, often co-played with parents who bend the rules.
  • 7–9 — "the age of reason." Independent gameplaying begins. Decisions about likes and dislikes start to form.
  • 10–13 — "the age of obsession." Tweens dive deep into their interests.
  • 13–18 — teens experiment with identity through play; gender preferences often diverge.
  • 18–24 — young adults: time, money, and established tastes.
  • 25–35 — peak family formation; mostly casual play, except for the hardcore.
  • 35–50 — career and family; play often family-oriented.
  • 50+ — empty nesters; social games and old favorites; smaller screens become harder.

All play activities connect to childhood. To make games for a particular age, you must speak the language of their childhood.

Stereotypes as inclusion tools

Generalizations about gender, when handled with care, are tools of inclusion rather than exclusion. Knowing that, on average, certain play patterns are more common in different groups lets you design games that support multiple styles of play at once. The classic example: the family target-shooting game whose scoring system unintentionally favored rapid-fire players (statistically male) until a second metric — accuracy — was added. Suddenly both styles felt rewarded.

LENS #19
The Lens of the Player
  • What do they like? What don't they like? Why?
  • What do they expect to see in a game?
  • If I were in their place, what would I want to see?
  • What would they like or dislike about my game in particular?

Psychographics & game pleasures

Demographics group by surface (age, gender). Psychographics group by what people actually enjoy. Marc LeBlanc proposed eight game pleasures:

  1. Sensation — sights, sounds, the tactile joy of moving pieces.
  2. Fantasy — being or doing what you cannot in life.
  3. Narrative — the dramatic unfolding of events.
  4. Challenge — the core pleasure of every game.
  5. Fellowship — friendship, cooperation, community.
  6. Discovery — exploring, finding the new.
  7. Expression — making your mark.
  8. Submission — leaving the world behind for the magic circle.

Richard Bartle's four player types — Achievers ♦, Explorers ♠, Socializers ♥, Killers ♣ — map onto a quadrant of acting/interacting vs. world/players.

There are many more pleasures: anticipation, completion, schadenfreude, gift giving, humor, possibility, naches, surprise, thrill, fiero, wonder. Keep an open mind for ones not on any list.

LENS #20
The Lens of Pleasure
  • What pleasures does your game give to players? Can these be improved?
  • What pleasures are missing from your experience? Why? Can they be added?

Chapter 10 — The Experience Is in the Player's Mind

The human mind is the most complex object in the known universe. Most of its workings are hidden from us — the cocktail-party effect, the way we read facial expressions, the silent calculations behind driving. Game experiences happen entirely inside this black box. So we must make a working knowledge of it.

Four powers that make play possible

  1. Modeling — the brain doesn't deal with reality; it deals with simplified models of reality. Games are predigested models that are easier to think about than the world.
  2. Focus — the brain's ability to pay attention to selected information. The pleasurable extreme of focus is flow.
  3. Imagination — the everyday power to fill gaps. Tell someone "the mailman stole my car," and dozens of details appear unbidden.
  4. Empathy — projecting ourselves into another. We can do it with humans, with animated dogs, with stick figures.

Flow

Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi's well-known concept: when challenges meet skills just right, attention deepens, time falls away, and we feel fulfillment. Flow requires clear goals, no distractions, direct feedback, and a continuously calibrated challenge. Too hard → frustration. Too easy → boredom. Both push us out of the channel.

Skilled designers move the player up the channel in a "tense / release / tense / release" pattern — gradually raising the challenge, then granting a brief easier period (often via new powers), then raising it again.

LENS #21
The Lens of Flow
  • Does my game have clear goals? Are the player's goals the same goals I intended?
  • Does my game provide a steady stream of not-too-easy, not-too-hard challenges?
  • Are the player's skills improving at the rate I hoped?

Chapter 11 — The Player's Mind Is Driven by Motivation

Games are not important. The entire goal of entertainment is to take something unimportant and make it seem very important indeed. Jesse Schell

Maslow & needs

Maslow's hierarchy is a workable map for thinking about why games engage us. Most game activities live on the self-esteem tier (challenge, mastery), but multiplayer games reach down into love/belonging and safety — which is why they tend to hook deeper. Minecraft covers the whole pyramid: shelter, safety, social, mastery, creative expression.

Self-determination theory

Edward Deci and Richard Ryan proposed that humans have three psychological needs as essential as physical ones:

  • Competence — I need to feel good at something.
  • Autonomy — I need freedom to do things my way.
  • Relatedness — I need to connect with other people.

Games naturally fulfill all three.

LENS #22
The Lens of Needs
  • On which levels of Maslow's hierarchy is my game operating?
  • Does it fill the needs of competence, autonomy, and relatedness?
  • How can I make my game fill more basic needs than it already does?

Intrinsic vs. extrinsic motivation

Not a binary — a gradient. A famous study found that paying children to draw caused them to make more pictures of lower quality, and to stop drawing entirely when payment ended. Extrinsic rewards can drain intrinsic motivation. Beware of "gamification" that strips activities of their natural appeal.

Wanna vs. Hafta

Pleasure-seeking and pain-avoiding are different brain systems. Imagine Big Red Button Software releases two products — Big Red Button Tax Prep, which finishes your taxes, and Big Red Button Angry Birds, which wins instantly. The tax prep is wonderful; the game is the worst game ever. Taxes are a hafta, games are a wanna. Many "free-to-play" games drift from wanna to hafta over time — and as designer Sheri Graner Ray put it, "people don't just stop playing these games, they divorce them."

LENS #23
The Lens of Motivation
  • What motivations do players have to play my game?
  • Which motivations are internal? Which are external?
  • Which are pleasure-seeking? Which are pain-avoiding?
  • Which motivations support each other? Which are in conflict?

Novelty

Players want what is new. Novelty drives purchases and keeps people playing — especially novelty that makes them think in a new way. Portal's slogan said it best: "Now you're thinking with portals." But novelty alone is a thin foundation; when it wears off, there must be something underneath.

LENS #24
The Lens of Novelty
  • What is novel about my game?
  • Does my game have novelties throughout, or just at the beginning?
  • When the novelty wears off, will players still enjoy my game?
LENS #25
The Lens of Judgment

People don't hate being judged — they hate being judged unfairly. Games are excellent systems for fair, objective judgment, and that is part of their appeal.

  • What does your game judge about the players?
  • How does it communicate this judgment?
  • Do players feel the judgment is fair? Do they care?
  • Does the judgment make them want to improve?
