Section 02 · Chapters 7–8

Process — Ideas & Iteration

The actual practice of game design: where ideas come from, why prototypes are throwaway, and the unbreakable Rule of the Loop.

Chapter 7 — The Game Begins with an Idea

You may worry that the method you used to design games — think of an idea, try it out, keep changing it until it's good — isn't the "real" method. Relax. That is the real method. The question is only how to do each step well.

Infinite inspiration

The most original work doesn't come from copying other people in your field. It comes from looking everywhere else — at ballet, at flocks of geese, at paper punch machines, at the messy life around you — and finding inspiration in things that nobody else thought to translate into game form.

LENS #13
The Lens of Infinite Inspiration

When you know how to listen, everybody is the guru. Stop looking at your game and at games like it. Look everywhere else.

  • What is an experience I have had in my life that I would want to share with others?
  • In what small way can I capture the essence of that experience and put it into my game?

State the problem

Don't fall in love with your solution. Fall in love with your problem.Anonymous

Most designers jump to solutions too fast. A clear problem statement opens a broader creative space, gives you a measurement of quality (does this solve the problem?), and makes collaboration easier. Notice that "how can I make a browser game teenagers will like?" is constraining — perhaps the question is really "how can I make a browser experience teenagers will like?"

LENS #14
The Lens of the Problem Statement
  • What problem, or problems, am I really trying to solve?
  • Have I been making assumptions about this game that have nothing to do with its true purpose?
  • Is a game really the best solution? Why?
  • How will I be able to tell if the problem is solved?

Your silent partner

Most good ideas don't arrive through reasoning. They surface from the subconscious — the same place dreams come from. Schell argues you should treat your subconscious like a separate person and learn to collaborate with it: pay attention to its hints, record its ideas, manage its appetites, and (above all) sleep. Most good ideas come on the other side of rest.

Five rules for your subconscious

1. Pay attention — if you ignore its suggestions, they stop coming. 2. Record your ideas — until they're written, they clutter your mind. 3. Manage its appetites judiciously — basic needs satisfied, it does its best work. 4. Sleep — the committee of sleep solves problems daylight can't. 5. Don't push too hard — looming over your subconscious slows it down.

Sixteen brainstorming tips (the short version)

  1. Write things down. Memory is terrible. Externalize.
  2. Pen or keyboard, whatever works. Unlined paper invites circles and arrows.
  3. Sketch. Even bad drawings spark ideas you wouldn't have had.
  4. Bring toys. Visual and tactile stimulation triggers creativity.
  5. Change your perspective. Stand on the chair. Go somewhere new.
  6. Immerse yourself. Become an anthropologist of your target audience.
  7. Crack jokes. Humor unlocks perspectives logic locks shut.
  8. Spare no expense. Don't let cheap materials inhibit million-dollar ideas.
  9. Use the walls. Big paper, sticky notes — get ideas out of your head and onto a surface you can walk around.
  10. The space remembers. Spatial memory is strong; lean on it.
  11. Write everything. Stupid ideas clear the way for good ones.
  12. Number your lists. Numbered things acquire dignity.
  13. Destroy your assumptions. List what you've assumed and ask: what if it weren't true?
  14. Mix and match categories. Lists of mechanics × story × aesthetic × tech yield surprising hybrids.
  15. Talk to yourself. Saying things aloud makes them real.
  16. Find a partner. Two minds bounce. Four is a meeting.

Chapter 8 — The Game Improves Through Iteration

Once you make a decision, the universe conspires to make it happen.Emerson

Pick an idea. Commit. The moment you commit, flaws and benefits become clear that weren't before. Be ready to reverse the decision when you realize it's wrong — ideas are paper cups, not china — but pick, and pick fast.

The eight filters

A finished design must pass through all eight. Change anything, and you have to re-run the whole gauntlet:

  1. Artistic impulse — does it feel right?
  2. Demographics — will the intended audience like it?
  3. Experience design — is it well-crafted under many lenses?
  4. Innovation — is it novel enough?
  5. Business and marketing — will it be profitable?
  6. Engineering — can it be built?
  7. Social / community — does it meet the social goals?
  8. Playtesting — do real players enjoy it?
LENS #15
The Lens of the Eight Filters

A design is only done when it can pass all eight tests without requiring change.

The Rule of the Loop

The Rule of the Loop

The more times you test and improve your design, the better your game will be. There are no exceptions.

This is an absolute truth, not a perspective. Faced with a long-term project, you must answer two questions:

  1. How can I make every loop count? → Assess risks; mitigate them.
  2. How can I loop as fast as possible? → Build many rough prototypes.

From waterfall to agile

The waterfall model was a fantasy of orderly steps. It violated the Rule of the Loop. Barry Boehm's spiral model (1986) put iteration at the center, and the 2001 Agile Manifesto codified it: working software over comprehensive documentation; responding to change over following a plan. Most game developers now work in some flavor of Agile — flexible goals, prioritized backlogs, short sprints, daily scrums, demo days, retrospectives.

Risk mitigation by prototyping

Before writing code, list the risks: the unfun mechanic, the engine limit, the schedule risk, the publisher curveball. Then build the smallest possible prototype that tells you whether each risk is real. Cheap prototypes look ugly on purpose — polished ones hide problems and lull you into false security.

LENS #16
The Lens of Risk Mitigation

Because the sage always confronts difficulties, he never experiences them. — Tao Te Ching

  • What could keep this game from being great?
  • How can we stop that from happening?

Ten prototyping tips

  1. Answer a question. Every prototype exists to answer one.
  2. Forget quality. Polish wastes time and hides defects.
  3. Don't get attached. Plan to throw it away — you will anyway.
  4. Prioritize prototypes. Biggest risks first.
  5. Parallelize. Artists, engineers, designers can each prototype simultaneously.
  6. It doesn't have to be digital. Paper prototypes spot problems faster.
  7. It doesn't have to be interactive. Animatics and sketches can answer questions too.
  8. Pick a fast-loop engine. Languages that let you change code while the game runs are gold.
  9. Build the toy first. Make sure the core interaction is fun before designing a game around it.
  10. Seize opportunities for more loops. Schedule surprises can be a gift.
LENS #17
The Lens of the Toy

Stop thinking about whether your game is fun to play. Start thinking about whether it is fun to play with.

  • If my game had no goal, would it be fun at all?
  • When people see my game, do they want to start interacting with it even before they know what to do?

How much is enough?

The work is never finished, only abandoned. You must loop enough times to be proud, before you spend the whole budget. Mark Cerny's "Method" observes that preproduction continues until you have two publishable levels — at which point the project is ~30% spent, and the rest can finally be scheduled with confidence.

Two useful rules of thumb

The Plan-to-Cut Rule: design so that if 50% of your budget vanished, you'd still have a shippable game.

The 50% Rule: all core gameplay should be fully playable at the halfway point in your schedule. Plan to spend the second half polishing.

LENS #18
The Lens of Passion
  • Am I filled with blinding passion about how great this game will be?
  • If I've lost the passion, can I find it again?
  • If the passion isn't coming back, shouldn't I be doing something else?
