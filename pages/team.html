The Team

Game design is rarely a solo activity. The skills of working together — loving the work, communicating clearly, documenting honestly, and playtesting fearlessly — matter as much as the design itself.

Chapter 26 — The Designer Usually Works with a Team

The secret

The secret of successful teamwork is love. Not love between teammates. Love of the game you are making together, and the audience you are making it for. With genuine love for the work, differences and disagreements dissolve in service of the thing being created.

Three love problems break teams:

  1. Team members incapable of loving any game. Get them off the team.
  2. Members in love with a different game. Find what they can love about this game. The animators on the pirate game grew dejected when characters were cut — until one came in with sketches of how the ships could burn, splinter, and explode. New love.
  3. Members in love with different visions of the same game. The most common case. Talk it out, hear them out, find shared ground. Don't declare a decision final until every member is genuinely on board.

If you can't love the game, love the audience. If you can't love the audience, pretend to — and watch real love emerge.

LENS #100
The Lens of Love
  • Do I love my project? If not, how can I change that?
  • Does everyone on the team love the project? If not, how can that be changed?

Designing together

Designers who shut the team out of the design process produce dead designs. Better: include everyone whenever possible. You'll have more ideas, weed out flawed ones faster, see the game from many angles, and — most importantly — give every team member a stake in the outcome. Leave the design slightly under-fleshed; the developers closest to each problem will think hardest about it.

Ten keys to team communication

  1. Objectivity. Use Lens #14 to discuss ideas in terms of how well they solve stated problems, not in terms of whose idea it was.
  2. Clarity. If you don't understand someone, ask. Don't pretend.
  3. Persistence. Write things down. Memory is unreliable; documents are not.
  4. Comfort. Uncomfortable people communicate badly.
  5. Respect. Disrespected people stop being honest.
  6. Trust. Built gradually through the quantity of communication, not its quality.
  7. Honesty. Without it, trust can't form.
  8. Privacy. One-on-one conversations reveal truths group meetings cannot.
  9. Unity. Eventually, the team must commit to a path. "Disagree and commit."
  10. Love. Where everything else comes from.
LENS #101
The Lens of the Team
  • Is this the right team for this project? Why?
  • Is the team communicating objectively?
  • Is the team comfortable with each other?
  • Is the team ultimately able to unify around decisions?

Chapter 27 — Documents

The myth

The "magic template" for a game design document does not exist. It never has. Anyone who tells you otherwise is mistaken. Documents are theories about what your game might be — written, they age the moment they're complete.

What documents are actually for

Two purposes only: memory (so you don't forget the hundreds of decisions you've made) and communication (so your team can do their work without guessing). The right documents for your project are determined by what needs to be remembered and what needs to be communicated — nothing more.

The likely set

  • Game design overview — a few pages for management.
  • Detailed design document — many small documents organized by subsystem, not one giant tome.
  • Story overview — for writers.
  • Technical design document — for engineers.
  • Pipeline overview — engineers writing for artists.
  • System limitations — what the tech can and can't do.
  • Art bible — visual consistency reference.
  • Concept art — woven through the design documents.
  • Game budget, asset tracker, project schedule — production documents.
  • Story bible, script — writing documents.
  • Tutorial and manual — for the player.
  • Walk-throughs — written by the players themselves.

Stone Librande's brilliant convention: every game should fit on a single one-page poster diagram showing how the whole thing fits together. This becomes the heart of every design conversation.

LENS #102
The Lens of Documentation
  • What do we need to remember while making this game?
  • What needs to be communicated while making this game?

Start simple: a bullet list of ideas. As questions arise, write them down. As answers settle, record them. Your design document grows organically — never from a template.

Chapter 28 — Playtesting

Most designers hate playtesting. It is, as Schell admits, a humiliating engraved invitation to be told why you suck. Do it anyway. Do it constantly. Do it earlier than you want to.

WUBALEW

How often should you playtest? When useful, but at least every week.

The six questions of any playtest

Why? Every playtest answers specific questions. "Is it fun?" is not specific enough. Better: "Do players understand how to play level three?" "Is the asparagus puzzle too hard?" "Does the lightning attack feel rewarding?"

Who? Developers (close, biased, available), friends (kind, biased), expert gamers (knowledgeable, niche), or "tissue testers" who have never seen the game. Match the testers to your questions.

When? Earlier than you think. Pre-idea (focus groups), initial concept, paper prototype, whitebox, working prototype, shipped game — all are valid testing moments.

Where? Your studio (convenient, intimidating to outsiders), a usability lab (expensive, ideal), a public venue (broad, distracted), the player's home (real conditions, limited observation), or online (scale, low fidelity).

What? The things you know you're looking for — and the surprises you don't yet know about. The surprises are usually more valuable.

How? Be there if you can be objective. Plan your introduction (it will become your tutorial). Watch faces, not just screens. Collect data automatically when possible. Decide carefully whether to interrupt mid-game. The "think-aloud protocol" works for some players and freezes others.

FFWWDD — six post-game questions

  1. F — Frustrating. "What was the most frustrating moment or aspect?"
  2. F — Favorite. "What was your favorite moment or aspect?"
  3. W — Wanted. "Was there anything you wanted to do that you couldn't?"
  4. W — Wand. "If you had a magic wand to change anything, what would it be?"
  5. D — Doing. "What were you doing in the experience?"
  6. D — Describe. "How would you describe this game to your friends and family?"

Asked in that order, for a reason. Frustration first vents pressure. Favorite reveals the peak. Wanted uncovers small unmet desires; wand opens dreams. Doing reveals the player's understood goal. Describe reveals the player's understood point.

LENS #103
The Lens of Playtesting
  • Why are we doing this playtest?
  • Who should be there?
  • When should we test? Where?
  • What will we look for? How will we get the information we need?
