Positioning:
The Battle for Your Mind

How to be seen and heard in the overcommunicated society — the classic framework that changed marketing forever.

By Al Ries & Jack Trout  ·  Originally published 1981, updated 2001

The Central Insight

Positioning is not what you do to a product. Positioning is what you do to the mind of the prospect. In our overcommunicated society, the only way to cut through the noise is to create a simple, compelling position that sticks in the mind — and to own that position before anyone else does. The battlefield is between six inches of gray matter, not on a shelf.

Chapter 1 · Foundation

What Is Positioning?

Positioning starts with a product but its real work happens entirely in the mind. The concept shattered advertising's self-centered creative tradition, replacing "product talk" with prospect-centered reality.

"Positioning is the first body of thought that comes to grips with the problems of getting heard in our overcommunicated society."

— Al Ries & Jack Trout

The Definition

Positioning starts with a product — a piece of merchandise, a service, a company, an institution, or even a person. But positioning is not what you do to a product.

Positioning is what you do to the mind of the prospect. Changes made in the name, the price, and the package are not changes in the product — they are cosmetic changes done to secure a position in the prospect's mind.

A newer definition: "How you differentiate yourself in the mind of your prospect."

The Three Eras of Advertising

1950s — Product Era
The Unique Selling Proposition
Ads focused on the product's one differentiating feature (Rosser Reeves's USP). Worked until me-too products made features indistinguishable.
1960s — Image Era
Reputation Over Features
David Ogilvy's era: "Every ad is a long-term investment in the image of a brand." But as every company fought for image, noise overwhelmed each voice.
1970s → Today — Positioning Era
Strategy Is King
In an overcommunicated world, a company must create a position in the prospect's mind that accounts for its own strengths, weaknesses, and competitors.

The Basic Approach

The basic approach of positioning is not to create something new and different, but to manipulate what's already in the mind — to retie the connections that already exist. The mind accepts only new information that matches its current state. Everything else is filtered out. You don't create a position. You find one.

Chapters 1–2 · The Problem

The Overcommunicated Society

Why does communication fail so spectacularly? Because we live in a world drowning in messages. The mind has built its own defense system — and most advertising never gets past the gate.

"The average mind is a dripping sponge that can only soak up more information at the expense of what's already there."

Problem 1

The Media Explosion

Television, radio, magazines, newspapers, outdoor, digital — each new medium added without replacing old ones. Every new channel created more noise without increasing the mind's capacity to receive it.

Problem 2

The Product Explosion

The average person's speaking vocabulary is 8,000 words. They must also track 40,000 supermarket products, 450,000 active trademarks, and millions of company names. The mind simply cannot cope.

Problem 3

The Mind's Defense

To protect itself, the mind rejects information that doesn't "compute." It accepts only that which matches prior knowledge or experience. Attempting to change a made-up mind is the road to advertising disaster.

The Solution: Oversimplify

In communication, as in architecture, less is more. You must sharpen your message to cut into the mind — jettison the ambiguities, simplify, and then simplify some more.

Own a Word in the Mind

The best positioning leads to owning a single word in the prospect's mind:

  • Volvo owns "safety"
  • BMW owns "driving"
  • FedEx owns "overnight"
  • Crest owns "cavities"
  • Coca-Cola owns "real thing"

Once you own a word in the mind, you have to use it or lose it.

Chapter 3 · Strategy

Getting Into the Mind

There is an easy way and a hard way. Most companies choose the hard way — or give up entirely — because they don't understand the fundamental law of mental real estate.

✓ The Easy Way: Be First
Charles Lindbergh — first to fly solo across the Atlantic (everyone knows)
Neil Armstrong — first to walk on the moon (everyone knows)
Your first love (everyone remembers)
Kodak, IBM, Xerox, Hertz — all first in their categories
The first brand earns 2× the long-term share of #2
✗ The Hard Way: Being Second
Bert Hinkler — second to fly solo across the Atlantic (who?)
Second person on the moon (can you name them?)
Second-largest cargo port in the US: Hampton Roads, VA (did you know?)
Second can survive, but #3 and #4 have the most serious problems

"It's better to be first than to be better. If you can't be first in a category, set up a new category you can be first in."

— The two most powerful positioning ideas in the book
The Amelia Earhart Strategy

Create a New Category

Amelia Earhart got famous not for being the third person to fly the Atlantic solo — but for being the first woman. She created a new category she could be first in. You don't have to be first in the market. You have to be first in the mind.

Michelob: Not the first high-priced beer (Heineken was), but the first high-priced domestic beer. New category, first position, massive success.
The Amerigo Principle

Position It, Then Publicize It

Columbus discovered America but Amerigo Vespucci got the continent named after him. He positioned the New World as a separate continent (not Asia) and wrote five widely-translated letters about his discoveries. Columbus died in jail.

Lesson: Discovery means nothing without clear positioning and relentless communication. The credit goes to whoever frames the story.
The Imprinting Analogy

First Impressions Are Indelible

Animal biologists call it "imprinting" — a newborn duck bonds permanently with the first creature it sees. In business, the first brand to occupy a position gets imprinted in the prospect's mind and holds that position against enormous competitive pressure for years.

What Made the Great Brands Great

In 1923, researchers identified the leading brands across 25 product categories. By 2000, 77 years later, 19 of those 25 brands were still the leaders. Leadership is not a product of great marketing skill so much as being first in the mind and then defending that position consistently.

Chapter 4 · The Framework

Those Little Ladders in Your Head

The mind copes with complexity by ranking everything on mental ladders. Each product category has its own ladder. The brand on the top rung wins disproportionately. Understanding this structure is the key to all positioning strategy.

The Rent-A-Car Ladder

#1 Hertz THE LEADER
#2 Avis "We try harder"
#3 National

Most prospects have this exact ladder — even customers who regularly rent from Avis. Understanding where your brand sits on the ladder is the first step in any positioning program.

The Cola Ladder (with "Uncola")

#1 Coke
#2 Pepsi
#3 7-Up The Uncola

7-Up positioned itself on the cola ladder by acknowledging what it wasn't. Sales rose from $87.7M to $190M+ after the "uncola" campaign — by relating to what was already in the mind rather than fighting it.

The Rule of Seven

Harvard psychologist Dr. George A. Miller found the average human mind cannot deal with more than seven units at once. Ask someone to name brands in a category — rarely more than seven. For low-interest products, one or two. This is why the top rung is so valuable, and why positions 4–7 are so precarious. There is no room for everyone.

Position New Products Against the Old

The mind has no room for what's new and different unless it's related to the old. The first automobile was called a "horseless carriage" — this let the public position the concept against the existing mode of transportation. "Offtrack" betting, "lead-free" gasoline, "tubeless" tires — all new concepts positioned against familiar ones.

The F.W.M.T.S. Trap

"Forgot What Made Them Successful" — the most common failure mode for brands that once had brilliant positioning:

  • Avis abandoned "We're No. 2, we try harder" and ran ads saying "Avis is going to be No. 1" — advertising aspirations instead of a real position
  • 7-Up replaced "The Uncola" with "America is turning 7-Up" — the same mistake. America was not turning 7-Up.
  • A position is built on long-term commitment. Walking away from what made you successful is almost always a fatal error.
Chapter 5 · Reality Check

You Can't Get There From Here

Some positions simply cannot be taken, no matter how much money you pour in. Recognizing this is not failure — it is wisdom. The story of IBM vs. RCA is the defining lesson.

"A company has no hope of making progress head-on against the position IBM has established in computers."

— Written in 1969. Proven dramatically right in 1971 when RCA wrote off $250 million.
Warning Story

RCA vs. IBM: The $250 Million Lesson

In 1969, Ries and Trout wrote that no company could take on IBM head-on in computers. RCA ignored this and launched a full-speed assault — their chairman predicting a "firm No. 2 position" by year's end. Headlines at the time: "RCA fires a broadside at No. 1." "RCA goes head-to-head with IBM." One year later: "$250 million disaster hits RCA." GE had already sold its computer operations to Honeywell for the same reason. Two major companies destroyed billions of dollars of shareholder value learning a lesson that should have been obvious.

The lesson: "Fight fire with fire" is poor strategy. As Howard Gossage said, "That's silly. You fight fire with water." Find the positions that are actually available rather than attacking positions that aren't.
What Worked Instead

Digital Equipment Corporation

While the "Seven Dwarfs" fought IBM head-on in mainframes, DEC created an entirely new category: the minicomputer. They became the second-largest computer company in the world by being first in a category IBM didn't own. The Amelia Earhart strategy in action.

Principle: It's better to be a big fish in a small pond — then grow the pond.
The Leadership Lesson

Jones vs. Smith at General Electric

Two executives competed for GE's top job. Smith fought to make GE's computer division work. Jones recognized it was a losing battle and recommended exiting entirely. Jones became CEO. Smith wound up at International Paper.

Principle: Knowing when "you can't get there from here" is itself a leadership quality — not a failure of nerve.
Chapter 6 · Strategy for the Top

Positioning of a Leader

Being first is the best way to become a leader. Staying first requires specific strategies that are different from what followers must do. Leaders have tools unavailable to everyone else.

19 of 25
Leading brands from 1923 still leading in 2000 — 77 years later
6:4
Coca-Cola vs. Pepsi bottles sold — despite Pepsi's decades of attacks
Strategy 1

"The Real Thing"

Every product that gets into the mind first is perceived as the real thing. Reinforce this constantly. Coca-Cola's "It's the real thing" simultaneously positioned every competitor as an imitation — perhaps the most powerful advertising slogan ever invented. Use it or lose it.

Strategy 2

Cover All Bets

When a competitor introduces a promising new concept, cover it immediately. Don't wait. GM bought a Wankel engine license as "$50M cheap insurance" on a $66B business. J&J cut Tylenol's price before Datril's attack advertising even ran — and crushed the threat.

Strategy 3

Multibrand Strategy

P&G's genius: each brand uniquely positioned for one specific position. Tide, Cheer, Bold, Ivory. When new categories emerge, launch new brands. With fewer brands (51 vs. Colgate's 65), P&G does twice the business and three times the profit.

What Leaders Should NOT Do

Don't run ads that say "We're No. 1." Either the prospect knows it (and wonders why you're insecure enough to say it), or they don't (and wonder why they don't know). The power of a product comes from the position it holds in the prospect's mind — not from the size of the company. Coca-Cola's power is in the cola position. Outside that position, the Coca-Cola Company starts at zero like everyone else.

The Instability of Equality

When two brands are running neck-and-neck, that situation is inherently unstable. Consumers are like chickens — they are much more comfortable with a clear pecking order. Sooner or later one brand gets the upper hand and opens a lead that reaches a stable ratio. The critical time to pour in resources is when the battle is still undecided. Winning the sales leadership in a single year often clinches the victory for decades.

Chapter 7 · Strategy for #2 and Beyond

Positioning of a Follower

What works for a leader does not work for a follower. The follower's challenge is to find a hole in the mind — a "creneau" — that the leader hasn't occupied. This requires going against conventional wisdom.

"Cherchez le creneau. Look for the hole — and then fill it. That advice goes against the 'bigger and better' philosophy ingrained in the American spirit."

Types of Creneaus (Holes in the Mind)

🔵 Size Creneau

Volkswagen found an open position in "small" when Detroit was fixated on bigger. "Think small" — the most famous ad ever. Sony used the same approach in television.

💰 High-Price Creneau

Michelob ("First class is Michelob"), Chivas Regal, Joy perfume ("costliest in the world"), Piaget watches. Works when you get there first with a valid product story.

🏷️ Low-Price Creneau

Often right for new product categories where consumers are uncertain. Works especially well when established leaders charge premium prices for products people are unfamiliar with.

♂ Sex / Gender Creneau

Marlboro found the masculine creneau in a sea of unisex cigarettes. Virginia Slims went feminine. Charlie perfume used a masculine name — and became the world's largest-selling perfume.

🕐 Time-of-Day Creneau

Nyquil: the first nighttime cold remedy. By owning a specific time of use, it sidestepped direct competition with every daytime remedy and built a dominant position.

📦 Distribution Creneau

L'eggs positioned itself as the first hosiery brand in supermarkets. A distribution channel no hosiery company owned. It became the market leader.

👤 Heavy-User Creneau

Schaefer beer: "The one beer to have when you're having more than one" — owned the heavy drinker position in NYC when five major breweries competed there.

⬆ Against Position

Avis's immortal "We try harder" — the most famous example of the "against" position. Not #1, but explicitly #2, which generated enormous sympathy and credibility.

Beware: The Everybody Trap

Some companies reject the creneau concept because they don't want to "limit" their appeal. But in today's marketplace, trying to be everything to everyone means you end up being nothing to no one. Rheingold beer tried to appeal to every ethnic group in New York with separate commercials — and alienated all of them. Meanwhile, Schaefer owned one specific position and dominated.

Beware: The Me-Too Trap

Don't copy the leader and just claim to be "better." The prospect won't believe you. Most me-too products fail because they accent "better" rather than "speed" — they spend time improving the product instead of being first to establish a distinctive position. Being better than the competition is not enough. Being first in the mind is everything.

Chapter 8 · Offensive Strategy

Repositioning the Competition

When there are no open creneaus, create one — by repositioning the brands that currently occupy positions in the mind. This is not comparative advertising. It's a surgical strike on a competitor's position.

"To move a new idea into the mind, you must first move an old one out. Once an old idea is overturned, selling the new one is often ludicrously simple. People will actively search for the new idea to fill the void."

Classic Example

Tylenol Repositions Aspirin

"For the millions who should not take aspirin… If your stomach is easily upset, or you have an ulcer, or you suffer from asthma, allergies or iron-deficiency anemia, it would make good sense to check with your doctor before you take aspirin. Aspirin can irritate the stomach lining, trigger asthmatic or allergic reactions, cause small amounts of hidden gastrointestinal bleeding. Fortunately, there is Tylenol." — Sixty words of copy before any mention of the advertiser's own product. Tylenol went on to become the #1 analgesic brand. Against aspirin. Against an institution. Amazing.

Key insight: A repositioning campaign changes what the prospect thinks about the competitor, not about you. Once the old idea is unseated, prospects will search for a new idea to fill the void — and you're right there.
Example 2

Stolichnaya vs. American Vodkas

"Most American vodkas seem Russian. Samovar: Made in Schenley, PA. Smirnoff: Made in Hartford, CT. Wolfschmidt: Made in Lawrenceburg, IN. Stolichnaya is different. It is Russian." Not a claim about quality — just a reality check on competitors. Sales soared.

Example 3

Royal Doulton vs. Lenox China

"The china of Stoke-on-Trent, England vs. Lenox. The china of Pomona, New Jersey." Prospects thought Lenox was English — the name sounded it. One ad revealing its New Jersey origin shifted perception and gained Royal Doulton 6 points of market share.

Example 4

Scope vs. Listerine

Two words repositioned the category leader: "Medicine breath." Listerine's own theme — "the taste you hate, twice a day" — was turned against it. Scope carved position #2 out of the mouthwash market and held it firmly.

Repositioning vs. Comparative Advertising

These are not the same thing. Comparative advertising ("We're better than our competitors") isn't repositioning — it's just boasting. The prospect's reaction: "If you're so smart, how come you're not rich?" Effective repositioning requires changing what the prospect thinks about the competitor. Simply claiming superiority reinforces the existing order rather than disrupting it.

Chapter 9 · The Name Game

The Power of the Name

The name is the hook that hangs the brand on the product ladder in the prospect's mind. In the positioning era, the single most important marketing decision is what to name the product. Shakespeare was wrong: a rose by any other name would not smell as sweet.

"Would 'Alfred' have sold as well as 'Charlie' (the perfume)? Don't bet on it. Hog Island in the Caribbean was going nowhere until they changed its name to Paradise Island."

Names That Begin the Positioning

  • Head & Shoulders — shampoo: communicates the benefit immediately
  • DieHard — battery: implies longevity and toughness
  • Intensive Care — hand lotion: medical authority and urgency
  • Close-Up — toothpaste: implies fresh breath and intimacy
  • Shake 'n Bake — cooking product: describes the process itself
  • Edge — shaving cream: precision and performance
  • People — magazine: so obviously right it's brilliant
  • Bubble Yum — bubble gum: sensory delight in two words

Names That Failed and Why

  • Edsel — perceived as a "loser" name before the car launched; contributed to disaster
  • Lite beer — too generic; became industry-wide rather than ownable by Miller
  • Frost 8/80 — "dry white whisky" positioned against itself (competing with vodka, gin, rum)
  • Mennen E — vitamin E as deodorant? Sounds bizarre and unbelievable
  • Maxim — missed the freeze-dried coffee category it invented (lost to Taster's Choice)
  • XD-12 — inside engineering joke with zero prospect meaning

The Generic Sweet Spot

Choosing a name is like driving a racing car — you must take names that are almost, but not quite, generic. Too generic and you lose trademark protection. Too abstract and you lose communicative power. The goal: a name strong enough to block competitors from muscling into your territory. People magazine is nearly generic for gossip — and it's a runaway success. Us, the copycat, always struggled.

The Mind Works by Ear, Not by Eye

Business executives swim in paper, but the mind works aurally. Before you can file a word in the mind, you must hear it. This is why names, headlines, slogans, and themes should all be evaluated for their sound — even when used only in print. Hubert and Elmer don't look bad on a page. They only sound bad. That's the filter that matters.

When a Meaningless Name Works

Coca-Cola, Kodak, and Xerox are all "meaningless" names that became enormously powerful. But here's the key: they were first. The first company into the mind with a truly new product will become famous regardless of the name. Only when you are first with a product millions will want can you afford the luxury of a coinaged, meaningless name. Otherwise, use descriptive names that do positioning work from day one.

Chapter 10 · The Initials Trap

The No-Name Trap

Companies fall in love with their initials. They see IBM and think: "That could be us." But IBM became famous as International Business Machines first — the initials only work because the name is already burned into millions of minds. The shortcut destroys the bridge that makes the shortcut work.

"The mind works by ear. Initials that look shorter often aren't shorter in syllables — and the shortcut destroys the meaning that drives recognition."

The Phonetic Test

When Initials Work

Initials work only when there's a phonetic advantage AND the full name is already famous. "GE" is two syllables vs. "General Electric" at six. But "WU" for Western Union? Both are three syllables — no advantage. Los Angeles → "L.A." (four syllables → two). But New York → "N.Y." (two syllables → two) — so people say New York.

The Cause/Effect Error

The Classic Confusion

IBM became rich and famous (the cause) — so everyone recognizes its initials (the effect). Companies try to reverse this: "If we use initials like IBM, we'll become famous like IBM." It doesn't work. You can't become rich by buying corporate jets first. Success must precede the symbols of success.

The Survey Result

The Numbers

Survey of Business Week subscribers: average awareness of "initial" companies was 49%. Average awareness of matched "name" companies was 68% — 19 percentage points higher. And the initial companies in the sample include well-known ones like RCA and CBS that earned their fame under their full names first.

Corporate Suicide by Initials — Real Examples

General Aniline & Film → GAF (the acronym literally means "clumsy error" — a gaffe in more ways than one). Trans World Airlines → TWA (phonetically longer than "Trans World" — two syllables — yet they abandoned the better name). Corn Products Company → CPC International (no phonetic advantage; people still said "Corn Products"). United Shoe Machinery → USM Corporation (nobody knew what USM stood for). Every one of these companies weakened their market position by abandoning communicative names for anonymous letters.

Chapter 11 · The Brand Piggyback Trap

The Free-Ride Trap

The logic seems airtight: "We already spend $20 million a year on the Alka-Seltzer name — let's put that on our new cold remedy." The money looks free. But the ride usually goes off a cliff.

Failure Case

Alka-Seltzer Plus

The logic was perfect: leverage $20M/year already spent on the Alka-Seltzer name. But instead of eating into Dristan and Contac's market, Alka-Seltzer Plus turned around and cannibalized Alka-Seltzer's own antacid business. Consumers thought it was an improved form of Alka-Seltzer rather than a new cold remedy. Every redesign made the "Plus" bigger and the "Alka-Seltzer" smaller — an admission that the name was the problem all along.

Key insight: When a product shares a name with an established brand, it inherits that brand's position — including all its limitations. It does not create a new position; it muddies the old one.

Tide, Cheer, Bold — separately positioned laundry brands
Joy, Cascade — dish products with their own identities
Pampers — not "Ivory Baby Diapers"
51 brands, 2× Colgate's business volume
3× Colgate's profit margin

Colgate Dental Cream, Colgate 100, Colgate Instant Shave...

Chapter 12 · The Marketing Disease of the Decade

The Line-Extension Trap

Line extension — taking an established brand name and putting it on a new product — has swept through the marketing world like Sherman through Georgia. The logic is overwhelming. The results are not.

"Line extension is the marketing sickness of the past decade. The logic is on the side of line extension. Truth, unfortunately, is not."

Classic Line-Extension Failures

Protein 21 Shampoo

Built to 13% market share. Then: hairspray (regular, extra hold, scented, unscented), conditioner, concentrate, Protein 29 for men. Market share collapsed from 13% to 2%.

Scott Paper Products

ScotTowels, ScotTissue, Scotties, Scotkins, BabyScott. On a shopping list, "Scott" means nothing specific. Meanwhile Charmin, Kleenex, and Bounty dominated by meaning one clear thing.

Bayer → Non-Aspirin

If Bayer IS aspirin in the mind, how can it also be non-aspirin? Each extension undercuts the brand's aspirin position. Total Bayer market share declined year after year.

Life Savers Gum

Life Savers means "the candy with the hole." Gum never exceeded a few percent of the market. Quietly killed in 1978. Meanwhile Life Savers' own Bubble Yum — a new, separate brand — became a runaway success.

Eveready Alkaline

Duracell introduced the alkaline category with a new name. Eveready extended their existing brand onto it. Duracell dominates. Eveready wound up copying Duracell's packaging to survive.

Pall Mall's Fatal Extension

Pall Mall was first with 100mm cigarettes — but called them "Pall Mall Gold." Benson & Hedges launched with a pure 100mm identity and owned the position. Pall Mall fell from #1 to irrelevance.

The Teeter-Totter Principle

One name cannot stand for two distinctly different products. When one rises, the other falls:

  • Xerox meant "copier." When Xerox meant "computer" too, it meant neither very convincingly. They wrote off $84.4 million.
  • Heinz owned pickles. Then Heinz conquered ketchup. Vlasic took the pickle position. The teeter-totter moved.
  • The stronger the original brand position, the more line extension damages it — the exact opposite of what companies expect.

The Shopping List Test

Write on a shopping list: "Kleenex, Crest, Listerine, Life Savers, Bayer, Dial." Your spouse returns with tissue, toothpaste, mouthwash, candy, aspirin, soap — every time. Now write: "Heinz, Scott, Protein 21, Kraft." Which Heinz? Which Scott? Which Kraft? Line extension has turned names that should be specific hooks into vague labels that could mean anything.

Chapter 13 · The Exceptions

When Line Extension Can Work

Line extension isn't always fatal — it's a trap, not an absolute prohibition. Understanding the conditions where it works is as important as understanding the dangers.

Short-Term vs. Long-Term

Line extension works like alcohol: stimulant in the short term, depressant in the long term. Initial recognition generates quick sales as retailers stock up. But when reorders don't materialize, the pipeline empties fast. The real damage accumulates silently over years as the brand loses focus and mental position erodes. By the time the damage is visible, it's too late.

The Five Rules: When to Use the House Name

1

Expected Volume

Potential major winners should NOT bear the house name — they deserve their own identity to build a separate position. Small-volume products should use the house name (the economics don't support a new name launch).

2

Competition

In a vacuum with no strong competition, the house name is fine. In a crowded field with established leaders, you need a new name with a distinct position that can be defended.

3

Advertising Support

Big-budget brands should NOT bear the house name — you have resources to build a new position. Small-budget brands should — you can't afford to establish a new identity anyway.

4

Significance

Breakthrough or innovative products should NOT bear the house name — they merit their own identity. Commodity products sold on relationships (chemicals, industrial goods) should use the house name.

5

Distribution

Off-the-shelf consumer items should NOT bear the house name. Products sold by sales representatives (where the company relationship matters more than the brand) should use it.

Successful Reverse Extension: Broaden the Concept, Not the Name

Johnson's Baby Shampoo broadened to adult users successfully — same product, same name, same package, only the application changed. Blue Nun white wine expanded beyond fish to meat courses. Arm & Hammer baking soda expanded to refrigerators. The key difference: same product, same core position, expanded application — rather than a diluted name draped over different products.

Chapters 14–19 · Real-World Application

Six Real-World Positioning Programs

Positioning strategy is universally applicable — to any entity: a company, a country, a product, a service, a bank, an institution. These six cases demonstrate the framework in practice.

Company · Chapter 14

Monsanto: Speaking for the Chemical Industry

In the 1970s, chemicals were under relentless public attack: "chemicals cause cancer" was the prevailing narrative, driven by daily news coverage. Monsanto had three options: fight defensively, ignore the attack, or do something entirely different. They chose to become the industry leader by being first to speak up. "Without chemicals, life itself would be impossible" — positioning Monsanto as the industry's voice, not a defender. Public approval of chemicals rose from 36% to 42% in under two years. Business Week credited Monsanto with "spearheading" the chemical industry's image recovery, noting that even DuPont followed their lead.

Principle: To be recognized as the leader of an industry, do what a leader does — speak for the industry. Being first to take a position costs you the role of follower but earns you the role of spokesperson. DuPont spent $4M following Monsanto's lead. Monsanto got the credit.
Country · Chapter 15

Belgium: "Five Amsterdams"

Sabena Belgian World Airlines faced an existential problem: few tourists wanted to visit Belgium. The key insight shifted the frame entirely — don't position the airline, position the country. And don't compare Belgium to Paris or Rome (already top-of-mind for first-time European travelers). Compare it to something seasoned travelers already knew and wanted: Amsterdam. The Michelin Guide listed five three-star Belgian cities vs. one Dutch three-star city (Amsterdam). The resulting ad: "In beautiful Belgium, there are five Amsterdams." It generated an enormous inquiry response and an angry phone call from the Dutch Minister of Tourism.

Principle: Start with a strongly held perception that already exists — in this case Amsterdam's reputation — then relate your position to it. Credibility borrowed from a trusted third party (Michelin) makes the claim bulletproof. The prospect doesn't need to be convinced; the proof already exists in their minds.
Product · Chapter 16

Milk Duds: The Long-Lasting Candy Bar

Milk Duds had almost no advertising budget compared to Hershey, Nestlé, Mounds, and Snickers. Fighting them head-on was not just difficult — it was impossible. The insight came from listening to 10-year-olds: "A candy bar just doesn't last very long." Kids felt ripped off by shrinking candy bars. Milk Duds' 15 individual chocolate-covered caramels could last far longer than any bar — and the caramel would cement your jaw shut if you tried to rush it. The campaign: "America's long-lasting alternative to the candy bar." Sales reversed a downward trend, and the company sold more Milk Duds than ever in its history.

Principle: The solution to a positioning problem is found in the prospect's mind, not in the product. The millions of dollars competitors spent advertising candy bars became the foundation that made Milk Duds' repositioning possible.
Service · Chapter 17

Mailgram: The Low-Cost Telegram

Western Union's new electronic mail service had to be positioned. Option A: "The high-speed letter" (vs. postal service). Option B: "The low-cost telegram" (vs. Western Union's own telegram, at 3× the price). Logic said avoid Option B — why cannibalize your own business? The test results were stark. "Low-cost Telegram" cities: awareness doubled from 23% to 47%, and volume increased sharply. "High-speed letter" cities: awareness actually declined from 27% to 25%. The conclusion: you cannot walk away from what's already in the prospect's mind. "Western Union" meant telegram. Mailgram needed to hook into that reality, not fight it.

Principle: Position new services against the existing mental model, even when that feels counterintuitive. The data resolved the debate decisively — not because the "low-cost telegram" logic was superior, but because the prospect's mind determined the winner.
Bank · Chapter 18

Long Island Trust: The Local Bank

When Citibank, Chase, and Chemical invaded Long Island, research showed Long Island Trust ranked last in every traditional banking attribute — branches, services, quality, capital. But first in everything "Long Island." Conventional wisdom said: address the weaknesses. Positioning said: start with what the prospect is already willing to give you. The campaign: "Why send your money to the city if you live on the Island?" After 15 months, Long Island Trust moved from last to first in perceived "large capital" and from sixth to fourth in service quality — without actually changing a single service.

Principle: Perception creates reality. You don't have to change the product to change the position. Changing how prospects think about your relationship to them can shift every metric — even ones you've done nothing to improve.
Institution · Chapter 19

The Catholic Church: Teacher of the Word

Post-Vatican II, the Catholic Church had moved away from its old position as "teacher of the law" but had no clear new identity. Regular Mass attendance dropped below 50%, vocations fell 60%, and the Church ranked dead last in perceived influence despite being "the largest community of moral authority in America." The positioning analysis led back to Scripture: Christ's mandate was to go and teach all nations what they had heard. The proposed position: "teacher of the word" — returning to a clear, scripturally grounded role while differentiating from the pre-Vatican II "teacher of the law" identity. Implementation required pulpit training, film, and integrated communication programs built around this single central theme.

Principle: Any institution — no matter how ancient, complex, or large — benefits from positioning thinking. A positioning exercise is always a search for the obvious: the simplest, clearest answer to "What is our fundamental role?"
Chapter 20 · Personal Strategy

Positioning Yourself and Your Career

The same principles that apply to products and companies apply directly to your career. The path to success is rarely found within yourself — it's found by finding a horse to ride.

The Central Insight

"The road to fame and fortune is rarely found within yourself. The only sure way to success is to find yourself a horse to ride. Success in life is based more on what others can do for you than on what you can do for yourself." This is not passivity — it is strategic positioning within systems that are already in motion. Trying harder is rarely the pathway to success. Trying smarter is.

Personal Trap 1

The No-Name Trap

Using initials on memos — "J. S. Smith from GMC" — seems professional. But it only works when everyone already knows who you are. On the way up, you need to burn your full name into management's mind. Use a name, not initials. Write it out.

Personal Trap 2

The Line-Extension Trap

Frank Sinatra Jr. started with two strikes — before any audience heard him, they'd already decided he wasn't as good as his father. Define one specific position for yourself. Specialist, not generalist. The job market belongs to people who can precisely define and position themselves.

Personal Trap 3

The Everybody Trap

Trying to be all things to all people means ending up with nothing. This is just as true for a career as for a product. Pick a lane. Establish a position. The best positioning programs are built on a single, clear concept that can be stated in one sentence.

Six Horses to Ride

1

Your Company

Is it going anywhere? No matter how brilliant you are, it never pays to cast your lot with a loser. Even the best officer on the Titanic wound up in the same lifeboat as the worst. Choose growth industries; avoid dead-end situations.

2

Your Boss

"Hitch your wagon to a star," said Ralph Waldo Emerson. Always try to work for the smartest, most competent person you can find. Many successful people crawled up the ladder right behind someone else. If your boss is going places, you probably are too.

3

A Friend

Most big breaks happen because a business friend recommended you. But you must exercise the friendship regularly — send articles, congratulatory notes, stay in touch. When you need the horse, it needs to be in shape to ride.

4

An Idea

An idea can take you to the top faster than anything else — but you must be willing to expose yourself to ridicule and controversy. You cannot be first with a new idea without sticking your neck out. Never be afraid of conflict. Where would Churchill have been without Hitler?

5

Faith in Others

Ray Kroc bought the McDonald brothers' idea when they had no faith in it themselves. The brothers sold for relatively little. Kroc built a global empire. Finding people with great ideas and no execution — and having the conviction to run with those ideas — is one of the great horses available.

6

Yourself

It is possible to succeed entirely on your own — but it's the hardest horse to ride. Business is a social activity. The jockey who wins is usually the one with the best horse. Pick the best horse available and ride it for all it's worth.

Chapter 21 · The Process

Six Steps to a Positioning Program

Positioning is a disciplined thinking process, not intuition. It starts with the prospect's mind, not the product. These six questions reveal both the problem and the solution.

1

What Position Do You Currently Own?

Start with the mind of the prospect, not the product. Don't let corporate ego distort the answer — get it from the marketplace. Sabena's problem wasn't the airline; it was Belgium's weak tourist image. Seven-Up's problem wasn't its flavor; it was Coke and Pepsi's two-thirds stranglehold on soft drinks. If this requires research, spend the money. It's better to know exactly what you're up against now than to discover it later when nothing can be done.

2

What Position Do You Want to Own?

What position is actually available — not just desirable? Ford failed with the Edsel because the "medium-priced car" position was hopelessly overcrowded. Nyquil succeeded by targeting the specific, unclaimed "nighttime cold remedy" position that Contac and Dristan had left open. Sometimes you can want too much — a position too broad to establish and too broad to defend.

3

Whom Must You Outgun?

Think as much from your competitor's perspective as from your own. Football is easy when you only think about the goal line — what makes it hard is the eleven players in between. If your proposed position requires head-to-head battle against a strong established leader, find a different angle. Going around, under, or over is almost always better than going head-on.

4

Do You Have Enough Money?

It takes money to build a position in the mind, money to hold it once established, and money to fight off competitive challenges. A better approach than underspending nationally: overspend in one city first, build a solid position, then roll out market by market. In a world where 200,000 ad messages hit one person each year, being present but weak is often worse than not being present at all.

5

Can You Stick It Out?

Positioning is cumulative. You must hang in there year after year. Most successful companies rarely change a winning formula — Marlboro's cowboy has been riding into sunsets for decades. Don't change the strategy; only adjust the tactics. A position is like valuable real estate: once you give it up, getting it back may be impossible.

6

Do You Match Your Position?

Creative people resist positioning thinking because it restricts their creativity — and it does. That restriction is the point. "Avis is only No. 2 in rent-a-cars, so why go with us? We try harder" doesn't sound like an ad. It sounds like the strategy presentation. In truth, it's both. The best advertisements ARE the strategy, not a creative interpretation of it.

Chapter 22 · The Mindset

Playing the Positioning Game

Successful positioning requires a specific kind of thinking. Outside-in rather than inside-out. "They" thinking rather than "we" thinking. And an understanding that words are not meanings — they are empty containers that the mind fills.

"We" vs. "They" Thinkers

"We" people are convinced that with proper motivation, anything is possible. They make dynamic speeches about will and determination.

"They" people focus on the competition. They scan the battlefield, seek competitive weaknesses to exploit, avoid competitive strengths.

The outcome of any market battle depends on which side has the better strategy — not better individuals. The differences in fighting abilities of individual employees across competing companies tend to average out. Strategy is what wins.

Words Are Empty Containers

Words don't have meanings. The meanings are in the people using the words. Like a sugar bowl — empty until someone fills it.

The word "Volkswagen" won't hold the concept of a luxury car — so "Audi" holds it instead. "Margarine" hides the product's soy origin — "soy butter" would have communicated it honestly and allowed a different position.

To reposition something, change the container first. The name IS the position, for better or worse.

Six Qualities You Need to Play the Game

Quality 1

Vision

Change is a wave on the ocean of time. Short-term the waves create agitation; long-term the underlying currents matter more. Make decisions about where your company will be in five to ten years. Changing the direction of a large company takes a mile before anything happens — so point yourself in the right direction now.

Quality 2

Courage

The common thread in how every leadership position was established — from Hershey to Hertz — is seizing the initiative before the competition establishes itself. The leader poured in resources while the situation was still fluid. Hershey didn't advertise for decades, feeling too secure — and paid the price when competitors surged past.

Quality 3

Objectivity

Eliminate ego from the decision-making process. You lose your objectivity the moment you think you've found the solution. You need someone to bounce ideas off. Like Ping-Pong, positioning is a game best played by two people — which is why this book was written by two people.

Quality 4

Simplicity

Only an obvious idea will work today. "This problem when solved will be simple" — Boss Kettering's sign at GM Research. If an idea is clever or complicated, be suspicious. The solution to most positioning problems is so simple that thousands of people have looked right at it without seeing it.

Quality 5

Patience

Very few companies can afford to launch nationally. Build the brand in one market, then roll out. Geographic, demographic, and chronological roll-outs all work. Philip Morris built Marlboro into the #1 campus cigarette long before it was #1 nationwide. "The Pepsi Generation" built first among youth, then reaped benefits as they grew up.

Quality 6

Subtlety

The winning position is rarely at the edge of the spectrum — it's near the center. Far right or far left, you'll preempt the position and also lose. The big winners find open positions near the center: unique enough to be distinctive, broad enough to appeal. Both in balance simultaneously.

Summary · The Core Framework

The 20 Core Principles of Positioning

Everything in the book distills to these principles. Internalize them and you will think about marketing — and communication — differently forever.

1
Positioning happens in the mind, not the product

It's not what you do to a product — it's what you do to the mind of the prospect. All else is cosmetic.

2
It's better to be first than to be better

The first brand in the mind earns twice the long-term market share of #2 — on average — and four times that of #3.

3
If you can't be first, create a new category

Amelia Earhart wasn't the first to fly the Atlantic solo — but she was the first woman. New category, first position.

4
The mind rejects information that doesn't match its prior state

Changing minds with advertising is nearly impossible. Work with existing perceptions, not against them.

5
Perceptions are reality

"In politics, the perception is the reality." This applies equally to advertising, business, and every human activity.

6
Simplify ruthlessly — less is more

In communication, as in architecture, less is more. The best advertisement IS the strategy — nothing more, nothing less.

7
Own a word in the mind

Volvo: safety. BMW: driving. FedEx: overnight. Crest: cavities. One word per brand — and use it or lose it.

8
Look for the creneau — the hole in the mind

The follower's strategy: find what position no one owns. Size, price, age, sex, time of day, distribution channel.

9
Relate your brand to what's already in the mind

New concepts need context. The first automobile was a "horseless carriage." 7-Up was "the uncola." Both hooked the new into the old.

10
Never go head-on against an established leader

RCA lost $250M learning this lesson. GE lost more. Go around, under, or over — never head-to-head against an entrenched position.

11
Repositioning the competition creates new positions

Move the old idea out first, then fill the void. Tylenol repositioned aspirin and owned analgesics. Scope repositioned Listerine with two words.

12
The name is the most important single positioning decision

Head & Shoulders, DieHard, Close-Up, Intensive Care — names that begin positioning work from the first moment they're heard.

13
Avoid the no-name trap — initials come after fame, not before

IBM → initials (earned). GAF, USM, CPC → obscurity (chosen). The cause and effect cannot be reversed.

14
Line extension almost always destroys major brand positions

One name cannot stand for two different products. When one rises on the teeter-totter, the other falls. The teeter-totter never stops.

15
New breakthrough products need new names

Xerox Data Systems failed. Taster's Choice beat Maxim. DieHard outsells JCPenney battery. New positions require new hooks in the mind.

16
The power is in the position, not the company

Coca-Cola has power. The Coca-Cola Company merely reflects that power. Outside the cola position, the company earns its way like everyone else.

17
Leaders must cover competitive moves rapidly

J&J cut Tylenol prices before Datril's ads ran. Gillette launched Good News before BIC established itself. Speed of coverage is everything.

18
Consistency wins; changing positions loses

Positioning is cumulative. The F.W.M.T.S. trap (Forgot What Made Them Successful) has destroyed more great brands than competition ever has.

19
Think outside-in, not inside-out

Don't look for the solution inside the product or inside your own head. Look inside the prospect's mind. That's where the battle is fought.

20
Find the horse and ride it — trying harder is rarely the answer

Success comes more from what others can do for you than from what you can do for yourself. Pick the best horse available and ride it for all it's worth.

"In our overcommunicated society, the name of the game today is positioning. And only the better players are going to survive."

— Al Ries & Jack Trout, final words of Positioning: The Battle for Your Mind
