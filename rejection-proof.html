The Beginning

A Dream, a Drawer Full of Blueprints, and One Spark

July 4, 2012. A pregnant wife. A fireworks sky. And a man who had quietly surrendered his deepest dream to fear.

Jia Jiang grew up in Beijing dreaming of becoming the next Bill Gates. At 14, he tore down his sports posters and replaced them with biographies of Edison and Konosuke Matsushita. He wrote a letter to his family promising to buy Microsoft by age 25.

He moved to America alone at 16. His first host family robbed him. He recovered, learned English with thousands of flash cards, earned a computer science degree from BYU, then an MBA from Duke. He landed a six-figure corporate job. From the outside: perfect.

But the dream was suffocating. When his uncle dismissed his shoe-skate invention idea, Jia buried the blueprint and never pursued it. Years later, Roger Adams patented the exact same idea — and founded Heelys, valued at ~$1 billion after its IPO. Jia watched kids skating on Heelys in malls and felt the unbearable weight of what might have been.

Sitting at Independence Day fireworks, his pregnant wife Tracy beside him, Jia realized his entrepreneurial dream might die not from failure — but from fear of rejection. Tracy issued a challenge: quit your job, give yourself six months, build something real, leave no regret.

When the investor rejected his start-up app four months in, Jia discovered Rejection Therapy — a concept developed by Canadian entrepreneur Jason Comely. He took it further: 100 days, 100 attempts, all video-recorded and shared with the world. What happened next changed everything.

Neuroscience & Evolution

Why Rejection Hurts So Much

It is not weakness or thin skin. Rejection activates the same brain chemistry as physical pain — and that is by evolutionary design. Understanding this changes everything.

🧠

The Opioid Response

University of Michigan researchers found that social rejection causes the brain to release natural opioids — the same painkilling chemicals triggered by physical injury. More stunning: participants were told the rejections were fake before the study. The brains released opioids anyway. The body cannot tell the difference between social and physical pain.

🦕

10,000 Years of Fear

Our ancestors lived in small tribes. Rejection by the group meant death — facing predators alone, without food or protection. Natural selection hardwired this fear into our DNA. That instinct still runs in our genetic code today, even though there are no mastodons in downtown Austin and a stranger's "no" will not get us eaten. Rejection fear is an evolutionary appendix: vestigial, but capable of causing real harm.

😂

Laughter: The Biological Antidote

Oxford evolutionary psychologist Robin Dunbar found that participants endured significantly more pain while watching comedy (Simpsons, South Park) than neutral or feel-good content — because laughter releases endorphins, a different class of opioids that both fight pain and generate pleasure. Dunbar believes laughter may have evolved specifically to bond human groups. It is a double shot of natural painkillers — which is exactly why Jia's funniest requests hurt the least.

🔬

Rejection vs. Failure

Failure can be blamed on timing, skill gaps, or market conditions. Rejection feels inescapably personal because another human being is specifically choosing against you. In Silicon Valley, entrepreneurs proudly collect failures. Nobody brags about rejections. The distinction matters: failure is impersonal, rejection is intimate — and your DNA treats it as a threat to tribal survival.

⚔️

The Revenge Trap

Colgate social psychologist Kevin Carlsmith ran lab experiments on perceived injustice. Everyone who could take revenge on a wrongdoer did so — and then felt worse than those who had no revenge option. We believe lashing back will restore dignity. It doesn't. Real-world evidence: school shootings and acid attacks trace back to the same cycle of rejection-pain escalating into catastrophic revenge.

🧙

The Wizard of Oz Effect

Like the terrifying Wizard behind the curtain, rejection feels catastrophic from a distance. When Toto pulls back the curtain, a harmless gray-haired man is running a machine. The fear is almost always worse than the actual rejection. Facing rejection 100 times gave Jia's brain the lived evidence it needed to finally believe: "No" is survivable — and often quite funny.

The Crucial Distinction

Rejection vs. Failure

We have conferences about failure, memes about failure, TED Talks about failure. But rejection? Silence. Here is why they are fundamentally different experiences.

Failure

📊Attributable to timing, market, execution — external factors absorb the shame
🏆Silicon Valley treats failures as badges of honor — "fail fast, learn fast"
🔄Yields lessons: "I made mistakes," "I'll improve," "I'm wiser now"
🗣️Discussed openly — entrepreneurs tell failure stories for credibility and relatability
😌Even feels noble sometimes — "I tried, I learned, I grew"
VS

Rejection

👤Always involves another person actively choosing against you — impossible to externalize
🤫Treated as shameful, rarely discussed, deeply personal — almost taboo
⚡Triggers opioid release — the brain processes it as a physical wound
😤When unjust, provokes rage and revenge fantasies — which make everything worse
🧬Evolution treats it as an existential threat — fight or flee, immediately

"Rejection doesn't just feel like a rejection of our request — it feels like a rejection of our character, looks, intelligence, and beliefs. Even when the rejector means nothing personal, it will feel deeply personal. The pain is real and it is chemical."

The 100 Days

Key Experiments & What They Revealed

Each rejection attempt was a laboratory. The experiments ranged from absurd to profound — and every one produced a lesson that no business school teaches.

Day 1
💵

Borrow $100 from a Security Guard

❌ Rejected — but the guard asked "Why?"

Jia walked up to the office building security guard, phone recording, heart pounding, and asked to borrow $100. The guard said no — but then paused: "Why?"

Paralyzed by shame, Jia fled without answering. Watching the replay that night, he realized the guard had offered to continue the conversation — an invitation he had completely missed because he was desperate to escape.

Lesson: Fear turns us into mumbling idiots who flee before the conversation can even begin. Confidence and composure change the entire experience of being rejected — for both people.
Day 3 ⭐ VIRAL
🍩

Olympic Ring Donuts at Krispy Kreme

✅ YES — and they were free

Jia asked shift leader Jackie Braun to make five interlinked donuts in the shape of the Olympic rings — in 15 minutes. Instead of saying no, Jackie pulled out paper, sketched the design, thought through the fryer logistics, and disappeared into the kitchen.

Minutes later: perfect Olympic ring donuts, iced in the correct five colors. Free of charge. Jackie's smile was "the kind you see only on someone who has made another person happy."

The video went viral: 15,000+ Reddit upvotes, Yahoo! front page, featured in dozens of countries. Krispy Kreme stock jumped 29% that week. Jackie became famous for her generosity.

Lesson: The world is far more generous than fear makes it seem. Amazing things happen the moment you find the courage to ask — things that are simply impossible without asking.
Day 6
⚽

Play Soccer in a Stranger's Backyard

✅ Scott the Cowboys fan said yes

Wearing cleats and holding a soccer ball, Jia knocked on a random door mid-Dallas Cowboys overtime. Scott stared at the cleats for a long moment, then said: "I guess so."

Why did he say yes? "It was so off the wall — how could I say no?" The sheer outrageousness of the request triggered curiosity rather than suspicion. Scott missed his overtime to snap a photo of a stranger playing soccer in his yard.

Lesson: Outlandish requests can be paradoxically easier to grant than ordinary ones. Personal curiosity on the part of the person being asked — not your strategy — often determines the outcome.
Day ~20
✈️

Give the Welcome Announcement on a Southwest Flight

✅ YES — earned a free drink

Flight attendant Jeff handed Jia the microphone. With 130 passengers staring, he announced himself as "just a customer who thinks Southwest is always on time, always friendly, always awesome" — and asked for applause. He got it.

But this attempt nearly broke him. His DNA was screaming: "You are in danger! Someone will think you're a terrorist and tackle you!" A public rejection by 130 people simultaneously felt incomparably more threatening than any private one.

Lesson: Fear of rejection is biological, not just psychological. You are fighting 10,000 years of evolution. But every time you survive the terror, the wiring slowly changes.
Day ~45
🌹

Plant a Rosebush in a Stranger's Yard

↗ Rejected → Referred to neighbor

A white-haired man said no — his dog would dig it up. But Jia stayed in the conversation and asked why. The man explained, then added: "Go across the street to Lauren. She loves flowers."

Lauren said yes immediately and was delighted with the peach rosebush. The "why" question transformed a rejection into a referral — and a planted rosebush in a stranger's garden.

Lesson: "Why?" is the most powerful word after a rejection. It reveals the true reason — usually impersonal — and sometimes opens a door that didn't seem to exist.
Day ~35
🏢

Find a Job in One Day (Walk Into Offices Cold)

✅ Hired by BigCommerce on 3rd try

After two stern rejections, Jia walked into a third office where Jennifer Carrier — raised by a Southern hospitality mom and a salesman dad, trained at modeling school, waitress-turned-office-manager — greeted him warmly and offered a trial assistant position.

Lesson: The same request gets polar-opposite responses from different people. Rejection reveals the rejector's history and personality — not the universal merit of your ask. It is almost never about you.
Day ~50
🥐

Order McGriddles at 2 PM (Breakfast-Only Item)

↗ Partial YES — honey griddle cake

The clerk said no (they'd cleaned the egg machine). Jia retreated: "Do you have something like a McGriddles?" The clerk offered a honey-roasted griddle cake with cheese. Jia took it, claimed victory over a fan's "impossible" challenge.

Lesson: Don't rout — retreat strategically. When the exact ask fails, retreat to a lesser ask. Every "no" is surrounded by invisible yeses. Your job is to find them.
Day ~40
✂️

Ask a Dog Groomer at PetSmart to Cut His Hair

❌ Rejected — with uncontrollable laughter

Jia asked the groomer how much a "hair trimming" would cost. "What kind of dog?" — "Mine. My own hair." Four groomers erupted in laughter. He pushed: "Can you treat me like a Tibetan mastiff? I'll sit, I won't bark." Laughter intensified. No one minded the rejection.

Lesson: Laughter is a biological antidote to rejection pain. When humor is present, endorphins suppress the pain. The opioid alarm never fires. Jia left PetSmart feeling great despite the "no."
Day 100 🏆
🔍

Help Tracy Get a Job at Google

✅ Google reversed its own rejection

Tracy quit her job and spent 6 months applying rejection-proof principles: hundreds of networking messages (nearly half responded), four formal rejections, authentic interviews where she focused only on what she could control. Each "no" was treated as data, not verdict.

After the fourth rejection, the recruiter was so moved by how gracefully Tracy handled each "no" that he advocated for her. The team reversed course. "I've never seen anyone take rejection so positively — never. That broke my heart."

Lesson: Rejection is so feeble it can even change. And how you take a no can matter as much as what you asked for. Tracy's number was four — though it felt like four hundred.
Complete Framework

The Rejection Toolbox

After 100 days and thousands of human interactions, Jia distilled the principles of rejection into a practical framework organized across five domains. This is the complete system.

01 Rethinking Rejection — New Mental Models
Principle 1

Rejection Is Human

Every rejection has at least two parties. The response you receive usually reveals more about the rejector — their mood, history, needs, and cultural background — than about you or your request.

Jennifer at BigCommerce said yes to the same request that two other office managers refused. Same Jia. Same words. Completely different people — completely different outcomes.
Principle 2

Rejection Is an Opinion

The rejector is offering their perspective, filtered through their psychology, upbringing, cultural context, and current circumstances. It is not objective truth about the merit of your idea or your worth as a person.

Jia's uncle rejected his shoe-skate idea. Heelys sold for ~$1B. The uncle gave an opinion, not a verdict.
Principle 3

Rejection Has a Number

If you ask enough people, a "no" will eventually become a "yes." There is no idea — however unconventional — that will be universally rejected by every person on Earth. The question is only how many asks it takes.

J.K. Rowling: 12 rejections before Harry Potter. Kathryn Stockett: 60 before The Help. Robert Pirsig: 121 — a Guinness record.
02 Taking a No — What to Do After Rejection
Tactic 1

Ask "Why" Before Good-bye

Do not flee after a rejection. Stay in the conversation and ask why. This single question often reveals the rejection was never personal — and can uncover an unexpected path forward.

The man who didn't want a rosebush explained his dog would dig it up — and then referred Jia to a flower-loving neighbor who said yes immediately.
Tactic 2

Retreat, Don't Run

When the exact ask fails, retreat to a lesser ask rather than abandoning entirely. Like military retreats vs. routs — retreating strategically preserves optionality. Running creates a rout of your own making.

"Can't do McGriddles" → "Do you have something like a McGriddles?" → Honey griddle cake with cheese. A partial win beats a complete loss.
Tactic 3

Collaborate, Don't Contend

Arguing with a rejector is the most reliable way to ensure a permanent "no." Framing them as a collaborator — someone helping you solve a shared problem — transforms adversaries into partners.

A documentary crew argued with a music studio employee: he shut down completely. Jia stepped in, said "we know it's unusual and you can say no" — the employee immediately said yes and played drums for them.
Tactic 4

Switch Up, Don't Give Up

Before quitting, try asking a different person, in a different environment, or under different circumstances. NBA reject Stephon Marbury moved to Beijing, led a team to its first-ever championship, and had a bronze statue built in his likeness.

Jia's job search: 2 rejections, then Jennifer at BigCommerce. Same request, different variables — completely different result.
03 Positioning for Yes — Increasing the Odds
Strategy 1

Give Your "Why"

Always explain the reason behind your request. Harvard psychologist Ellen Langer's landmark "Copy Machine" study found that requests with a reason (even a trivial one) succeeded 93–94% of the time, versus 60% without any reason at all.

In New York City, Jia asked dozens of random strangers to pose in photos with him — and told them why (people are part of a city's fabric). Every single one said yes.
Strategy 2

Start with "I"

Make it clear you are asking for a favor rather than pretending to benefit them. UT Austin researcher James Pennebaker found "I"-statements are perceived as more honest. Never manufacture false benefits for the other person without genuinely knowing their interests.

The hairdresser incident: Jia said "you must be bored with your job" — she was deeply offended. He didn't know her. Starting with "I would love to cut your hair" would have been honest and respectful.
Strategy 3

Acknowledge Doubts

Preemptively name the obvious objections or strangeness of your request. This disarms suspicion, demonstrates empathy, and paradoxically builds credibility — because it signals you are not crazy, just honest.

At Starbucks: "Is that weird?" → barista Eric visibly relaxed, felt understood, and said yes. Domino's ran ads brutally criticizing their own old pizza — and recorded a historic 14.3% sales jump.
Strategy 4

Target the Audience

The finest request aimed at the wrong audience is wasted. Match your ask to people who are most likely to be receptive by context, interest, personality, and circumstances.

Joshua Bell, Grammy-winning violinist, earned almost nothing busking anonymously in a DC metro. Days earlier, the same performance earned a standing ovation at the Kennedy Center. Same talent. Wrong audience.
04 Giving a No — The Art of Graceful Rejection
Delivery 1

Patience and Respect

The way you deliver a "no" can matter more than the rejection itself. Jordan at 24 Hour Fitness was so patient and respectful that his "no" felt like a "yes" — he even found Jia an alternative gym through his personal contact.

Kelly Blazek's rude LinkedIn rejection email went viral and destroyed a reputation built over years of genuine volunteer work. Jackie Braun's generous yes made Krispy Kreme's stock jump 29%.
Delivery 2

Be Direct

Avoid two forms of indirect rejection: "big setups" (long preambles before the bad news) and "yes-buts" (false acknowledgment followed by rejection). Apple forbids the word "unfortunately" in customer service because it undermines trust.

Microsoft's Stephen Elop buried layoff news for 12,500 employees in paragraph 11 of an 1,100-word memo. The response: ugly protests and global PR coverage headlined "How Not to Cut 12,500 Jobs."
Delivery 3

Offer Alternatives

When you must say no, offering alternatives signals you are rejecting the request — not the person. This crucial distinction turns potential enemies into loyal advocates who remember your generosity.

Costco manager Robert couldn't allow the intercom request. He bought Jia and his family dinner and suggested the company magazine. Jia became a vocal fan. Robert later recognized by customers who saw the video.
05 Finding Power — Detachment and Inner Freedom
Core Principle

Detachment from Results

Buddhism, Hinduism, Tao Te Ching, and the Bhagavad Gita all converge on this: detach from outcomes, focus on what you can control. Jia and Tracy made two lists — controllable (effort, networking, authenticity) and uncontrollable (whether someone says yes). They poured everything into the first list and released the second.

UCLA basketball coach John Wooden, who won 10 NCAA championships in 11 years, never talked to his team about winning. His metric was effort and preparation — not results. Tracy's Google recruiter reversed course precisely because she was detached: "She took it so positively — I've never seen that. It broke my heart, and I wanted to advocate for her."

Jack Canfield (Chicken Soup for the Soul): "Live with high intention and low attachment." Lao Tzu: "Care about people's approval and you will be their prisoner."
Freedom 1

Freedom to Ask

A 2011 Accenture study found only 44–48% of working Americans have ever asked for a raise. Yet 85% of those who ask receive something. We are systematically self-rejecting — withholding the very asks that could change our lives.

Freedom 2

Freedom to Accept Yourself

Constant approval-seeking forces us to bend into shapes that are not our own. What we need is not acceptance from others first — it is acceptance from ourselves. Internal worth should be the prerequisite to seeking external validation, not its reward.

Research Deep Dive

The Science Behind the Principles

Jia's discoveries weren't just personal intuitions. They map directly onto decades of peer-reviewed research in behavioral psychology, social influence, and evolutionary biology.

Harvard · 1978
Ellen Langer · Psychology

The Copy Machine Study

Langer asked strangers at a copy machine if she could cut in front of them. With no reason: 60% complied. With a real reason ("I'm in a rush"): 94%. With a nonsensical reason ("because I have to make copies"): 93%. Even a trivially circular reason triggered compliance.

🔑 The brain needs to hear any reason to lower its guard. Always give your "why" — even a simple one dramatically changes outcomes.
U Michigan Medical School
Neuroimaging Research

Social Rejection Triggers Opioid Release

Brain scans showed that when participants were told potential dates rejected them, their brains immediately released natural opioids — identical to the response to physical injury. Crucially: participants knew the profiles and rejections were fabricated before the study. The brains released opioids anyway. The body cannot distinguish social pain from physical pain.

🔑 "Don't take it personally" is biologically useless advice. The pain is real, chemical, and automatic. Empathy — not dismissal — is what helps.
Oxford University
Robin Dunbar · Evolutionary Psychology

Laughter Measurably Reduces Pain

Participants exposed to identical physical pain (frozen sleeves, wall-sits) could endure significantly more pain while watching comedy than neutral or positive-but-not-funny content. Only genuine laughter — not just happiness — triggered the effect. Dunbar believes laughter evolved specifically to bond social groups, and produces endorphins that fight pain and create pleasure simultaneously.

🔑 Humor is a biological weapon against rejection pain. When you make a request amusing, the opioid alarm never fires. Inject humor wherever possible.
Yale University
Stanley Milgram · Social Psychology

The Milgram Shock Experiment

Ordinary people delivered potentially fatal electric shocks to strangers — not because they were cruel, but because an authority figure in a lab coat told them to. The experiment demonstrated that social context, authority signals, and group pressure can override individual moral judgment, sometimes completely.

🔑 People's opinions and rejections are heavily shaped by social forces outside their control. This is why rejection is an opinion — not objective truth about your merit.
U of Pennsylvania
Jennifer Mueller · Psychology

The Bias Against Creativity

People say they love creativity. But Mueller's research showed that subconsciously, novel ideas trigger anxiety because they're uncertain and disruptive to established order. People consistently reject genuinely creative ideas in favor of comfortable, conventional ones — even while insisting they value innovation. The more creative the idea, the higher the probability of subconscious rejection.

🔑 If your creative idea is rejected, it may be evidence of its worth, not its weakness. George Bernard Shaw: "All great truths begin as blasphemies."
UT Austin
James Pennebaker · Social Psychology

The Pronoun Study

Analysis of e-mail language patterns revealed that people who use "I" more are perceived as more honest and sincere. People who favor "you," "he/she/they" as subjects are more likely to be seen as distancing themselves or being evasive. Utility companies that write "to better serve you" before raising fees are rarely believed.

🔑 Start requests with "I" — it signals authentic honesty. Pretending a request benefits someone else, without genuinely knowing their interests, reads as manipulation.
The Hidden Gift

Finding the Upside in Rejection

Every rejection has hidden value — if you are willing to look for it. Jia identified four specific ways rejection can become a resource rather than a setback.

🔥

Motivation

Michael Jordan's Hall of Fame speech was essentially a thank-you note to every person who rejected him — from the high school coach who cut him from varsity to the media that said he wasn't as good as Magic or Bird. Tom Brady cried at the draft, being pick #199. He went on to win multiple Super Bowls. The rage of proving doubters wrong is one of the most powerful human motivators.

"You look for any kind of messages that people may say or do to get you motivated to play at the highest level." — Michael Jordan

📈

Self-Improvement

Jia stood at an Austin intersection soliciting charity donations and changed his sign four times based on how cars responded. Each rejection was a data point. The best product developers treat rejection as a feedback loop — A/B testing their approach in real-time without emotional attachment to any particular version. What works is what matters.

💎

Worthiness

University of Pennsylvania research shows we subconsciously reject creative ideas because novelty is uncomfortable. If your idea is radical enough to be called "incredibly stupid" by a close friend, consider whether you might be onto something important. Jia's best friend later admitted he would have talked Jia out of the video blog entirely — "it sounded incredibly stupid."

🦁

Character Building

Courage is a muscle. Jia gave a 23-minute speech to 3,000 people at the World Domination Summit and walked off to a standing ovation — because he had already given the same speech to 6 people on an Austin street where most passersby ignored him. The small rejections are training for the large arenas. You cannot skip the street corner.

Finding Meaning

When Rejection Goes Deeper Than a "No"

Sometimes facing rejection is not about getting a yes or a no. Sometimes it is about enduring rejection because there is a profound reason — a cause that matters more than the pain.

🪖 Major Scotty Smiley

In 2005, Army lieutenant Scotty Smiley was blinded by a suicide car bomb in Iraq. He felt rejected by God: "My life, in my opinion, was taken from me." He faced losing the career that defined him.

Instead of quietly retiring, Smiley petitioned to remain on active duty. He became the army's first blind active-duty officer. He climbed mountains, skied, surfed, skydived, completed a triathlon, received a Purple Heart and Bronze Star, won the ESPY Best Outdoor Athlete award, and taught leadership at West Point.

"I didn't want to be Lt. Dan Taylor — bitter about what happened to me. I chose to define myself by my reaction, not by my injury."

📖 Viktor Frankl

In Nazi concentration camps, prisoners were stripped of everything: dignity, comfort, security, justice, loved ones. Death was decided randomly by sadistic guards. Frankl observed that even in this total abyss, one freedom could not be taken: the freedom to choose your attitude toward suffering.

Having meaning — whether it was spiritual growth, caring for other prisoners, or preserving one's inner life — not only prevented despair but gave people the strength to survive what seemed unsurvivable.

"We may not have freedom to control our situations, but we always have freedom to find meaning in every experience — even in rejection." — Frankl

😊 Massoud Adibpour: Make DC Smile

Every Monday morning, Adibpour holds large signs with positive messages at a busy DC intersection — waving, smiling, asking passersby to join him. Week after week, through heat and cold, with no monetary reward. Most people ignored or rejected him.

Why did he keep going? Because he found that making others happy made him happier. The rejections did not erode his purpose — they reinforced it. His cause inoculated him against the pain.

"You can't buy happiness. Rejection is terrible, especially when the weather is cold. But gradually, as I made others happy, I became happier myself."

🎤 Louis C.K.: 8 Years of Empty Rooms

Louis C.K.'s first stand-up attempt ended after 90 seconds when the audience stared in silence. The emcee mocked him for 10 minutes afterward. "It was a terrible feeling and I wanted to die."

Saturday Night Live director Jim Downey came to Boston looking for talent and selected every single comedian who auditioned — except Louis C.K. Yet he still didn't quit. For 8 years he performed to empty rooms because clubs required a show "in case someone walked in."

"A little time went by and I was like, well, I didn't die, and I'm still interested. I made it through the bad feeling. I can handle feeling that bad."

Transformation

Before and After 100 Days

The journey didn't just teach Jia tactics. It fundamentally rewired how he experienced the world, himself, and other people.

😰 Before: Rejection-Fearing Mind

  • 🔴Treated rejection as a personal verdict on his worth and intelligence
  • 🔴Fled every "no" without asking why, running before the conversation ended
  • 🔴Constantly sought approval from family, colleagues, and strangers
  • 🔴Buried his own ideas at the first sign of resistance or skepticism
  • 🔴Surrendered a decade of entrepreneurship because his uncle didn't like one idea
  • 🔴Monitored others' faces for approval signals during every conversation
  • 🔴Told himself not to "bother people" with asks and requests
  • 🔴Cried alone in a closet, trapped by others' expectations, unable to act

✨ After: Rejection-Proof Mind

  • 🟢Understands rejection reveals more about rejectors than about himself
  • 🟢Stays in conversations after "no," asks why, retreats strategically

Build Your Own Rejection Practice

Courage is a muscle. It weakens without use. Here is a progressive practice, inspired by Jia's journey, to build rejection immunity week by week.

Week 1 · Beginner
👋

Low-Stakes Asks

Ask for a table upgrade at a restaurant. Request a discount at a coffee shop. Ask someone for directions you don't need. Practice the mechanics of asking with low emotional investment.

Week 2 · Building
❓

Practice the "Why"

When you get a "no" this week — in any context — pause. Instead of retreating, ask: "I understand completely. Can I ask why?" Do this once a day. Notice what you discover.

Week 3 · Advancing
🎯

The Real Ask

Identify one thing you have been afraid to ask for — a raise, a date, a creative collaboration, a second chance. Plan your "why," acknowledge doubts upfront, target the right person. Then ask.

Week 4 · Expert
🔥

The Outlandish Request

Design your own Jia-style challenge. Find a request that seems absurd but is harmless — something that makes you laugh, might make someone's day, and reminds you the world is kinder than fear suggests.

The Three Transforming Insights

🧩

Rejection Is Not About You

The same request gets wildly different responses from different people. The "universe" is not a single judge rendering a verdict on your worth — it is billions of individuals with different histories, contexts, and needs. Rejection reflects the rejector.

🌊

Every No Leads Somewhere

The rosebush rejection became a referral. The failed start-up rejection became the viral blog. The Google rejections became Google reversing course. Every "no" creates a path — if you stay in the conversation and ask why.

🔓

Freedom Lives on the Other Side

Real freedom — to ask, to dream, to take risks, to be yourself without needing everyone's approval — lives on the far side of rejection fear. The only path through it is through it. There are no shortcuts. Ask anyway.
