Introduction

Typography is a tool for doing things with.

Shaping content, giving language a physical body, enabling the social flow of messages — typography is an ongoing tradition that connects designers with one another, past and future.

Thinking with Type is assembled in three sections: Letter, Text, and Grid, building from the basic atom of the letterform to the organization of words into coherent bodies and flexible systems. Each section opens with a narrative about the cultural and theoretical issues that fuel typographic design. The demonstration pages that follow show not just how typography is structured, but why.

This book is not about fonts. It is about how to use them. Typefaces are an essential resource employed by graphic designers, just as glass, stone, steel, and other materials are employed by architects. To use type with wit and wisdom requires knowledge of how — and why — letterforms have evolved.

"Typography is an interface to the alphabet. User theory tends to favor normative solutions over innovative ones, pushing design into the background. Readers usually ignore the typographic interface, gliding comfortably along literacy's habitual groove. Sometimes, however, the interface should be allowed to fail." — Ellen Lupton

Part One

Letter

From the origins of movable type to the digital font — how letterforms evolved over five centuries

History

Five Centuries of Typographic Evolution

Words originated as gestures of the body. The history of typography reflects a continual tension between the hand and the machine, the organic and the geometric, the human body and the abstract system.

c. 1450

Gutenberg & Movable Type

Johannes Gutenberg revolutionized writing in the West. His famous Bible took the handmade manuscript as its model, emulating the dense "blackletter" script by creating variations of each letter and numerous ligatures. Movable type allowed mass production of text for the first time.

c. 1465–1490

Humanism: Nicolas Jenson & the Roman Type

In fifteenth-century Italy, humanist scholars rejected gothic scripts for lettera antica, a classical mode of handwriting with wider, more open forms. Nicolas Jenson established an influential press in Venice. His typefaces — the first Roman types — had strong vertical stems and transitions from thick to thin that emulate a broad-nibbed pen.

1529

Enlightenment: The Body in Letters

Geofroy Tory linked letter anatomy to human anatomy. A royal committee under Louis XIV created the romain du roi — letters constructed on a finely meshed grid, distanced from calligraphy. Printers like Caslon (1720s) and Baskerville (1750s) refined contrast and precision; contemporaries accused Baskerville of "blinding all the Readers in the Nation."

1800s

Monster Fonts & Industrial Typography

Bodoni and Didot carried sharpness to new extremes, unleashing a new world where structural attributes of the letter would be subject to bizarre experiments. The rise of industrialization brought Fat Faces, Egyptian slabs, Gothic letterforms, and wood type at gigantic scales. The pantograph (1834) mechanized wood-type manufacture, treating the alphabet as a flexible system divorced from calligraphy.

1900s–1930s

Reform: De Stijl, Bauhaus, Futura

Avant-garde artists reduced the alphabet to perpendicular elements and geometric forms. Herbert Bayer's universal typeface at the Bauhaus (1925) used only lowercase letters built from straight lines and circles. Paul Renner's Futura (1927) embodied avant-garde obsessions in a commercially available typeface.

1967

Programmed Typography

Wim Crouwel published designs for a "New Alphabet" constructed from straight lines, designed for optimal display on CRT screens. He proposed a design methodology where decisions are rule-based and systematic — an "introduction for a programmed typography."

1984–present

Digital Revolution & the Screen

Zuzana Licko began designing typefaces that exploited the rough grain of early desktop systems. The 1990s brought fonts of chaos and decay (Template Gothic, Dead History), but also refined workhorse families. Today, Gotham, Mrs Eaves, and thousands of OpenType superfamilies coexist in the vast digital library.

Anatomy

The Parts of a Letter

Visually speaking, baselines and x-heights determine the real edges of text. Understanding the anatomy of letterforms is fundamental to making informed typographic decisions.

Rg
Baseline The invisible line on which all letters sit. The most stable axis along a line of text, crucial for aligning type with images.
x-height Height of the main body of the lowercase letter (the letter "x"). The larger the x-height relative to cap height, the bigger the letters appear.
Cap Height The distance from the baseline to the top of a capital letter. This determines the letter's point size.
Ascender The part of a lowercase letter that extends above the x-height (as in b, d, f, h, k, l, t).
Descender The part of a lowercase letter that falls below the baseline (as in g, j, p, q, y).
Serif The finishing stroke at the end of a letterform. Derived from calligraphic pen strokes; transformed from organic end-stroke into geometric element.
Stem The main vertical stroke of a letter — its structural backbone.
Bowl The curved stroke that creates an enclosed or partially enclosed space in letters like b, d, o, p, q.
Counter The enclosed or partially enclosed space within a letterform (inside an O or P). Important for legibility at small sizes.
Spine The main curved stroke of the letter S — distinguishing feature of this particular letterform.
Terminal The end of a stroke that does not use a serif — a freely ending stroke, often rounded or tapered.
Ligature A glyph that combines two or more letters into a single form — such as fi or fl — to avoid awkward collisions.

Size

Measuring Type

One point equals 1/72 inch or .35 millimeters. Twelve points equal one pica. Typography can also be measured in inches, millimeters, or pixels.

A typeface is measured from the top of the capital letter to the bottom of the lowest descender, plus a small buffer space. This is the point size. But point size does not tell you how big a letter actually looks — x-height does.

When two typefaces are set in the same point size, one often looks bigger than the other. Differences in x-height, line weight, and set width affect apparent scale.

The set width is the body of the letter plus a sliver of space that protects it from other letters. You can change the set width by using horizontal or vertical scaling — but this distorts line weight. Instead, choose a typeface that has the proportions you need: condensed, compressed, wide, or extended.

Optical sizes: In the era of metal type, different punches were cut for each size. Display styles have delicate, lyrical forms; caption styles have heavier strokes. Today, some OpenType families (like Adobe Garamond Premiere Pro) include optical size variants.

Classification

Families of Type

A basic system for classifying typefaces was devised in the nineteenth century. Humanist letterforms are closely connected to calligraphy and the hand. Transitional and modern forms are more abstract and geometric.

Rg

Humanist / Old Style

Emulate classical calligraphy. Diagonal stress, bracketed serifs. Examples: Garamond, Bembo, Jenson, Palatino, Sabon. Named for printers of the 15th–16th centuries.

Rg

Transitional

Sharper serifs, more vertical axis than humanist. Introduced mid-18th century. Examples: Baskerville, Times New Roman, Caslon. Considered shocking in their day.

Rg

Modern / Didone

Radically abstract. Thin, straight serifs; vertical axis; sharp contrast between thick and thin strokes. Examples: Bodoni, Didot, Walbaum. Designed late 18th century.

Aa

Egyptian / Slab Serif

Heavy, slablike serifs. Introduced 1806, quickly denounced as "a typographical monstrosity." Popular in advertising. Examples: Clarendon, Rockwell, Memphis.

Rg

Humanist Sans

Calligraphic variations in line weight. Note the small counter in the letter 'a'. Examples: Gill Sans, Frutiger, Myriad. More organic than geometric sans.

Rg

Transitional Sans

Uniform, upright characters. "Anonymous" sans serif. Examples: Helvetica, Arial, Univers. One of the world's most widely used type styles.

Rg

Geometric Sans

Built around geometric forms. Perfect circular O's, sharp triangle peaks. Examples: Futura, Avenir, Gotham. Embody the obsessions of the avant-garde.

Rg

Monospace / Display

Each character occupies the same width. Originally for typewriters and code. Display faces designed for large sizes — headlines, posters — not sustained reading.

Superfamilies A superfamily consists of dozens of related fonts in multiple weights and widths — often with both sans-serif and serif versions. Examples include Scala + Scala Sans (Martin Majoor), Thesis (Lu(cas) de Groot with 21 weights), and Univers (Adrian Frutiger, designed as a total system from inception). Small capitals and non-lining numerals, once found only in serif fonts, are now included in sans-serif superfamily members.

Capitals

Caps, Small Caps & Mixed Case

A word set in ALL CAPS within running text can look big and bulky. Small capitals are designed to match the x-height of lowercase letters — slightly taller than the lowercase x-height, but not as tall as full capitals.

Many designers prefer to use all small caps (no ascending elements), creating a clean line. Lines of text set in all caps can be tightly line-spaced because they have no ascenders or descenders.

Pseudo small caps — automatically generated by software — are merely shrunken versions of full-size caps. They look puny, underweight, and do not match the stroke weight of surrounding text. They are "an abomination against nature."

Only use small caps when they are officially included with the type family. When working with OpenType fonts (labeled Pro), access small caps via the Character Options → OpenType menu.

Numerals

Lining vs. Old Style

Lining (Modern) Numerals

1234567890

Uniform height (same as capitals). Uniform width (tabular). Introduced around 1900 for business — designed to line up in columns. Look large and bulky in running text.

Old Style (Non-Lining) Numerals

Punctuation

Typography's Silent Marks

Designers and editors must master typographic conventions in addition to grammatical rules. A pandemic error is the use of straight prime or hatch marks (often called "dumb quotes") in place of apostrophes and quotation marks (also known as "curly quotes," "typographer's quotes," or "smart quotes").

❌ Crime

"Hello," she said.

Straight prime marks (dumb quotes) — these indicate inches and feet, not quotation. A common blight across the typographic landscape.

✓ Correct

"Hello," she said.

Curly quotes — distinct open and close forms. Option+[ and Shift+Option+[ on Mac. Know thy keystrokes!

❌ Crime

It's a dog--its life.

Double hyphens instead of em dash. No spaces around dashes.

✓ Correct

It's a dog — its life.

Em dash (Shift+Option+Hyphen on Mac). Expresses a strong grammatical break. En dash (Option+Hyphen) connects numbers: 1914–1918.

Typefaces on Screen

The Web Typography Revolution

During the early years of the web, designers were forced to work within the narrow range of typefaces commonly installed on end-user computers. Verdana and Georgia (both designed by Matthew Carter for Microsoft in 1996) were among the handful that could be reliably used online.

Anti-aliasing creates the appearance of smooth curves on screen by changing the brightness of pixels along the edges of each letterform. At very small sizes, strongly anti-aliased type can look blurry.

The @font-face rule in CSS allows any kind of digital font hosted on a server to be downloaded by users — but only typefaces licensed for this use can be accessed legally.

Bitmap typefaces are built out of pixels that structure a screen display. While PostScript letters consist of vector outlines, true bitmap characters contain a fixed number of rectilinear units. Bitmap forms amplify the systematic quality of typography, and designers like Zuzana Licko have used them as visual ethos in both print and digital media.

Font Formats & Licensing

Typeface ≠ Font

Critical Distinction A typeface is the design of the letterforms. A font is the delivery mechanism. In metal type, the typeface is embodied in punches from which molds are made; a font consists of the cast metal printing types. In digital systems, the typeface is the visual design, while the font is the software. Most people use the terms interchangeably — type nerds insist on using them precisely.

Intellectual property law in the United States protects the font as software but does not protect the visual design of the typeface itself. Part Two

Text

Letters gather into words, words build into sentences — the massing of letterforms into larger bodies and fields

The Nature of Text

Body, Blood, and the Flow of Words

Text can be viewed as a thing — a sound and sturdy object — or a fluid poured into the containers of page or screen. Text can be solid or liquid, body or blood. Although many books define the purpose of typography as enhancing the readability of the written word, one of design's most humane functions is, in actuality, to help readers avoid reading.

Kerning

Adjusting Space Between Two Letters

Kerning is an adjustment of the space between two letters. Gaps occur around letters whose forms angle outward or frame an open space (W, Y, V, T). In metal type, a kerned letter extends past the lead slug, allowing two letters to fit more closely. In digital fonts, the spacing between letter pairs is controlled by a kerning table created by the type designer.

Metric kerning uses the kerning tables built into the typeface. Optical kerning is executed automatically by the page layout program, assessing all character shapes and adjusting spacing wherever needed.

Tracking vs. Kerning Tracking (letterspacing) adjusts the overall spacing across a group of letters. Kerning adjusts the space between two specific letters. Tracking is commonly applied to capitals and small caps, which appear more regal standing slightly apart. Loosely spaced lowercase letters — especially italics — look awkward because these characters are designed to sit closely together.

Line Spacing

Leading: The Space Between Lines

The distance from the baseline of one line of type to another is called line spacing, or leading — named after the strips of lead used to separate lines of metal type. The default setting in most software is 120% of the type size. Thus 10-pt type is set with 12 pts of line spacing.

Alignment

The Four Modes

Choosing to align text in justified, centered, or ragged columns is a fundamental typographic act. Each mode carries unique formal qualities, cultural associations, and aesthetic risks.

Interactive Demo: Text Alignment

Print situates words in space more relentlessly than writing ever did. Writing moves words from the sound world to a world of visual space, but print locks words into position in this space. Control of position is everything in print. Printed texts look machine-made, as they are. Typographic control typically impresses most by its tidiness and invisibility: the lines perfectly regular, all justified on the right side, everything coming out even visually, and without the aid of guidelines or ruled borders that often occur in manuscripts. This is an insistent world of cold, non-human, facts.

Flush Left / Ragged Right

The left edge is hard; the right edge soft. Word spaces do not fluctuate, so there are never ugly holes inside lines. A good rag looks pleasantly uneven. A bad rag forms regular shapes — wedges, moons, diving boards.

Justified

Both edges even. Efficient use of space; the norm for newspapers and books. Ugly gaps can occur when line length is too short relative to type size. Hyphenation breaks up long words and keeps lines tightly packed.

Centered

Symmetrical, like the facade of a classical building. Common on invitations, title pages, certificates, tombstones. Used without care, centered text looks staid and mournful. Break lines for sense.

Flush Right / Ragged Left

Common wisdom holds that flush right text is hard to read. Used in smaller blocks, however, flush right text forms effective marginal notes, sidebars, or pull quotes. A flush edge can suggest attraction — or repulsion — between chunks of information.

Hierarchy

Expressing the Organization of Content

A typographic hierarchy expresses the organization of content, emphasizing some elements and subordinating others. A visual hierarchy helps readers scan a text, knowing where to enter and exit. Each level should be signaled by one or more cues, applied consistently across a body of text. A cue can be spatial (indent, line spacing, placement) or graphic (size, style, color).

Some redundancy is acceptable, even recommended. Paragraphs are traditionally marked with a line break and an indent — a redundancy that has proven practical. Try using no more than three cues for each level in a document.

Hierarchy in Action

Common Typographic Disorders

Various Forms of Dysfunction

Various forms of dysfunction appear among populations exposed to typography for long periods of time. Listed here are a number of frequently observed afflictions.

Typophilia

An excessive attachment to and fascination with the shape of letters, often to the exclusion of other interests and object choices. Typophiliacs usually die penniless and alone.

Typophobia

The irrational dislike of letterforms, often marked by a preference for icons, dingbats, and — in fatal cases — bullets and daggers. The fears of the typophobe can often be quieted (but not cured) by steady doses of Helvetica and Times Roman.

Typochondria

A persistent anxiety that one has selected the wrong typeface. This condition is often paired with OKD (optical kerning disorder), the need to constantly adjust and readjust the spaces between letters.

Spacing & Linearity

The Space Between Things

Design is as much an act of spacing as an act of marking. The typographer's art concerns not only the positive grain of letterforms, but the negative gaps between and around them. In letterpress printing, every space is constructed by a physical object — a blank piece of metal or wood.

Although we take the breaks between words for granted, spoken language is perceived as a continuous flow, with no audible gaps. Spacing has become crucial to alphabetic writing, which translates the sounds of speech into multiple characters. Spaces were introduced after the invention of the Greek alphabet to make words intelligible as distinct units.

"Alphabet letterpress printing, in which each letter was cast on a separate piece of metal, marked a psychological breakthrough of the first order. Print situates words in space more relentlessly than writing ever did." — Walter Ong, Orality and Literacy

The French philosopher Jacques Derrida noted that although the alphabet represents sound, it cannot function without silent marks and spaces. Typography manipulates the silent dimensions of the alphabet, employing habits and techniques — such as spacing and punctuation — that are seen but not heard.

Linearity: All typographic devices — the index, appendix, footnote, table of contents — are attacks on linearity, providing means of entrance and escape from the one-way stream of discourse. Tapping the spatial dimension of writing — and liberating readers from the bonds of linearity — is among typography's most urgent tasks.

Birth of the User

From Reader to User

Graphic designers embraced the idea of the "readerly text" in the 1980s and early 1990s, using layers of text and interlocking grids to explore Roland Barthes's theory of the "death of the author." Typography became a mode of interpretation.

Another model surfaced at the end of the 1990s, borrowed from human-computer interaction (HCI) studies. The dominant subject became neither reader nor writer but user — a figure conceived as a bundle of needs and impairments, cognitive, physical, emotional. Like a patient or child, the user is a figure to be protected and cared for, but also scrutinized and controlled.

How texts are used becomes more important than what they mean. The interactive environment not only provides users with a degree of control and self-direction but also, quietly and insidiously, gathers data about its audiences.

Typography is an interface to the alphabet. By making itself evident, typography can illuminate the construction and identity of a page, screen, place, or product. Do not squander the reader's attention.

Part Three

Grid

Spatial organization — from the classical frame to modular systems, databases, and universal design thinking

The Nature of the Grid

A Flexible and Resilient Structure

A grid breaks space or time into regular units. Typographic grids are all about control. They establish a system for arranging content within the space of a page, screen, or the built environment. An effective grid is not a rigid formula but a flexible and resilient structure — a skeleton that moves in concert with the muscular mass of information.

"Grids belong to the technological framework of typography, from the concrete modularity of letterpress to the rulers, guides, and coordinate systems employed in graphics applications." — Ellen Lupton

Grid Structures

From Simple to Modular

Single Column

The classic book format. A single text block surrounded by margins. Elegant and economical — though not very interesting on its own.

Two Column

Common in newspapers, academic journals, and magazines. Allows text and image zones. The gutter between columns creates a visual pause.

Three Column

Flexible — elements can span one, two, or all three columns. Allows hierarchy: one wide column for main content, narrow column for captions or notes.

Modular Grid

Consistent horizontal and vertical divisions. Images are cropped to fit modules. Swiss designers in the 1950s–60s devised these systems, generating variation from a repeated structure.

Grid as Frame

Typography as Framing

Until the twentieth century, grids served as frames for fields of text. The margins of a classical book page create a pristine barrier around a flush, solid block of text. The philosopher Jacques Derrida described the frame in Western art as a form that seems separate from the work, yet is necessary for marking its difference from everyday life — a frame or pedestal elevates the work, removing it from the realm of the ordinary.

Typography is, by and large, an art of framing — a form designed to melt away as it yields itself to content. Designers focus much energy on margins, edges, and empty spaces: elements that oscillate between present and absent, visible and invisible.

Various alternative layouts existed during the first centuries of printing — from Gutenberg's two-column Bible to polyglot books displaying a text in several languages simultaneously. Such formats permit multiple streams of text to coexist while defending the sovereignty of the page-as-frame.

Dividing Space

The Grid Unleashed: Futurism, Dada, De Stijl

In the nineteenth century, multi-columned newspaper and magazine pages challenged the supremacy of the book. By questioning the protective function of the frame, modern artists and designers unleashed the grid as a flexible, critical, and systematic tool.

F. T. Marinetti's Futurist movement (1909) combined different styles and sizes of type, allowing lines of text to span multiple rows. His manipulations work against — but inside — the constraints of letterpress, exposing the technological grid even while trying to overturn it.

The Dutch De Stijl group (Mondrian, van Doesburg, Zwart) applied the grid to design and typography. Converting curves and angles of the alphabet into perpendicular systems, they forced the letter through the mesh of the grid — and suggested its expansion beyond the edges of the page.

Jan Tschichold's The New Typography (1928) conveyed these ideas as practical advice for commercial printers, theorizing the grid as a modular system based on standard measures. He advocated design as a means of discipline and order.

Grid as Program

Swiss Rationalism: A Total Methodology

During the post–World War II period, graphic designers in Switzerland honed ideas from the New Typography into a total design methodology. Max Bill, Karl Gerstner, Josef Müller-Brockmann, and Emil Ruder were practitioners and theorists of a new rationalism that aimed to catalyze an honest and democratic society. Rejecting artistic clichés of self-expression and raw intuition, they aspired to what Ruder called "a cool and fascinating beauty."

Gerstner's book Designing Programmes (1964) is a manifesto for systems-oriented design. He defined a design "programme" as a set of rules for constructing a range of visual solutions — connecting his methodology with the new field of computer programming.

This approach, quickly known as "Swiss design," found adherents — and detractors — around the world. Programmatic thinking is now being revived as designers confront large-scale information projects requiring flexible "programs" designed to accommodate dynamic bodies of content.

Key Swiss Rationalist Texts Josef Müller-Brockmann, Grid Systems in Graphic Design (1961) · Emil Ruder, Typography (1967) · Karl Gerstner, Designing Programmes (1964) · These books remain foundational references for systematic typography and grid-based layout.

Golden Section

The Ratio: 1 : 1.618

The golden section is a ratio that has been used in Western art and architecture for more than two thousand years. The formula: a : b = b : (a+b). The smaller of two elements relates to the larger in the same way that the larger relates to the two parts combined. Expressed numerically, the ratio is 1 : 1.618.

Some graphic designers are fascinated with the golden section and use it to derive grids and page formats. Other designers believe it is no more valid as a basis for deriving proportions than other methods — such as beginning from standard paper sizes, dividing surfaces into halves or squares, or simply picking whole-number page formats and making logical divisions within them.

A surprising property: when you remove a square from a golden rectangle, the remainder is another golden rectangle — a process that can be infinitely repeated to create a spiral. This logarithmic spiral appears throughout nature and has fascinated designers and mathematicians for millennia.

Grid as Table

Data, Charts, and Information Design

Tables and graphs are a variant of the typographic grid. A table consists of vertical columns and horizontal rows, each cell occupied by data. As explained by Edward Tufte, the leading critic of information design, tables and graphs allow relationships among numbers to be perceived and rapidly compared by the eye. In tables and graphs, the grid is a cognitive tool.

Designers (and software defaults) often over-emphasize the linear grid of a table rather than allowing the typography to command the page. As columns of text align visually, they create implied grid lines. Tables should illuminate the relationships among data — not trap each entry inside a heavily guarded cell. Tufte calls over-gridded tables "data prisons."

The table feature was incorporated into HTML in 1995 for tabular data. Graphic designers quickly devised unauthorized uses, transforming this tool for representing data into a typographic grid — to control placement of images and captions, build margins, gutters, and multicolumn screens. Advocates of web standards reject such workarounds as unethical design tactics that cause problems for sight-impaired users.

Return to Universals

A Second Modernism

William Gibson's 1984 novel Neuromancer envisions cyberspace as a vast ethereal grid — a "transparent 3D chessboard extending to infinity." The grid as infinite space, defying edges and dominated by the mind rather than the body, is a powerful instrument within modernist theory: a form both rational and sublime.

The rise of the Internet has rekindled interest in universal design thinking. The web was invented in the early 1990s (in Switzerland!) to let scientists share documents created with different software. Tim Berners-Lee never guessed that the web would become a design-driven medium connecting vast numbers of differently-abled and divergently-motivated people around the globe.

Universal design systems can no longer be dismissed as the irrelevant musings of a small, localized design community. A second modernism has emerged, reinvigorating the utopian search for universal forms — against the opacity and singularity of unique visual expressions, ideas of commonality, transparency, and openness are being reborn as information seeks once again to shed its physical body.

Editing

Three Phases of Editorial Work

Developmental editing addresses broad issues of content and structure — including judging a work's fitness for publication in the first place.

Copy editing (also called line editing or manuscript editing) roots out redundancies, inconsistencies, grammatical errors, and other flaws across the body of the work. The copy editor studies every word and sentence but does not question the overall meaning or alter the author's style.

Proofreading checks the correctness, consistency, and flow of designed, typeset pages. It is the final stage — new errors can appear at any time during the handling of a document.

PE's (Printer's Errors) are errors assignable to the printer — obvious divergences from the digital files provided by the designer. They must be corrected at no expense to the client. Surprisingly rare in the digital age.

AA's (Author's Alterations) are changes to approved text or layout. If the change originates with the client, the client is responsible. Designers can charge AA fees for any change to an approved document, even before the printer's proof.

EA's (Editor's Alterations) are the responsibility of the editor's employer. Good editors help prevent everyone's errors in the first place.

Free Advice

Essential Principles for Working Designers

01

Think with a concept. Many desperate acts of design — gratuitous gradients, drop shadows, transparency — are perpetrated in the absence of a strong concept. A good idea provides a framework for all design decisions.

02

Say less. Designers should avoid filling up space with arbitrary visual effects; writers should remember that no one loves their words as much as they do.

03

Spend more. Cheap stuff is usually cheap because of how it's made, what it's made of, and who made it. Buy better quality goods, less often.

04

Make type smaller. Amateur typographers make their type too big. The 12-pt default looks okay on screen but often looks horsey on the printed page. Experienced designers, however, make their type too tiny.

05

Density invites exchange. In typography as in urban life, density invites intimate exchange among people and ideas. White space is not always a mental kindness — a single surface packed with well-organized information is sometimes better than multiple pages with blank space.

06

Make systems, not things. Rather than force content into rigid containers, create systems that are flexible and responsive to the material they are intended to accommodate.

07

Design is social. It lives in society, it creates society, and it needs a society of its own — a community of designers committed to advancing and debating shared hopes and desires. Read, write, and talk about design.

08

Pay attention. To your clients, users, readers, and friends. Your design will improve as you listen to other people. Design responds to a need, a problem, a circumstance that arises in the world.

Sometimes fail. By making itself evident, typography can illuminate the construction and identity of a page, screen, place, or product. The interface should sometimes be allowed to fail — to reveal the system beneath.

In Summary

Typography Connects

Typography is an ongoing tradition that connects you with other designers, past and future. Type is with you everywhere you go — the street, the mall, the web, your apartment. This book aims to speak to, and with, all the readers and writers, designers and producers, teachers and students, whose work engages the ordered yet unpredictable life of the visible word.

"Typography is a tool for doing things with: shaping content, giving language a physical body, enabling the social flow of messages." — Ellen Lupton, Thinking with Type, 2010
