1

Flow Like a River

Before you can be witty, you need to keep conversation alive. These five techniques eliminate awkward silences and create the fertile ground where wit can bloom.

Technique 01

Never Speak in Absolutes

Replace questions like "What's your all-time favourite movie?" with bounded alternatives. Absolute questions demand impossible commitment; bounded questions invite easy, natural answers.

Technique 02

Think Before You React

Reactions prove you're listening. Match the emotion, mirror the intensity, and wait two seconds before replying — this reads as intelligence and genuine engagement.

Technique 03

Practice Free Association

When a topic leaves you blank, mentally list five things the keyword connects to. Conversation is just a chain of free associations — practising this daily unlocks instant fluency.

Technique 04

Use Double Explanations

For predictable questions ("What do you do?"), lead with a pithy layman's answer that creates curiosity, then follow with an expert answer once interest is established.

Technique 05

More Effective Compliments

Compliment choices and effort, not genetics. Praising a person's unusual hairstyle or work ethic validates their autonomy; complimenting eye colour does nothing meaningful.

Deep Dives
The Problem with Absolute Questions
▾

Absolute questions force your conversational partner to mentally index their entire life experience, make a definitive decision, and commit to it publicly — all while maintaining a casual chat. That's exhausting. Most people either answer with the first thing that pops to mind (and feel vaguely dishonest) or confess they don't know (which kills momentum).

Bad → Better

Absolute: "What's your all-time favourite band?"

Bounded: "Any bands you've been listening to lately?" / "Top few artists you'd put on right now?"

Adding constraints like "lately" or "a few" removes the pressure of a definitive, permanent answer.

Bounded questions also protect both parties from judgment. Saying "Forrest Gump is my favourite of all time" is a binary stance — either you agree or you don't. Saying "I watched Forrest Gump recently and it held up" is a softer contribution that invites dialogue rather than debate.

💡
The rule: every time you catch yourself about to use words like absolute, favourite, best, worst, only, ever — pause and add a qualifier: a time frame, a number, or a context. "What are a few movies you'd recommend?" is always better than "What's the best movie ever made?"
Mastering Reactions — The Two-Second Rule
▾

Reactions are the invisible connective tissue of conversation. Too fast and you look dismissive (you clearly weren't listening); too slow and you seem disengaged. The sweet spot is a deliberate, calibrated pause.

1
Identify the primary emotion — joy, annoyance, anger, sadness, or humour. Three out of five common emotions are negative, so don't default to positivity.
2
Match the intensity. "Wow, that must've been inconvenient" undersells sympathy. "I can't imagine what I'd do in your position" may oversell it. Equal measure is the goal.
3
Wait two seconds after they finish. Put your hand on your chin. This signals processing, not hesitation — people literally perceive two-second responders as smarter.
4
Mirror body language subtly. Furrowing your brows when they describe frustration, smiling when they're excited — mirroring amplifies the sense of being understood.
Free Association Practice Exercises
▾

Free association isn't about being right — it's about being fast. When someone says something you know nothing about, the topic is just a keyword. Mentally list five things that keyword connects to, then choose the best one to transition to.

Example Chain

Topic: "I love cats!"

Free associate: kittens → sandboxes → lions → allergies → the musical Cats → fish → dogs

Conversation pivot: "Have you ever seen Cats the musical? I've always been curious whether cat lovers enjoy it more or hate it."

How to practise: Write five random words on paper. For each word, instantly list three associations. Take the last association as your new word and repeat three times. Do this daily for a week and conversational pivots become automatic.

Napkin→ table → spoon → fine dining
Fine dining→ France → Michelin Star → butler
Butler→ Jeeves → white gloves → formality
🧠
If association fails in the moment, ask the person to elaborate — more words give you more material to work with. "Tell me more about that" is never a conversation killer.
The Double Explanation Strategy
▾

There are roughly ten questions you'll hear in every new social encounter. Preparing two-level answers for each turns a potential dead end into a conversation launch pad.

Level 1 — The Layman's Explanation

Short, pithy, slightly surprising. Creates curiosity and invites follow-up. "I file paperwork for a living" (lawyer) or "I'm paid to argue with people". Avoids jargon. Casts a wide net.

Level 2 — The Expert Explanation

Detailed, specific, shows depth. Delivered only when the person signals genuine interest — or reveals they share your field. "I'm a corporate lawyer specialising in business transactions, M&A filings, and loan documents."

Skiing Example

Layman's: "I went skiing and basically flattened the snow with my butt all weekend."

Expert's: "Hit two Black Diamonds and a Blue Diamond — got fitted for new poles too since my old ones bent on the moguls."

The expert explanation only fires if they ask more — or reveal they ski themselves.
The Science of Effective Compliments
▾

A compliment lands best when it validates a choice the person made consciously and cares about. The brain pairs positive feelings with whoever delivered them — compliment well, and people genuinely look forward to seeing you.

✓
Compliment choices and effort: wardrobe, hairstyle, fitness results, their way of phrasing ideas, unusual home decor, a skill they've worked on.
✗
Avoid genetics: eye colour, height, natural bone structure — the person did nothing to earn these. The compliment falls flat because there's no ownership.
★
Choose the least obvious thing. If she's wearing a stunning dress, shoes, and earrings — compliment the earrings. Noticing the subtle shows you're truly paying attention.
⚡
Don't repeat compliments. Once is validation. Twice is okay. Three times on the same thing reads as hollow or manipulative.

Conversation Is Play

Most people treat conversation like a job interview. The secret is to treat it like recess. These four techniques inject fun, spontaneity, and genuine connection.

Technique 01

Break the Fourth Wall

Step outside the conversation and comment on it — like an actor addressing the audience. Positive, observational meta-comments create instant intimacy and show heightened awareness.

Technique 02

The "Us Against the World" Technique

Create a two-person in-group by calling out something you both notice but others don't seem to. It forges immediate kinship and makes the other person feel uniquely understood by you.

Technique 03

Use Fallback Stories

Keep 3–5 short interpersonal stories in your back pocket. They follow a four-part formula: bridging sentence → story → your opinion → series of open-ended questions.

Technique 04

Instant Role Play

Assign the other person a hyperbolic role based on a compliment or tease, then ask in-character questions. It breaks interview mode and turns conversation into collaborative comedy.

Deep Dives
Breaking the Fourth Wall — When & How
▾

Think of your conversation as a TV show. "Breaking the fourth wall" means pausing to comment on the show itself — what's happening, what both of you are noticing, what's delightfully strange about this moment.

✓
Use it positively. Comment on something funny, surprising, or mutually entertaining. "Did we just spend ten minutes discussing toilet paper brands? We're clearly soulmates."
✓
Point out the unspoken. "I think we're both pretending to understand this music but neither of us has any idea."
✗
Never use it negatively. "Are you really directing the conversation toward yourself again?" — this reads as condescension, not wit.
✗
Don't overdo it. Constant meta-commentary makes people self-conscious and choose their words carefully around you.
Good Fourth-Wall Moments

"Wow, this conversation has really taken an odd turn, hasn't it?"

"Did you just make a reference to both the Spice Girls and Greek mythology in the same sentence?"

"I apologise in advance — I'm about to talk about coffee for the next five minutes."

Us Against the World — Creating an In-Group
▾

This tactic exploits one of the most fundamental human drives: the desire to belong. By creating a micro in-group of two, you tap into the psychological feeling of tribal alliance. Research shows the number-one rule of likability is making people feel you are like them.

Anatomy of the Technique

Notice something contextually shared: You're both at a loud party. Everyone else is dancing; you two are watching.

Voice the shared observation: "Can you believe everyone is voluntarily getting their eardrums destroyed in there? We're clearly the only sane people at this party."

Result: You've implied shared taste, shared sanity, and a unique bond — in one sentence.

1
Noteworthy in the moment: "Did you see that sixty-year-old Michael Jackson impersonator walk by?"
2
Shared context contrast: "Can you believe how aggressive everyone is queuing? We're the last civilised people left."
3
Shared emotions: "Glad I'm not the only one who finds this unbearably awkward."

This works non-verbally too — a knowing glance or raised eyebrow toward something absurd establishes the bond without a word.

The Four-Part Fallback Story Formula
▾

A fallback story is a pre-prepared mini-narrative with universal appeal, held in reserve for lulls. It isn't about the story itself — it's about the discussion it ignites.

1
Bridging sentence: "Hey, you know what I heard recently?" Creates a plausible, casual transition from anywhere. Alternatives: "Want to hear something interesting?" / "You won't believe what happened last week."
2
The story: Short. One or two premises only. Focus on the primary emotion, not nitty-gritty detail. "A female friend of mine just proposed to her boyfriend — ring and everything."
3
Your opinion: Keep it positive or neutral so others feel safe disagreeing. "Honestly, I thought — why not? It suits them." This makes you vulnerable first, which opens the other person up.
4
Series of questions: "What do you think? Would you ever do that? How would you react if your partner did it?" Multiple phrasings ensure one version resonates even if others don't.
📦
The best fallback stories are interpersonal dilemmas with universal themes — moral questions everyone has an opinion on. Would you tell your friend their partner was cheating? Would you take a $40k pay cut for your dream job? Would you go to Antarctica if you had two weeks to live?
Instant Role Play — Step by Step
▾

Role play lets both parties drop their social masks and enter a creative space together. It's improv comedy — but you control the setup.

1
Make a relative statement. A compliment elevates them above you; a playful tease places them below. "Your sense of navigation is genuinely incredible — I get lost in car parks."
2
Assign a label. "You're basically a modern-day Magellan." This is their role. Make it hyperbolic — bigger characters are more fun to play.
3
Ask in-character questions. "Which continent did you most enjoy discovering?" — you're treating the label as real. If they catch on, they'll play along.
4
Stay consistent. Keep asking questions that fit the role. The comedy deepens the longer you both maintain it without breaking.
You
"This roast chicken is amazing. You must be the local Bobby Flay."
Them
"Ha — yes, I used the recipe from my new cookbook."
You
"Will you sign my copy? And are you appearing on Food Network this season?"
They're now fully in character — the conversation has become collaborative play.

A Touch of Witty Banter

Wit is spontaneous creativity. These techniques train you to see multiple angles in any statement — and to respond from the best one rather than the most obvious one.

Technique 01

The Witty Comeback Machine

Three methods for instant comebacks: pick apart their word choice for an alternative meaning, agree-and-amplify to absurdity, or use an outlandish comparison. Deliver with 50% indifference.

Technique 02

Instigate a Banter Chain

Misconstrue a remark to enter "joke mode." If they play along, agree-and-amplify their response. Keep escalating the absurdity — the chain builds a shared comedic arc that bonds you.

Technique 03

Go Beyond the Literal

When someone makes a statement, it's an invitation — not a test question. Respond to the emotion and the subtext rather than the literal content. Initiate the joke rather than making one.

Technique 04

The Art of Misconstruing

Intentionally misinterpret what someone says in a playful direction. Exaggerated Conclusion, Playful Tease, and acting shocked are three reliable variations.

Deep Dives
Three Types of Witty Comebacks
▾

A witty comeback takes an attack (playful or otherwise) and redirects it in a way that is funny, not hostile. The shorter and punchier, the better. Deliver with a wry smirk and 50% indifference.

Type 1 — Pick Apart Their Words

Focus on their word choice and reinterpret it in a way that is favorable to you. If they use a simile, lean into the positive parts of the comparison.

Example

Bob: "You work as slow as a glacier."

You: "You mean strong and cool under pressure? Fair point."

No simile version: "The tortoise always wins the race!"

Type 2 — Agree and Amplify

Agree with the insult and then exaggerate it to a ridiculous degree. The absurdity takes the sting out and makes everyone laugh.

Example

Bob: "Your cooking was pretty terrible last time."

You: "You're lucky you left early — the rest of us got our stomachs pumped. Dinner at mine tonight?"

Type 3 — Outlandish Comparison

Amplify using an analogy that's extreme but not impossible. The imagery makes both parties laugh at the absurdity.

Example

Bob: "Your cooking was terrible."

You: "True — I should've served the eggs as hockey pucks."

⚡
Delivery is everything. The same words said with aggression vs. 50% indifference land completely differently. Think James Bond responding to a failed assassination attempt — cool, not reactive.
How to Start and Sustain a Banter Chain
▾

A banter chain is collaborative comedy — improv performed in real life. It's funny not because of any single line but because of how both players build on each other.

1
Misconstrue something to enter. Make a non-serious statement, an exaggeration, or an assumption. "I had trouble finding a skirt to fit over my powerful thighs." (in response to a comment about your outfit)
2
See if they play ball. They'll either engage (join the joke) or return to the literal topic. If they engage, you're in.
3
Agree and amplify their response. Take their exaggeration as true and raise the stakes. "Closer to 350 lbs — dogs cross the street when I walk by."
4
Know when to end it. A banter chain has a natural peak. Recognise it and transition before the joke goes stale.
A"That's a heck of a pant suit."
B"Thanks — I had trouble finding a skirt to fit over my powerful thighs."
A"You're squatting 250 now, right?"
B"Closer to 350. Dogs are afraid of me."
A"You could use them as a drive-in movie screen."
B"Did that last week. The double feature paid my rent. Did you know those two skyscrapers downtown were modelled after my legs?"
Beyond Literal — The Humour Mindset
▾

The single biggest difference between witty people and everyone else is a mindset shift: they're not trying to answer questions — they're looking for opportunities to play.

Literal vs. Non-Literal

Statement: "I spent a fortune at the Apple Store today."

Literal reply: "How much?"

Non-literal reply: "It's so expensive there I had to sell a kidney for my new phone."

Another Example

Statement: "He plays guitar like a deity."

Literal reply: "What song did he play?"

Non-literal reply: "More Buddha-style, or Ganesha-style?"

🎭
You're not making a joke — you're initiating one. Jokes have setup/punchline structures that create pressure. Initiating a joke is an open invitation with no pressure — both parties can join or not.
Three Misconstruing Techniques
▾

Misconstruing is the art of intentionally taking something in a different direction than intended. It subverts expectations and is the engine of most conversational humour.

1. Exaggerated Conclusion

Take their statement and multiply it by 100. The absurdity makes it obvious you're playing. If you don't exaggerate enough, it reads as a real insult rather than a joke.

Examples

"That coffee was terrible!" → "My car's battery water is tastier."

"My handwriting is horrible." → "Deciphering hieroglyphs would be easier."

"I'll call when I get home." → "I didn't know you could call from Mars, but okay."

2. Playful Tease

Misconstrue their positive statement as self-deprecating and agree with the imagined criticism. Use warm body language so the tease reads as play, not attack.

Examples

"I love this shirt!" → "Don't worry, we'll find one that actually looks good."

"She's my favourite singer." → "We'll work on your taste."

If they seem stung, immediately follow with a compliment on the same topic.

3. Shocked Reaction

React to an ordinary statement with exaggerated, theatrical disbelief. The disconnect between your reaction and the mundane statement is where the comedy lives.

Examples

"I love watching television." → "Oh my gosh, TELEVISION?!"

"That shirt is terrible." → "Terrible?! Are you crazy?!"

Funny on Command

You don't need to tell jokes to be funny. Replacing boring vocabulary, mastering the comic triple, and wielding misdirection make humour a natural side-effect of how you speak.

Technique 01

Vivid Imagery

Replace generic adjectives with specific, unusual descriptions that paint a picture. "Good" becomes "better than using the bathroom after a six-hour drive." The unexpected image is inherently funny.

Technique 02

The Comic Triple

List three things — two that share a sentiment, and a third that completely subverts it. The pattern builds expectation; the third element releases tension with a laugh.

Technique 03

Misdirection Aplenty

Sarcasm, irony, hyperbole, ironic similes — all share the same DNA: say one thing, mean another. The contrast between literal and implied meaning is the engine of the laugh.

Deep Dives
Vivid Imagery — Three Levels of Use
▾

Usage 1 — Replace Tired Adjectives

Instead of "good/bad/interesting," think for a second longer. Synonyms, analogies, and unexpected comparisons make you memorable.

Upgrade Chains

Good → "splendid" → "like a big Bloody Mary" → "better than using the bathroom after a long car ride" → "almost as good as Christmas morning"

I love coffee → "it's my lifeblood" → "I'm dead without it" → "I would bathe in it if I could"

Usage 2 — Unconventional Descriptions

Describe things by what they resemble, not what they are. Disassociate from the meaning and focus on the physical elements.

Real Examples

Amy Schumer on sleep: "I sleep in the shape of a swastika" / "as if I fell from the top of a building"

PJ O'Rourke on a small fierce policeman: "He looked like an attack hamster."

Avengers: Endgame on a changed Thor: "You look like melted ice cream."

Usage 3 — Pop Culture References

Use a widely-known reference to replace an adjective. Assign the reference's traits to what you're describing. Works best when the reference is universal enough that everyone gets it.

Examples

"That cashier shortchanged me. Does he work for the Olympics?" (traits: corrupt, unfair)

"This pie is almost as addicting as Game of Thrones." (traits: compulsive, can't-stop)

"He assembles toys like MacGyver." (traits: resourceful, improvised)

Tip: match references to the age of your audience.
The Comic Triple — Step by Step
▾

The human brain is conditioned by threes everywhere — fairy tales, laws of physics, act structures, comedy itself. The comic triple exploits this conditioning to produce a surprise that relieves accumulated tension as laughter.

1
Choose a topic. Almost anything works — coffee, relationships, holidays, your job. The topic needs at least one positive and one negative quality.
2
List two aligned items. Both should share the same sentiment (positive or negative). Listeners will feel the pattern solidifying.
3
Subvert with the third. The third element is the opposite sentiment. Deliver it in the same casual tone — the contrast does the work.
Canonical Examples

Mark Twain: "There are lies, damned lies, and statistics."

Chris Rock: "Women need only three things: food, water, and compliments."

Jon Stewart: "We feasted, gave thanks, then I killed them all and took their land."

Big Bang Theory: "Greeks gave us science, democracy, and little cubes of charred meat that taste like sweat."

Build Your Own: Coffee

Two positives: the energy boost, the aroma

One negative: the yellowing effect on your teeth

Result: "I have coffee every morning for the energy, the smell… and the dental bills."

Misdirection — Sarcasm, Irony & Ironic Simile
▾

Sarcasm — The Framework

Sarcasm says the opposite of what you mean. It's context-dependent — works between equals who share a sense of humour; backfires with insecure people or in formal settings. Use deadpan delivery, then break into a slight smile.

Sarcasm Patterns

When someone says something obvious: "That road is very long." → "You are very observant."

When something bad happens to them: "I dropped my coffee mug." → "You've always been so graceful."

When something good happens to them: "I got an A!" → "Must be a tough grading scale."

Irony — Observational Contrast

Irony is about situations, not people. A fire station burning down. A traffic committee stuck in traffic. A "No birds" sign used as a bird perch. You observe it; you don't create it.

Ironic Simile — The Formula

Compare something to something completely unlike it except in one specific, ironic way. The deceptive setup + unexpected backpedal = comedy.

Ironic Simile Examples

"I'm as likely to vote for him as I am to book a proctologist with trembling hands."

"I'm as sad as a dog with a bone." (dogs with bones are delighted — so you're thrilled)

"She has all the social graces of a steamroller."

"Our teacher's explanation was as clear as mud."

"Being the third wheel was as enjoyable as a root canal."

🎯
Words vs. Tone: Say positive words in a deeply sarcastic tone. Or say negative words with an angelically sweet tone and body language. Both create irony through contrast. The mismatch is the joke.

Captivating Stories

Stories are how humans connect at depth. You don't need to be a novelist — you just need a simple structure and the habit of seeing your life as a series of mini-narratives.

Technique 01

A Life of Stories

Replace one-word answers with three-sentence mini-stories. Every common small-talk question is an invitation to share something interesting. Prepare mini-stories in advance for predictable questions.

Technique 02

The 1:1:1 Method

One action. One emotion to evoke. One sentence. Start as close to the end as possible, then let the listener's questions guide the details. Keep it short — the discussion after is the point.

Technique 03

The Story Spine

An eight-beat framework: Once upon a time / Every day / But one day / Because of that (×3) / Until finally / And ever since then. Works for everything from office disputes to movie pitches.

Technique 04

Inside Stories

Identify the conversational high point, pocket it, then call back to it in a later context. The repeated reference across topics becomes an inside joke and a unique bond between just the two of you.

Technique 05

Ask for Stories

Phrase questions to require a story, not an answer. Give context, direction, and emotional prompts. "Tell me about the moment in the third quarter" beats "How'd you win?" every time.

Deep Dives
Mini-Stories — Transforming Small Talk
▾

A mini-story is three sentences that answer a question while revealing something interesting about you. It's the difference between a response and an invitation.

Q "What do you do?"
Bad "I'm a marketing executive." → "Oh cool." → silence
Good "I'm a marketing executive. Just last week a client threatened to send bodyguards to our office. I definitely wish I spent more time on the creative side." → "Wait — BODYGUARDS? Tell me more."
★
Prepare mini-stories for: your job, your week, your upcoming weekend, your hometown, your hobbies. Three sentences each. Have them ready before any social event.
✓
Details are the engine. Specific songs, names, places — these trigger the listener's own memories and emotions, making them feel drawn into your story involuntarily.
The 1:1:1 Method — Ruthless Simplicity
▾

Most people over-complicate stories. They add context before context is needed, details before interest is established, and lose their audience before arriving at the point. The 1:1:1 method forces you to lead with impact.

1
One action: The story is about exactly one thing happening. No sub-plots, no background history. Just the event.
1
One emotion: Name it before you tell the story. Are you going for shock? Humour? Envy? Sadness? This guides which details you include.
1
One sentence: If you can't summarise it in one sentence, you haven't figured out what the story is actually about yet.
1:1:1 Examples

"I was attacked by a dog and was so frightened I nearly wet my pants." (Action: dog attack. Emotion: fear/shock. One sentence.)

"My job interview went so badly the interviewer laughed at me as I walked out." (Action: interview. Emotion: embarrassment. One sentence.)

"When I met Joshua I spilled baked beans all over his white trousers in front of the entire room." (Action: spill. Emotion: mortification. One sentence.)

✂️
Start as close to the end as possible. The friend being in jail is still in jail whether you spend two sentences or ten getting there. Lead with the punchline; let them ask for the context.
The Story Spine — Eight Beats of Any Great Story
▾

Created by Kevin Adams of Synergy Theater, the story spine explains why every great movie, novel, and memorable anecdote feels satisfying. It works for a two-minute story about your weekend or a two-hour blockbuster.

1

Once upon a time…

Set the world and the characters. Establish what "normal" looks like. Without this, nothing that follows will matter to the listener.

2

Every day…

Deepen the routine. Build tension by showing a character growing restless, curious, or dissatisfied. Give them a personality and a motive.

3

But one day…

The inciting event. Something disrupts the status quo. A stranger arrives. A secret is revealed. The world tips.

4

Because of that…

First consequence. The character responds. This launches the main body of the story — what actually happened.

5

Because of that…

Stakes rise. Complications compound. New characters enter. The plot thickens — we're invested now.

6

Because of that…

Three dilemmas (the brain loves threes). The hero faces the third and hardest challenge. Resolution feels earned because we've watched the struggle.

7

Until finally…

Resolution. Does the guy get the girl? Is the case solved? Is the world saved? Reveal everything.

8

And ever since then…

The new normal. Close the loop. Signal you're done and invite the listener to respond. A moral, a punchline, or a callback all work here.

"In west Philadelphia born and raised / On the playground is where I spent most of my days" — Once upon a time + Every day. "I got in one little fight and my mom got scared" — But one day. "To sit on my throne as the prince of Bel-Air" — Until finally + And ever since then. — The Fresh Prince of Bel-Air theme song, a near-perfect story spine in 90 seconds
Inside Stories — Constructing Shared Jokes
▾

An inside joke is simply the same topic appearing multiple times between two people in different contexts. You don't need years of friendship — you can manufacture one in a single conversation.

1
Identify the conversational high point. The loudest laugh, the strongest mutual reaction, the most surprising revelation. This is your asset.
2
Pocket it. Don't let it drop. Keep it in mental reserve.
3
Call back when a new topic connects. Even loosely. The repetition + new context = instant inside joke.
Callback Example

Earlier: You both laughed comparing someone to a wiener dog.

Current topic: Jackets and fashion.

Callback: "Yeah, I can't wear that style anyway — I'm basically a wiener dog, remember?"

The high point has now become a permanent shared reference.
Asking for Stories — The Reporter Technique
▾

Sports broadcasters interview athletes who are breathless, adrenaline-soaked, and barely coherent — and still walk away with compelling soundbites. They do this by asking questions that can only be answered with a story.

Closed vs. Open Questions

Closed: "How was your weekend?" → "Fine."

Open: "How was your weekend? What was the best part? It was so nice out — please tell me you actually went outside."

Closed: "What do you do?"

Open: "What's the most exciting part of your job? What does it feel like to make that kind of difference?"

✓
Ask for a story, not an answer
✓
Be broad but offer specific directions or prompts
✓
Ask about feelings: "How did that feel?"
✓
Give multiple hints so one will resonate
✓
When all else fails: "Tell me the story about…"
🐴
Pin the Tail on the Donkey: When someone tells a story, add a comment that amplifies the primary emotion. "Did you feel like Scrooge McDuck for a second?" (after a story about dropping cash). You're assisting their story, not stealing their thunder — and they'll love you for it.
